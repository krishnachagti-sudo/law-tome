## Game night

{{ornament}}

It works wherever people already sit together: family dinners, road trips, pub quizzes, the staffroom, the last ten minutes of a class.

**Two teams.** Use the team scoresheet at the back. Teams take turns reading a question to the other side. The reading team gets a bonus point if the other team calls it wrong and the reader can explain why from the answer page.

**Quiz host.** Each round is a ready-made quiz round of ten. Read the question, give everyone ten seconds, collect answers, then read the verdict and the Tell someone line. DISPUTED is a real answer, not a hedge: it scores only when the stamp says DISPUTED, and the answer page says what keeps the question open.

**Scores.** Out of 100: 85 and over, Myth Buster. 70 to 84, Fact Checker. 55 to 69, Healthy Skeptic. Under 55, Easily Fooled (and in good company: these stories spread because they are convincing).

## Using this book in class

{{ornament}}

Each story is a short exercise in checking a claim. A lesson can take one story in about fifteen minutes:

1. Read the question aloud. Students vote and write one reason for their vote.

2. Ask what evidence would settle it: who would have to have written what, and when?

3. Read the verdict and the story. Compare it with the students' reasons.

4. Discuss why the myth spread. Each answer names the mistake or motive behind it.

Rounds 3 (Famous experiments, retold) and 4 (Charts and numbers) suit psychology and statistics classes; rounds 1, 6 and 9 suit science; rounds 2 and 7 suit history and English.

## How this book was checked

{{ornament}}

Every entry comes from a record in the Law Tome (conyso.com/lawtome), a free, sourced directory of more than a thousand named laws, effects and principles. Each record cites its sources, and the book names the ones each verdict rests on.

The entries were drafted with AI from the Law Tome's records. Krishna Chagti, the editor, then read and checked them.

Where the record hedges ("likely", "reportedly", "almost certainly"), the book keeps the hedge. A verdict is only as strong as its evidence, which is why DISPUTED exists.

Corrections are listed at conyso.com/lawtome/errata. If a verdict here is wrong, that page will say so.
