## Did the 1938 War of the Worlds broadcast cause mass panic?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Orson Welles's radio version of *The War of the Worlds*, aired on CBS on October 30, 1938, did frighten some listeners. A nationwide panic is another matter. A ratings survey taken that same night found only a small share of the homes it called tuned in at all.

The popular version is a night of chaos. Millions of Americans heard that Martians had landed in New Jersey, believed it, and fled their homes in terror.

The record starts with the audience, which was small. The play was staged as a run of urgent news bulletins breaking into a music program, so a listener who tuned in late could mistake it for news. But few were listening. The C. E. Hooper ratings service phoned 5,000 households for its national survey that evening. About 2 percent named the Welles program or a radio play; none said they were hearing a news broadcast. Most of the country was listening to something else, including Edgar Bergen's comedy show on NBC.

The large numbers came later. Hadley Cantril's 1940 study, *The Invasion from Mars*, estimated that about a million people had been frightened. That figure leaned on a poll taken six weeks after the broadcast, and Cantril himself conceded that its audience estimate was more than double any other measure. The media historians Jefferson Pooley and Michael Socolow have pointed out that being scared, upset or excited for a few minutes is not the same as panic.

The panic story spread through newspapers. The next morning *The New York Times* ran the front-page headline "Radio Listeners in Panic, Taking War Drama as Fact," and papers across the country followed. Radio had been drawing advertising away from print through the Depression, and a story about radio sowing chaos told advertisers and regulators that the new medium could not be trusted with news. Later, textbooks used the night as the classic proof of the hypodermic needle model, the idea that media inject their effects straight into a passive audience. Research from Paul Lazarsfeld's 1940 election study onward found media effects far more limited than that.

The documented night is quieter. Some listeners were frightened, and some called the police or the papers. But on the evening itself, a survey of 5,000 homes found about 2 percent listening to the play.

**Tell someone:** On the night of the 1938 War of the Worlds broadcast, a ratings survey of 5,000 homes found only about 2 percent listening; the mass panic was largely a newspaper story.

## Did novelist William Golding name the Gaia hypothesis?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The name came from William Golding, the author of *Lord of the Flies*, who lived in the same Wiltshire village as the scientist James Lovelock. Lovelock took his neighbor's suggestion, and his 1979 book *Gaia: A New Look at Life on Earth* carried the name to a wide public.

The popular assumption runs the other way. A hypothesis named after a Greek earth goddess sounds like a New Age label, stuck on a scientist's idea by admirers who wanted the planet to be alive.

The record starts with the science. From the 1960s Lovelock argued that Earth's living things and their surroundings form one self-regulating system, and that life holds the atmosphere far from the chemical balance a dead planet would reach. He had been calling it the "Earth feedback hypothesis." Golding, his neighbor in Bowerchalke, offered something better. In Lovelock's later account, given to Golding's biographer John Carey, the two were walking to the village post office when Lovelock described his big idea. Golding told him an idea that size needed a proper name, and suggested Gaia. Lovelock dated the walk to the late 1960s. He set the idea out in journal papers in 1972 and, with the microbiologist Lynn Margulis, in 1974, before the 1979 book.

The name helped the idea travel and also hurt it. A goddess invites a mystical reading, the Earth as a conscious being. The record calls that a misreading: the living-planet language is a metaphor, which is why Lovelock came to prefer the plainer word "geophysiology." Biologists attacked the strong version. W. Ford Doolittle argued in 1981 that nothing in any organism's genes could encode planet-wide feedback, and Richard Dawkins objected in 1982 that there is no population of planets for selection to act on. Lovelock and Andrew Watson answered in 1983 with Daisyworld, a model planet of black and white daisies that keeps its temperature steady through ordinary competition, with no foresight at all.

The mystical sound of the name is what makes its origin a surprise. It was a novelist's suggestion to a working scientist on a village walk. A non-purposive Gaia is now influential in Earth system science, and the purposive version is rejected.

**Tell someone:** The Gaia hypothesis got its name from William Golding, the author of *Lord of the Flies*, who suggested it to his neighbor James Lovelock on a village walk.

## Was Clever Hans reading his questioners' body language?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** In 1907 the psychologist Oskar Pfungst showed that the horse answered by watching tiny, involuntary movements of the person asking. When Hans could not see the questioner, his arithmetic collapsed.

The popular version comes in two forms. In one, Hans was a horse that could genuinely add and tell the date. In the other, he was a stage trick, and his owner signaled the answers on purpose.

The record rules out both. Hans belonged to Wilhelm von Osten, a Berlin mathematics teacher, who exhibited him in the early 1900s. The horse answered questions by tapping a hoof, and the act drew crowds and newspaper coverage abroad, including in *The New York Times* in 1904. That year a panel of 13 people, organized by the psychologist Carl Stumpf, concluded that no trickery was involved. Pfungst then tested the horse properly.

His method was to change one thing at a time. Sometimes Hans wore blinders so that he could not see the questioner; sometimes the questioner did not know the answer. In one series with large blinders, Hans got 89 percent of 56 questions right when he could see the questioner, and about 6 percent of 35 when he could not. Pfungst found the signal. A questioner who asked a problem leaned the head and trunk slightly forward, and Hans began to tap. When the count reached the right number, the questioner gave a slight upward jerk of the head, and Hans stopped. The movements were often too small for observers to notice, and Pfungst found them in himself as well as in von Osten. By producing or suppressing them on purpose, he could start and stop the horse.

The hoax version spread because fraud is a simpler story than unconscious cueing. But Pfungst concluded that von Osten was not cheating; the owner never accepted the findings and kept showing the horse.

The lasting result was a rule of method. If the person testing a subject knows the expected answer, the subject may be reading the tester rather than solving the problem. That is why careful studies of animal minds, and many human experiments, keep the tester blind to the answer.

**Tell someone:** Clever Hans got 89 percent right when he could see his questioner and about 6 percent when he could not; he was reading tiny head movements the questioner did not know he was making.

*Sources:* Pfungst, *Clever Hans (The Horse of Mr. von Osten)* (1907; English edition 1911). "Clever Hans," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-clever-hans-effect/

## Did Eratosthenes measure the Earth to within 1%?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** Eratosthenes did estimate the size of the Earth from shadows, around the third century BC, and his method was sound. How close he came cannot be settled, because nobody knows for certain how long his unit of distance was.

The popular version is precise. At noon on midsummer's day the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene, in southern Egypt, while a stick at Alexandria cast a shadow. From the angle and the distance between the cities, Eratosthenes worked out the Earth's circumference to within about 1 percent.

The method holds up. Eratosthenes of Cyrene, head of the Library of Alexandria, described it in a book, *On the Measure of the Earth*, that is lost; a simplified account survives in the later writer Cleomedes. At the summer solstice the noon Sun cast no shadow at Syene, while at Alexandria a vertical pointer showed it about one-fiftieth of a circle, roughly 7 degrees, from overhead. Taking the cities as 5,000 stadia apart, he multiplied by fifty and got about 250,000 stadia. Roman writers later gave 252,000.

The accuracy is where the trouble starts. Greek stadia came in several lengths, and historians disagree about which one Eratosthenes used. The American Physical Society gives a range of about 500 to 600 feet, which turns his result into anything from about 24,000 to 29,000 miles. The real figure is about 24,900. On a short stadion he was a few percent low; on a long one he was more than 15 percent high. A 1 percent result needs one particular choice of unit. His inputs had flaws too: Syene lies a little north of the tropic and well east of Alexandria's meridian, errors that partly cancel.

The well is a separate problem. Strabo and Pliny both describe a well at Syene lit to the bottom at noon on the solstice, but the classicist Peter Gainsford points out that no ancient source connects it to Eratosthenes.

The tidy version spread because it makes a perfect lesson: a stick, a well and a page of geometry beat the whole planet. Carl Sagan told it with the well in *Cosmos*, and popular retellings tend to quote whichever stadion gives the closest match.

What is safe to say: the reasoning was correct and the estimate was in the right range. The 1 percent depends on a unit nobody can pin down.

**Tell someone:** Eratosthenes' shadow method was sound, but whether he was within 1 percent depends on the length of his stadion, which historians still dispute.

## Did a waiter's memory inspire the Zeigarnik effect?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The waiter is the usual account of how the idea began, but it is an anecdote credited to the psychologist Kurt Lewin, told decades later in versions that disagree. What is documented is Bluma Zeigarnik's 1927 report of experiments on interrupted tasks.

The popular version is set in a Berlin restaurant. A waiter remembered every unpaid order without writing anything down, then forgot them all once the bill was settled. Lewin noticed, and his student Zeigarnik turned the scene into a law of memory: unfinished tasks stick.

The record keeps the experiments and the anecdote apart. Zeigarnik, born in Lithuania, studied under Lewin in Berlin and published her report in *Psychologische Forschung* in 1927. Participants worked through a series of short tasks, half of which were interrupted, and were then asked to recall them. In her main experiment, with 32 adults and 22 tasks, interrupted tasks were recalled about 1.9 times as often as finished ones. Lewin's field theory supplied the explanation: starting a task creates a tension that finishing releases.

The waiter is less secure. The record calls the observation anecdotal. The psychologist Colin MacLeod, in a 2020 history, sets out two versions. In Edwin Boring's 1957 telling, Lewin paid the bill and the waiter later could not say how much he had been paid. In Donald MacKinnon's account, as quoted by Alfred Marrow in 1969, the waiter had remembered what each person ordered and indignantly forgot it once they paid. MacKinnon was there; both versions were written down long afterward.

The story spread because it makes a laboratory result feel obvious, an effect anyone has seen in a café. It also outlived the effect it illustrates. Later studies in other countries, including Van Bergen's in 1968, found no recall difference between finished and interrupted tasks, and a 2025 meta-analysis found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks, while confirming that people tend to go back to them. The much-repeated figure of about 90 percent better recall traces to secondary sources, not the 1927 paper.

So the experiments are on the record and the waiter is a story told about them, in more than one version. The memory effect he stands for has not held up well.

**Tell someone:** The waiter behind the Zeigarnik effect is an anecdote told decades later in two versions; the 1927 experiments are real, but a 2025 meta-analysis found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks.

## Did Diderot write about a new dressing gown that ruined his study?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Denis Diderot, the editor of the French *Encyclopédie*, wrote "Regrets on Parting with My Old Dressing Gown" in 1769. A grand new scarlet gown made his study look shabby, and one by one he replaced almost everything in it.

The popular assumption is that the Diderot effect is a modern marketing term with an illustrative fable attached, the way business books invent a story to fit a lesson.

The record has the essay. Diderot opens by mourning the old gown, which had molded itself to his body and served as a rag for dusty books and a spilled pen. The new one is stiff and makes him look, in his word, stodgy. Then the room follows. The straw chair goes to the anteroom and a leather one takes its place. The plain wooden table gives way to an expensive desk. A Bergamo rug comes off the wall for damask hangings, and a mirror, a clock and new paintings arrive. Of the old furnishings, one rug survives. In Mitchell Abidor's translation, Diderot says of the gown: "I have become the slave of the new one."

The name is modern. The anthropologist Grant McCracken coined the "Diderot effect" in a 1986 article and developed it in his 1988 book *Culture and Consumption*. His point was that possessions form matching sets, which he called Diderot unities, and that one item out of step with the rest can set off a run of purchases to restore the match. McCracken named the pattern; Diderot described living through it, two centuries earlier.

The suspicion of invention is easy to understand. A neat story attached to a term from consumer research looks like a parable made up to sell the idea. But here the order runs the other way: the story came first, and the term was built on it.

Two cautions keep the claim honest. The effect is a descriptive idea from anthropology, not a measured law with an effect size. And the modern examples, from new sofas to new phones, are extensions of the essay, not parts of it. The essay itself, with its scarlet gown and its replaced chair, is from 1769.

**Tell someone:** The Diderot effect is named after a real 1769 essay in which a new scarlet dressing gown led Diderot to replace almost everything in his study.

## Is the rule of 72 more than 500 years old?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The rule appears in Luca Pacioli's *Summa de arithmetica*, printed in Venice in 1494. Pacioli gave it without any explanation, which suggests it was already in use before him.

The popular assumption is that the rule of 72 is a modern finance shortcut, something invented for investment classes and retirement brochures. To find how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the annual interest rate: at 6 percent, about twelve years.

The record goes back to the fifteenth century. Pacioli, a Franciscan friar and mathematician, published the *Summa* as a compendium of the arithmetic, algebra and geometry of his day. It is best known for the first printed description of double-entry bookkeeping. In a section on interest, he asks how many years a sum lent at a given rate per hundred will take to double, counting interest and capital together. His instruction, in the Italian, is "tieni per regola 72": keep 72 as the rule, and divide it by the rate. His own example is 6 percent, which gives twelve years.

What Pacioli does not do is derive it. He offers no proof and no table. The MacTutor history of mathematics archive notes that the rule is a rough form of 100 times the natural logarithm of 2, about 69.3, so the exact answer at 6 percent is about 11.9 years. Logarithms were not published until John Napier's work of 1614, more than a century later. Whoever first used 72 most likely found it by working through compound interest tables and noticing the pattern. Seventy-two has a practical virtue too: it divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12, which makes the mental arithmetic easy.

The modern-shortcut assumption spread because the rule now lives in personal finance books and online calculators, often with no history attached. Some retellings overcorrect and say Pacioli invented it, which the record does not support either. He is the earliest known source, not the inventor.

The safe statement is dated. The rule of 72 was in print by 1494, more than 530 years ago, and it was probably older still.

**Tell someone:** The rule of 72 is in Luca Pacioli's 1494 *Summa de arithmetica*, stated without explanation, so it is more than 500 years old and probably older.

## Did "bandwagon" come from a circus clown's campaign wagon?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** Dan Rice was a real and famous circus clown, and he may well have welcomed Zachary Taylor aboard in 1848. But band wagons were already rolling at political rallies before then, and the idiom does not turn up in print until the 1880s.

The popular version is a neat origin story. During the 1848 presidential campaign, Rice invited Taylor to ride on his circus bandwagon. As Taylor's campaign surged, other politicians wanted a seat, and "jumping on the bandwagon" came to mean joining whatever looks like a winner.

The record supports the clown and not much else. Rice was among the best-known entertainers in nineteenth-century America and later tried politics himself, entering races for Congress, the Senate and even the presidency in 1868 before dropping out of each. The link to Taylor rests on secondary retellings: Wikipedia's article on Rice draws on David Carlyon's 2001 biography, and its article on the bandwagon effect tells the story without any citation at all.

The word itself is older than the story. The language historian Peter Jensen Brown found "four band wagons" carrying Whig leaders at a political rally in an Ohio newspaper of September 1842, six years before Taylor ran. A cartoon from July 1848 shows a brass band's wagon heading to a Taylor meeting, with no showman in sight. The figure of speech comes much later: Brown's earliest example of a state "getting into the band wagon" is a report of the 1884 Republican convention, and "jump on the band-wagon" appears in the *New York Tribune* in 1888. A Theodore Roosevelt letter of 1899 is often cited as an early use.

So the record shows wagons with bands at rallies and at circuses for decades, and a metaphor that took off in the 1880s. It does not show the metaphor starting with Rice. Nor does it rule him out.

The Rice version spread because it gives a phrase a face. A clown, a war hero and a parade wagon make a better story than a slow drift from literal wagons to figurative ones.

The economics is firmer than the etymology. Harvey Leibenstein gave the bandwagon effect its formal economic treatment in 1950, as demand that rises because others are buying.

**Tell someone:** Band wagons were carrying politicians to rallies by 1842, and "jump on the bandwagon" is not found in print until the 1880s, so the Dan Rice origin story is unproven.

## Did a doomsday group double down when the world didn't end?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** For seventy years the answer was yes, on the authority of a classic 1956 book. In late 2025 a historian working from the authors' newly unsealed papers argued that the group held on for only days, that its leader soon recanted, and that the researchers shaped what they recorded.

The popular version comes from *When Prophecy Fails*, by Leon Festinger, Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter. A Chicago-area woman, Dorothy Martin, received messages predicting a great flood on December 21, 1954, and promised her followers rescue by flying saucer. The researchers and paid observers joined the group posing as believers. When no saucer came, Martin received a new message: the group's light had persuaded God to spare the world. The group, which had shunned reporters, now called the press. The book presented this as proof of a new idea, cognitive dissonance: people deeply committed to a belief will defend it harder when the world refutes it.

The record now has a second account. In "Debunking 'When Prophecy Fails,'" published in the *Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences*, Thomas Kelly argues that the group had been seeking publicity for months before the date, through magazine pieces and press releases. On that reading, the calls were nothing new. He accepts that members kept their beliefs for a few days afterward. But he cites a 1955 interview in which Martin told the UFO magazine *The Saucerian* that she had not expected to be picked up physically, and he finds that the group soon dissolved. From the archive, he also argues that the observers steered the group, and that Riecken, posing as a spiritual authority, later admitted he had "precipitated" a key scene.

Other evidence points the same way. Later studies of groups whose prophecies failed, from Pentecostals to Jehovah's Witnesses, mostly found that failure weakened recruiting rather than strengthening it.

The doubling-down version spread because it was vivid, readable and useful. It explained stubborn belief in one story, and scholars applied it to religious movements as far back as early Christianity.

Kelly's is one paper, and the debate it opens is young. What it does not touch is the theory itself, which rests on laboratory work such as Festinger and Carlsmith's 1959 one-dollar experiment. The flood story was its most famous illustration, not its foundation.

**Tell someone:** The doomsday group in *When Prophecy Fails* held on for only days, according to a 2025 study of the authors' own papers, and its leader recanted the next year.
