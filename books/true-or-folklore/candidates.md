**What the verdicts mean.** TRUE: the record confirms the story, usually a surprising one. FOLKLORE: the record says the popular story is unsupported, apocryphal, misattributed or false. DISPUTED: the record calls it contested, uncertain or hedged. NEEDS-SOURCE: the record does not settle it. **Fame**: 3 = most general readers have heard it; 2 = widely known among educated readers; 1 = niche but delightful. The table is sorted by fame, most famous first. **Flags**: `LIVING?` means a named, possibly living person appears in an unflattering verdict, so the entry needs defamation or legal review. The corpus does not record whether people are alive, so these flags are deliberately conservative.

## Ten strongest opening entries

1. **Did Edward Murphy coin "If anything can go wrong, it will"?** (#44, DISPUTED). This is the founding law of the genre, and the honest answer is the brand: it is attributed to Murphy in 1949, the events are anecdotal, and the earliest firm print is 1952, credited to an unnamed physicist. It shows on page one that the book won't fake a clean verdict.
2. **Newton's apple, as a pair** (#2 FOLKLORE, #3 TRUE). The apple story goes back to Newton himself, but the apple hitting his head was added later. One spread teaches the reader that the answer is often "partly". The record needs a dated source for Newton's recollection before print.
3. **Did Archimedes shout "Eureka!" in the bath?** (#1, FOLKLORE). This is the most famous science anecdote there is. The source is Vitruvius, about two centuries later, and the record adds that his method would not even have worked.
4. **Did 38 neighbours watch Kitty Genovese die?** (#5, FOLKLORE). The story is serious and well known. The record says there is no basis for the number 38, and that at least one witness shouted at the attacker. The 2007 paper documenting this is cited in the record.
5. **Did Einstein write E = mc² in 1905?** (#15, FOLKLORE), paired with **Einstein's Nobel** (#16, TRUE). Two surprises about the most famous equation and the most famous scientist. The first is the kind readers will bet on and lose.
6. **The Cobra Effect** (#19, FOLKLORE), paired with **the Hanoi rat tails** (#52, TRUE). The anecdote the law is named after was never documented, but a real case shows the same mechanism. This is the book's argument in miniature: the idea can be sound even when its founding story isn't.
7. **Wald's bombers** (#28, FOLKLORE diagram; #29, TRUE reasoning). The meme image is a later recreation, but the 1943 reasoning behind it is real. It is very well known online and makes a clean split verdict.
8. **Is the Baader–Meinhof phenomenon named after a militant group?** (#65, TRUE). Most readers assume the name is a joke or a mistake. It actually comes from a 1994 reader's letter to a newspaper. Delightful, and it puts a TRUE entry up front.
9. **Is the "Mount Stupid" curve from the Dunning–Kruger paper?** (#7, FOLKLORE). This is the famous-chart category in one entry: the 1999 paper has no such curve. It sets up #8, where the effect itself is DISPUTED, which needs legal review because it names living people.
