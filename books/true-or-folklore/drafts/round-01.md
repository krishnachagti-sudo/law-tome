# Round 1: Famous scenes

The stories everyone has heard. A warm-up.

These are the scenes told in classrooms and at dinner tables: a bath, an apple, a tower, an equation, a wartime speech. Each has a version almost everyone knows and a record that can be checked. Some of the famous details come from the people involved. Others were added long afterward by people who were not there. Guess first, then turn the page.

## Did Archimedes shout "Eureka!" in the bath?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Archimedes stated the law of buoyancy around 250 BC, in his treatise *On Floating Bodies*. The bath, the crown and the cry of "Eureka!" are almost certainly a later embellishment.

The popular version is a single moment. Archimedes stepped into his bath, saw the water rise, and realized how to settle a problem about a crown. He ran out shouting "Eureka!"

The record separates the principle from the anecdote. The principle is in Archimedes' own work. *On Floating Bodies* states that a body in a fluid is buoyed up by the weight of the fluid it displaces, and its Proposition 5 gives the corollary for floating bodies: a floating object settles until it displaces exactly its own weight. That is why a steel hull floats and why a balloon rises when it displaces more than its own weight of air. Within its assumptions the principle is exact.

The anecdote has a different author. It was first told by Vitruvius, writing some two centuries after Archimedes. The method Vitruvius describes, judging the crown by the water it pushes aside, is crude, and too imprecise to have worked. Archimedes more likely used hydrostatic weighing.

The story spread because it gives an abstract law a face and a date. A rule about fluid pressure is hard to picture; a man running from his bath is not. It also fits a popular idea of discovery as one flash of insight rather than patient measurement. Once Vitruvius had written it down, each retelling had a source to point to, and nobody needed to ask how a writer two centuries later could know what was said in a bathroom.

The physics stands on its own. Archimedes' principle is one of the oldest quantitative results in physics, and the earliest source for it is his own treatise of around 250 BC. The anecdote is not evidence for it. The earliest source for the bath is Vitruvius.

**Tell someone:** Archimedes did state the law of buoyancy, but the bath and the "Eureka!" almost certainly come from Vitruvius, writing some two centuries later.

## Did an apple fall on Newton's head?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** There is an apple in Newton's own account of his thinking about gravity. It does not hit him on the head. That detail is a later embellishment.

The popular version is a piece of slapstick. An apple dropped onto Newton's head, and the idea of gravity arrived with the bump.

The record is plainer. Newton published his law of universal gravitation in *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica* in 1687, a book whose printing Edmond Halley paid for. The law says that any two particles of matter attract each other with a force that grows with the product of their masses and falls with the square of the distance between them. Robert Hooke had suggested an inverse-square attraction in letters, but he could not derive the orbits of the planets from it. The mathematical derivation was Newton's own.

The apple story itself, according to *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, traces back to Newton's own later recollection. That recollection includes an apple. It does not include an apple striking him.

The bump spread because it improves the story as a story. A falling apple seen from a distance is a thought. An apple landing on a scientist's head is an event, with a victim and a punchline, and it makes discovery look like something that happens to a person by accident. Each retelling had a reason to keep the joke and none to check it.

The apple is also a fair symbol of what the law did. Newton's rule covers an apple falling to the ground and the Moon orbiting the Earth with the same mathematics, which is why the law is often called the first great unification in physics. The symbol worked without any help from a bruise.

So the fruit belongs to Newton and the bump belongs to his retellers. The law is in the *Principia* of 1687. The apple comes from Newton's own later account, and in that account it stays off his head.

**Tell someone:** According to *Britannica*, Newton's own recollection includes an apple but no bump; the hit on the head was a later embellishment.

## Did Newton himself tell the apple story?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The apple story is not a fable invented by Newton's admirers. According to *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, it traces back to Newton's own later recollection. Only the apple hitting his head was added afterward.

The popular version here is a skeptic's one. The apple, it says, is pure myth: a tidy tale invented by later admirers to dress his work in a moment of genius.

The record does not support the skeptic. The account of an apple comes from Newton himself, in his own later recollection. What the record marks as embellishment is narrower: the detail of the apple striking him. That detail is not in Newton's account.

The law the apple is attached to is fully documented. Newton published it in 1687, in *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica*. It states that every mass attracts every other mass, with a force that scales with the product of the masses and falls with the square of the distance between them. Newton showed that this rule, combined with his laws of motion, produces Kepler's three laws of planetary motion. Later work kept testing it. Galle found Neptune in 1846 within about a degree of where Le Verrier's Newtonian prediction placed it.

The myth of a wholly invented apple spread for a reason that runs opposite to most myths in this book. The head-bump is so plainly comic that it invites suspicion, and people who rightly doubted the bump threw out the apple with it. A debunking can overshoot. Once "the apple story is a myth" became a known correction, it traveled as easily as the original, and with as little checking.

The safe statement keeps both halves. Newton told of an apple; nobody needed to invent it for him. The bump on the head is the later addition. The law itself rests on the earliest dated source in the record, the *Principia* of 1687, and the apple rests on Newton's own word.

**Tell someone:** Newton himself told of the apple, according to *Britannica*; only the bump on the head was added later.

## Did Galileo drop balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The tower drop is likely a legend. It is not in Galileo's own writings. His documented work on falling bodies used balls rolling down ramps, with notes from around 1603 to 1604 and results published in 1638.

The popular version is a public demonstration. Galileo climbed the Leaning Tower of Pisa, let go of a heavy weight and a light one together, and proved that both fall at the same rate.

The record traces the tower story to a biography by Galileo's student Viviani, written decades later. Because it is absent from Galileo's own writings and comes from that late source, it is widely regarded as legendary.

What Galileo did document was cleverer. The clocks of his day could not time a free fall, so he slowed the fall down. He rolled a polished ball down a gently sloping grooved ramp and timed each descent with a water clock, marking equal intervals by equal weights of water. In successive equal times the ball covered distances in the ratio one, three, five, seven. The total distance grew as the square of the time, which is the mark of constant acceleration. The acceleration was the same whatever the ball's weight. Without air resistance, all bodies fall at the same rate. These results overturned the old Aristotelian teaching that heavier objects fall proportionally faster.

The tower story spread because it does in one afternoon what the real work did over years. A drop from a famous building is visible, dramatic and easy to retell. Ramps, water clocks and ratios of odd numbers are not. The legend also has the right moral, an old authority refuted in a single visible test, which made it useful to anyone telling the story of modern science.

The documented record is the ramp. Galileo's working notes date to around 1603 to 1604, and he published the results in *Two New Sciences* in 1638. That book, not the tower, is the earliest dated source for his law of falling bodies.

**Tell someone:** The tower drop is likely a legend; Galileo's documented work on falling bodies rolled balls down ramps and timed them with a water clock.

*Sources:* "Galileo's Leaning Tower of Pisa experiment," Wikipedia. "Two New Sciences," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/galileos-inclined-plane/

> **QA notes.** 332 words after the page turn. Both sources are Wikipedia. The verdict depends on the Viviani attribution and on the claim that the drop is absent from Galileo's writings; check against Viviani's biography and *Two New Sciences* (1638) before print. The record gives no date or title for Viviani's biography, only "decades later." Hedges kept as the record has them: "likely a legend," "widely regarded as legendary." The record calls the timing "necessarily approximate"; not stated in the entry. No living person named.
> **Source check 2026-11-12:** CONFIRMED. Viviani's *Racconto istorico della vita di Galileo* was composed in 1654 and published in 1717, and there is no account of a tower drop by Galileo himself; *Two New Sciences* (Leiden, 1638) describes the water clock with the water weighed, and Galileo's working papers date from 1604. Viviani and *Two New Sciences* themselves were not opened. ## Did Pythagoras discover the Pythagorean theorem?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The relation that carries Pythagoras's name was known and used long before him. A Babylonian tablet from around 1800 BC lists its number triples, and Pythagoras lived from about 570 to 495 BC.

The popular version is simple. Pythagoras of Samos discovered that in a right triangle the square on the longest side equals the sum of the squares on the other two, and the theorem is named after him for that reason.

The record puts the relation much earlier and in several places. Babylonian tablets such as Plimpton 322, from around 1800 BC, record Pythagorean triples. The relation also appears in the Indian *Sulba Sutras* and the Chinese *Zhoubi Suanjing*. Their dates are less certain: the *Sulba Sutras* are placed between the 8th and 5th centuries BC, and the surviving *Zhoubi Suanjing* dates from around the 1st century BC, though its contents are thought to be older. The simplest triple, three, four and five, has been used since antiquity to lay out right angles, and builders still check a corner that way.

As for Pythagoras himself, the record is cautious. *Encyclopaedia Britannica* calls the attribution to him uncertain, and the record adds that it is doubtful he personally proved the theorem.

The myth spread because names on theorems work like labels, and labels get read as credits. Once a result is called "Pythagorean," the natural assumption is that Pythagoras found it. A name is repeated every time the theorem is taught; the older tablets and texts are not. Repetition turned a label into a history.

The theorem itself is not in doubt. Within flat, Euclidean geometry it holds without exception. Its limits are of scope: it covers right triangles only, and for any other triangle the law of cosines applies, with Pythagoras's relation as its special case. What is in doubt is the credit. The earliest dated source in the record for the relation is a Babylonian tablet of around 1800 BC, more than a thousand years before Pythagoras was born.

**Tell someone:** A Babylonian tablet from around 1800 BC already lists Pythagorean triples, more than a thousand years before Pythagoras was born.

*Sources:* "Pythagorean theorem," *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. "Pythagorean theorem," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-pythagorean-theorem/

The record gives no dates for the *Sulba Sutras* or the *Zhoubi Suanjing*; the entry says so. The "why it spread" paragraph names a general mechanism (repetition of the name); the record gives no history of how the attribution formed. "More than a thousand years" is arithmetic on the record's two dates. No living person named.

## Did Einstein write E = mc² in his 1905 paper?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Einstein's 1905 paper contains the idea but not the famous formula. It states in a single sentence that a body which gives off energy as radiation loses mass in proportion.

The popular version has Einstein setting down E = mc² in one short line in 1905, the equation appearing fully formed in the paper that announced it.

The paper exists and can be read in translation. It is a short follow-up to his special relativity paper of the same year, titled "Does the Inertia of a Body Depend Upon Its Energy Content?" Einstein's derivation ends in a sentence about a body radiating energy and losing mass: if it gives off energy L, its mass diminishes by L/V², with V for the speed of light. That is not the compact formula now known everywhere.

The record is clear that the idea was his in a way that mattered. Others before him, Poincaré and Hasenöhrl, had linked mass and energy only for electromagnetic cases. Einstein was the first to state the relation as a general principle that applies to all energy. A compressed spring, a heated object and a chemical reaction all change mass by their energy divided by the speed of light squared. The change is far too small to weigh. Nuclear reactions are where it becomes large enough to notice.

The myth spread because the formula became an icon, and icons get read back into their origins. Readers who meet E = mc² everywhere assume it must appear in the founding document, much as people assume a famous quotation has a famous speaker. The popular tie to atomic bombs is a second misreading the record corrects: the equivalence is not specifically about nuclear physics at all.

The statement survives the check; the typography does not. The earliest dated source is Einstein's 1905 paper, and in it the mass-energy relation is set out in a sentence, not as E = mc². The record does not say when the compact form first appeared in print.

**Tell someone:** Look for E = mc² in Einstein's 1905 paper and it isn't there: he set out the mass-energy relation in a sentence.

*Sources:* Einstein, "Does the Inertia of a Body Depend Upon Its Energy Content?" (1905), English translation. "Mass-energy equivalence," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/mass-energy-equivalence/

## Did Einstein win his Nobel Prize for something other than relativity?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Einstein's Nobel Prize, the physics prize designated for 1921, was for his work on the photoelectric effect. NobelPrize.org gives the citation as "for his services to Theoretical Physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect." Relativity is not what the prize named.

The popular version assumes the obvious. Einstein is famous for relativity, so the Nobel committee must have given him the prize for relativity.

The record tells a longer story about light. Heinrich Hertz first noticed in 1887 that light striking a material can knock electrons out of its surface. Philipp Lenard studied the effect quantitatively. Its features defeated classical wave theory. Below a threshold frequency no electrons come out, however bright the light. Above it, the energy of each electron depends on the light's frequency, and brightness changes only how many electrons emerge.

In 1905 Einstein explained this in *Annalen der Physik* with the light-quantum hypothesis and an equation relating electron energy to frequency. Robert Millikan confirmed the frequency relation around 1916. By the American Physical Society's account, Millikan spent about a decade testing Einstein's equation, convinced it had to be wrong, and his own measurements confirmed it and pinned down Planck's constant, though he kept doubting the photon behind it.

The myth spread because fame and prizes are assumed to line up. Relativity is the work most people connect with Einstein, and E = mc² is the formula they know. The photoelectric effect is a laboratory result about electrons and metal surfaces. It is easy to assume the most famous work won the most famous prize.

The record adds one caution in the other direction. The effect is popularly overcredited as proof that light comes in quanta. It motivated the idea powerfully, but on its own it is not airtight; the decisive evidence for photons came later, from other experiments.

The fact holds. The earliest source is Einstein's 1905 paper on light quanta, and the prize designated for 1921 named his law of the photoelectric effect, not relativity.

**Tell someone:** Einstein's Nobel was not for relativity: the prize designated for 1921 named his law of the photoelectric effect.

## Did Gordon Moore say chips double every 18 months?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Gordon Moore never gave 18 months. His 1965 article predicted that the number of components on a chip would double roughly every year, and in 1975 he revised that to every two years.

The popular version is that Moore's law says computing power doubles every 18 months.

The record goes back to the article. On 19 April 1965 Moore, then head of research and development at Fairchild Semiconductor and later a co-founder of Intel, published "Cramming more components onto integrated circuits" in *Electronics* magazine. His rate was a doubling roughly every year. In 1975 he slowed it to every two years. The name "Moore's law" is usually credited to the Caltech professor Carver Mead.

The 18 months came from someone else. Intel's David House combined the two-year doubling in transistors with rising performance and projected that chip performance would double every 18 months. Moore said he never used the figure.

The myth spread because House's figure answered the question most people were asking. Moore's law is about transistor count and cost, an economic trend with no physical principle behind it. A claim about speed was easier to use and repeat than a claim about component density. The record notes that the popular reading of ever-doubling "speed" mixes Moore's trend up with a separate one, Dennard scaling, under which shrinking transistors kept power density roughly constant. Dennard scaling broke down around 2005 to 2006, ending the free gains in clock speed.

The two-year version held for more than forty years, from a few thousand transistors per chip in the early 1970s to billions. Its pace has slowed since about 2010.

So the law is Moore's and the 18 months is House's. The earliest dated source is Moore's own article of 19 April 1965, which said about once a year. His 1975 revision said two years.

**Tell someone:** Gordon Moore predicted a doubling roughly every year in 1965 and every two years in 1975; the 18 months was David House's.

## Did Churchill promise "blood, sweat and tears"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** In May 1940 Churchill offered "blood, toil, tears and sweat." That is four items, not three, and "toil" is the word that dropped out.

The popular version is the three-word phrase almost everyone quotes: Churchill, in May 1940, offering the nation blood, sweat and tears.

The record states his actual words and their date, May 1940, and files the shorter version as a misquotation. It lists the misquote alongside another piece of shaky lore attached to the same idea: the Latin tag *omne trium perfectum*, "everything that comes in threes is perfect." That motto is popularly cited as an ancient proverb, but it is not attested in the classical Latin corpus in reliable references, and is better treated as a modern slogan.

Both belong to the rule of three, the principle that a trio of words, phrases or examples is more satisfying and memorable than other numbers. The usual explanation is that three is the smallest number of items that can set up a recognizable pattern. A triad is short, has rhythm, and feels complete. The device runs from classical rhetoric, where three parallel clauses are called a tricolon, to Caesar's *veni, vidi, vici*, the three rights of the US Declaration of Independence and the closing phrase of Lincoln's Gettysburg Address.

That is the most likely reason the misquote spread. Memory and retelling favor the shape that feels finished. Four items are one too many for the pattern the rule describes, so the phrase was trimmed to the form that sounds right. The record warns that a well-made group of three can lend an idea an unearned air of completeness. Here it did more: it rewrote a famous line to fit the pattern.

The words on the record are Churchill's own. In May 1940 he promised "blood, toil, tears and sweat." The three-word version is what the rule of three made of them.

## Did Edward Murphy say "Anything that can go wrong will go wrong"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** A maxim like it came out of an Air Force rocket-sled project around 1949, and Murphy's name stuck to it. Who first said it, and in what words, is not settled. The earliest firm printing under Murphy's name, from 1952, credits an unnamed physicist.

The popular version has an engineer called Murphy, annoyed by a botched job, coining the law on the spot.

The record is more tangled. Around 1949 Edward A. Murphy Jr., an aerospace engineer, worked on project MX981, a series of rocket-sled deceleration tests at what became Edwards Air Force Base. In one run, strain-gauge transducers wired to a harness returned zero readings because they had been installed the wrong way round. A part that could be fitted two ways had been fitted the wrong way. The project's lead, Colonel John Stapp, then popularized the maxim at a press conference. He framed it as a strength: the team's habit of designing against every failure it could imagine.

The wording is where the trail breaks. Murphy's son and his colleagues later recalled materially different phrasings, and the accounts of the 1949 events are anecdotal. The earliest firm appearance in print of Murphy's law by name, as "If anything can go wrong, it will," is in Anne Roe's 1952 book *The Making of a Scientist*. Roe credits it to a physicist she does not name. Similar sayings without Murphy's name were in print as early as 1877.

The gloomy reading won because the law is so easy to confirm. Across enough events some failures are statistically certain, which the statistician David Hand calls the law of truly large numbers. People remember the toast that landed butter-side down and forget the far more numerous times nothing went wrong. That turned a working engineer's rule into a cosmic joke.

The safe statement is narrower than the legend. A rule about failure came out of the MX981 tests around 1949 and carries Murphy's name. Its first wording is contested, and its first firm appearance in print under his name is 1952. What it meant to the people who used it was practical: if a part can be fitted the wrong way, design it so it can't be.

**Tell someone:** Who first said Murphy's law is disputed; its earliest firm printing under his name, in 1952, credits an unnamed physicist.
