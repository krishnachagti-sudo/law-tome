# Round 2: Who really said it?

Lines pinned on the wrong person, or on nobody in particular.

A good line rarely stays with the person who wrote it. It drifts toward a more famous name, loses a word, or gains a sharper one. Each saying here comes with a name: a medieval friar, a psychologist, an economist, a science-fiction writer and several famous wits, and one label comes with the two psychologists who named it. ## Did William of Ockham write "Entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The famous Latin sentence is nowhere in Ockham's surviving works. Its earliest close version was written by John Punch in 1639, nearly three centuries after Ockham died. Ockham argued for the same principle, in other words.

The popular version is that the medieval friar William of Ockham wrote *Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*, "entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity," and so gave his name to the razor.

The record starts with Ockham himself. William of Ockham (c. 1287 to 1347) was an English Franciscan who argued repeatedly for parsimony. One of his own statements is in his *Summa Logicae*, part I, chapter 12. The phrasing the record quotes from him is *Pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate*: "plurality should not be posited without necessity."

The famous sentence came later. The Irish Franciscan John Punch wrote a close version, *non sunt multiplicanda entia sine necessitate*, in a 1639 commentary on Duns Scotus, and called it a common axiom of the Scholastics. The name came later still. Libert Froidmont coined *novacula occami*, Occam's razor, in 1649. And the idea is older than all of them: Aristotle and Ptolemy both stated versions.

The misattribution spread because the sentence and the name fit together so neatly. Punch's line is crisper than Ockham's own, and Punch presented it as a shared saying rather than his own invention, so it traveled without an author. Ten years later Froidmont's coinage supplied one. A sentence without a writer attached itself to the nearest famous name, and the razor has been quoted in words close to Punch's under Ockham's name ever since.

None of this weakens the principle, which is a tie-breaker between explanations that both fit the facts: prefer the one that assumes less. It changes only the credit. Ockham's own wording is in the *Summa Logicae*. A close version of the famous Latin sentence first appears in Punch's commentary of 1639, and the name in Froidmont in 1649.

**Tell someone:** The famous Latin line of Occam's razor is not in Ockham's works; John Punch wrote a close version in 1639, nearly three centuries after Ockham died.

## Did Hebb say "neurons that fire together wire together"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Donald Hebb set out the rule in 1949. The slogan is not his. It came in 1992, usually credited to Carla Shatz as a mnemonic, and crediting it to Hebb is, in the record's words, the most common error about the rule.

The popular version puts the rhyme in Hebb's mouth, as his own summary of how learning works in the brain.

The record goes back to the book. The Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb stated his rule in *The Organization of Behavior* in 1949. His version is careful and causal: when one neuron repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing another, the connection between them is strengthened. Hebb also proposed that this strengthening welds groups of neurons into working units, which he called cell assemblies.

The slogan flattens that. "Fire together" suggests that firing at the same moment is enough. Hebb's wording is about one cell helping to fire the next. Later research backed the causal reading. Under spike-timing-dependent plasticity, a signal arriving shortly before the receiving cell fires strengthens the connection, and the reverse order weakens it.

The record corrects two other credits that drift to Hebb. He did not discover long-term potentiation, the physiological effect consistent with his rule; Bliss and Lømo found it in 1973. And the idea was not wholly his: earlier associationist proposals came first. His contribution was the crisp synthesis and the cell-assembly framework.

The slogan spread because it was built to. A mnemonic is designed to be remembered, and a rhyme is easy to remember. Once it was in circulation, it was natural to attach it to the scientist whose rule it summarized rather than to the scientist who wrote the summary. The shorter and catchier a line is, the faster it loses its author.

The rule is Hebb's, set out in *The Organization of Behavior* in 1949. The rhyme is usually credited to Carla Shatz, who printed it in 1992; Siegrid Löwel and Wolf Singer printed a similar line the same year.

**Tell someone:** Hebb set out his rule in 1949, but the rhyme "fire together, wire together" came in 1992 and is usually credited to Carla Shatz.

## Did Mark Twain say "If all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** There is no evidence that Mark Twain said it. The idea was named by the philosopher Abraham Kaplan in 1964, after a talk in 1962. Its best-known hammer-and-nail form is Abraham Maslow's, from 1966.

The popular version is a Twain quip about the habit of using the one tool a person knows for every job.

The record traces a different line. Kaplan named and formalized the idea as the law of the instrument in *The Conduct of Inquiry* (1964), having aired it in a 1962 talk. His image was a small boy who, given a hammer, finds that everything he meets needs pounding. Two years later Abraham Maslow gave the most famous version in *The Psychology of Science* (1966). That is why the bias is often called Maslow's hammer. Maslow also imagined a machine built to wash cars that treats everything fed into it as a car to be washed.

The record adds two cautions. First, Maslow did not coin the idea; "Maslow's hammer" credits his phrasing, not the invention. Second, the familiar line about hammers and nails is a paraphrase, not a word-for-word quotation from either man. The Twain attribution is the most common misattribution of all, and there is no evidence for it.

The saying drifted to Twain for a reason that recurs throughout this round. A line with no obvious author tends to settle on a famous wit. The paraphrase helped: once the wording had moved away from Kaplan's and Maslow's own, nothing in the line itself pointed back to them. A quip that sounds like Twain gets credited to Twain.

The point of the law survives the mix-up. It warns against applying a familiar method where it does not fit, not against reuse or expertise. The earliest dated source in the record is Kaplan's 1962 talk, set in print in 1964. Maslow's version followed in 1966. Twain has no place in the record.

## Did Goodhart say "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The famous sentence is Marilyn Strathern's, from 1997. Charles Goodhart's original point, in 1975, was narrower and about monetary policy.

The popular version credits the one-liner to Goodhart himself, as the summary of his law in his own words.

The record has both texts. In 1975 the economist Charles Goodhart wrote a paper on British monetary policy, "Problems of Monetary Management: the U.K. Experience." His point was that any statistical regularity the authorities leaned on would collapse once they used it for control. The record calls it a monetary cousin of the Lucas critique.

The sentence everyone quotes appeared 22 years later, in a different field. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, writing about audit culture in British universities in "'Improving ratings': audit in the British University system" (*European Review*, 1997), generalized Goodhart's point into "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." She was rephrasing a 1996 gloss on Goodhart's law by Keith Hoskin.

The general form is the one that traveled, because it applies everywhere. Rank schools on test scores and teachers teach to the test. Hold emergency departments to a waiting-time target and some meet it by reclassifying patients. Reward engineers for lines of code and the code bloats. A rule about monetary statistics reaches economists; a rule about every target reaches everyone. In 2018 David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant sorted the ways it bites into four types: regressional, extremal, causal and adversarial.

The misattribution spread because the generalization kept the eponym. Strathern's sentence generalized Goodhart's law and traveled under his name, and a law is assumed to be quoted in its namesake's words. Each repetition under his name made the original 1975 paper less likely to be read, and the narrower point it made less likely to be noticed.

So the law is Goodhart's and the sentence is Strathern's. The earliest dated source is Goodhart's 1975 paper on monetary management. The famous wording first appears in Strathern's article of 1997.

**Tell someone:** The famous line about measures becoming targets is Marilyn Strathern's, from 1997; Goodhart's 1975 point was narrower and about monetary policy.

## Did Godwin say whoever mentions Hitler first loses?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Mike Godwin's 1990 law says only that a long online argument is likely to produce a comparison to Hitler or the Nazis. The rule that whoever makes the comparison loses was added later by the Usenet community, not by Godwin.

The popular version treats the "loser" rule as part of Godwin's law: whoever invokes Hitler ends the argument and loses it.

The record goes back to 1990. Godwin, an American attorney, coined the law that year on early internet forums, chiefly Usenet. In its usual form it says that as an online discussion grows longer, the probability of a comparison to Hitler or the Nazis approaches one. Godwin has described it as a deliberate experiment in memetics: a counter-meme, seeded to discourage the reflexive Hitler comparisons he felt trivialized the Holocaust. He documented the method in a 1994 *Wired* article, "Meme, Counter-meme."

The loser rule came from the community that adopted the law. A Usenet tradition, recorded in the hacker Jargon File, added that once someone invokes the Nazis the thread is over and the person who made the comparison has lost. Godwin resists it. He has said the law should start a conversation, not end one, and that it was never meant to be predictive. After the 2017 rally in Charlottesville he publicly set the law aside for marchers carrying swastikas, saying that comparing them to Nazis was fair.

The corollary spread because it turned an observation into a weapon. Godwin's version describes a pattern. The loser rule gives anyone in an argument a way to win it, by declaring the other side out of bounds. A rule that ends arguments is more useful in an argument than a remark about how arguments tend to go, so it was repeated more. The record calls citing the law to shut down a well-founded comparison its most common misuse.

The law itself is narrow. The earliest dated source in the record is Godwin's 1990 coinage, with his own account in *Wired* in 1994. The loser rule is folk tradition added afterward.

**Tell someone:** Godwin's 1990 law says only that a long online argument is likely to produce a Hitler comparison; the loser rule came later, from Usenet.

## Did Sturgeon say "Ninety percent of everything is crap"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Theodore Sturgeon's printed word was "crud," not "crap." His *Venture Science Fiction* column first printed it in September 1957. In March 1958 the column gave it as "Ninety percent of everything is crud." He also called it his Revelation, not his law.

The popular version quotes Sturgeon's law in a blunter word: "Ninety percent of everything is crap."

The record has a timeline. Theodore Sturgeon, a science-fiction author, aired the idea in a lecture around 1951 and at the 1953 World Science Fiction Convention in Philadelphia. He first put it in print in his *Venture Science Fiction* book column in September 1957, and in March 1958 set it out formally, "Ninety percent of everything is crud," with two corollaries.

The context was a defense. Critics were judging all of science fiction by its pulpiest work. Sturgeon's answer was that most science fiction is indeed poor, because most of every genre is, and that the best of it stands with the best writing anywhere. The saying was a case for judging a field by its finest work rather than its most abundant.

There is a second slip in the popular version. Sturgeon called the 90% line his Revelation. His actual law was a different sentence: "Nothing is always absolutely so," a warning against absolutes. Popular usage, and the *Oxford English Dictionary*, folded the Revelation into the law, and that is now the standard sense.

The record does not say when "crap" replaced "crud." The general pattern is familiar: a quotation that travels by word of mouth tends to get sharper, and a blunter word makes a stronger line. The same drift turned the Revelation into the law, because a named law is easier to cite than a revelation.

The "90%" was never a measurement, as the record notes, and the word was never "crap." The earliest printing is Sturgeon's column of September 1957, and like the March 1958 version it says crud.

## Did Twain or Einstein say "A man with one watch knows what time it is"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Neither did. The saying first appears without a name in the *San Diego Union* in 1930. The attributions to Mark Twain and Albert Einstein are unsupported, and no documented Segal wrote it either.

The popular version credits the line about the man with one watch and the man with two to Twain or to Einstein.

The record follows the line through three stages. It first appears, anonymously, in the *San Diego Union* in 1930: "a man with one watch knows what time it is, and a man with two watches could never be sure." In 1961 a newspaper columnist attributed it to Lee Segall, a broadcaster in Dallas. It then picked up the label "Segal's law," spelled with one l, through Arthur Bloch's *Murphy's Law* books. The record calls the eponym unidentified: no documented person named Segal is its author.

The saying is also less true than it sounds. Two sources that disagree do leave a person unsure, but the remedy is not to go back to one. The sailor's version, quoted by Fred Brooks, is: "Never go to sea with two chronometers; take one or three." With three, a majority vote shows which one is wrong. Engineers build the same idea into aircraft and satellite navigation.

The Twain and Einstein credits spread because anonymous wit looks for an owner. A sharp line with no author attached tends to be given one, and the owners it finds are the most quotable names available. Einstein adds a further pull for a saying about time and measurement. The "Segal" label shows the same process in miniature: a 1961 guess at an author became, through repetition in popular books, the name of a law.

The record dates the line firmly. Its earliest source is the *San Diego Union* of 1930, where it ran with no author's name. Twain and Einstein do not appear in that record, and neither does a Segal who wrote it.

**Tell someone:** Neither Twain nor Einstein said it: the one-watch line first ran, with no author's name, in the *San Diego Union* in 1930.

## Did the researchers who named it call it "impostor syndrome"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The two psychologists who named it, Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes, called it "the impostor phenomenon" in a 1978 paper. "Syndrome" is popular usage, not their term, and the experience appears in neither of the two main diagnostic manuals.

The popular version is that impostor syndrome is a recognized condition, described under that name by the researchers who first identified it.

The record starts with the paper itself: "The Impostor Phenomenon in High Achieving Women: Dynamics and Therapeutic Intervention," published in *Psychotherapy: Theory, Research & Practice*. Clance and Imes drew on clinical work with high-achieving professional women who believed their success came from luck, timing or a fooled assessor, whatever their record showed. They described a loop. Over-prepare, or put the work off and then rush it; succeed either way; then credit the effort or the charm rather than the ability.

The difference in wording matters. A phenomenon names an experience a person has; a syndrome suggests a disorder. The record keeps to the first. The experience is in neither DSM-5 nor ICD-11, so its prevalence and its best treatment are still unsettled, and a 2019 systematic review of 62 studies found that none had tested whether any treatment works. Later research also found the experience across genders and professions, not only among the women of the first study.

"Syndrome" spread because it sounds like medicine. A clinical label makes a private feeling seem official and shared, and sharing was much of what people wanted from the name: those who thought they were uniquely fraudulent learned the feeling was common. The term became one of the most searched psychological labels in working life. The record adds that popular use has drifted toward ordinary, proportionate nerves about new work, which is not what the 1978 paper described.

So the word on the original page is "phenomenon." Clance and Imes coined that term in 1978, and the "syndrome" most people now say came from popular usage, not from the researchers.

**Tell someone:** The psychologists who named it in 1978 called it the "impostor phenomenon"; "impostor syndrome" is popular usage, and it is not a diagnosis.
