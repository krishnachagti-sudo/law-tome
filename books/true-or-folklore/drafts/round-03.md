# Round 3: Famous experiments, retold

What the textbook version leaves out.

Psychology's most famous experiments reach most people as stories: a few lines, a striking number, a lesson about human nature. The stories are shorter than the studies. A percentage loses the condition that produced it, and pilot runs, script changes and single subjects drop out of the retelling. Each question in this round asks whether the version people repeat matches what the record shows.

## Did 38 witnesses watch Kitty Genovese die and do nothing?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The record finds no basis for the story that 38 witnesses watched Kitty Genovese die and did nothing. At least one witness shouted at her attacker.

The popular version is short and bleak. Kitty Genovese was murdered in 1964 while 38 people watched, and not one of them acted. It is often told as proof that a crowd will leave a person to die.

In a 2007 paper in *American Psychologist*, Manning, Levine and Collins documented the exaggerations in the account of her murder. The number 38 has no basis, and at least one witness shouted at the attacker. Kitty Genovese's death was real. The image of a silent crowd watching it is not supported by the evidence.

The case, as misreported, did prompt real research. In two experiments in 1968, John Darley and Bibb Latané formalized what became known as the bystander effect. People who believed they alone could hear someone having a seizure over an intercom almost all responded quickly. Those who thought several others were listening responded less often and more slowly. The effect holds in the laboratory under narrow conditions: ambiguous, non-dangerous emergencies among strangers.

Outside the laboratory the picture is kinder. A 2020 analysis by Philpot and colleagues of 219 real public conflicts recorded on CCTV found that in nine cases in ten at least one bystander intervened, and that more bystanders meant more help. In clearly dangerous emergencies the effect weakens or reverses.

The false version spread because it was vivid and because it became attached to a real finding. A single shocking case with a precise number is easy to remember and easy to repeat, and each retelling of the science carried the anecdote along with it. The effect even acquired a colloquial name, the Genovese syndrome, which tied her name to a partly mythologized event. Once a story becomes the standard illustration of a result, few people go back to check it.

The bystander effect is real in narrow laboratory conditions, and in real public conflicts intervention is the norm. The earliest source in the record to set out the corrections to the Genovese story is Manning, Levine and Collins, 2007. Their paper finds no basis for the 38, and at least one witness acted.

**Tell someone:** At least one witness shouted at Kitty Genovese's attacker, and there is no basis for the story of 38 silent witnesses.

## Did the Stanford prison guards turn cruel on their own?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The guards did not become cruel on their own. Archival recordings and later scholarship show that the experimenters, including the warden, instructed and pressured them to be tough.

The popular version runs as follows. In 1971 ordinary students at Stanford were given guard uniforms and authority over fellow students playing prisoners, and within days they turned abusive without being told to. The lesson drawn is that a bad situation can make anyone cruel.

The basic events are on record. The study was led by the psychologist Philip Zimbardo in 1971. Twenty-four screened male student volunteers were randomly assigned to be guards or prisoners in a mock prison in the basement of the psychology building. It was planned for two weeks and stopped after six days, as guards grew abusive and prisoners showed extreme distress. Zimbardo argued that the situation and the roles, not the participants' dispositions, drove the cruelty.

The later evidence points elsewhere. Archival recordings and later scholarship, notably by Thibault Le Texier, show that the experimenters actively instructed and pressured the guards to be tough; Le Texier concluded that the abuse was substantially coached rather than spontaneous. The volunteers answered an advertisement for a study of prison life, which the sources say attracted people higher in aggression and authoritarianism. A key prisoner later said his breakdown was acting, and many guards said they were consciously playing a role. The study had no control condition, and critics have said it never went through normal peer review, which Zimbardo disputed. The sources also record serious ethical problems, including participants who effectively could not leave.

The popular version spread because it offered a simple, dramatic answer to a hard question. The study was widely taught, and it was invoked in discussions of prisons, abuse and atrocities such as Abu Ghraib. A story that seems to explain real cruelty is easy to repeat and hard to give up. The sources now treat the study as an example of a compelling narrative outrunning weak evidence.

The defensible statement is narrower than the legend. The study did end early amid abuse and distress. The claim that the guards' cruelty arose from the situation alone is Zimbardo's interpretation, and the record pairs it with archival evidence that the guards were coached.

**Tell someone:** The Stanford prison guards were instructed and pressured to be tough, archival recordings show; Thibault Le Texier argues the abuse was substantially coached.

## Did Hawthorne factory output rise whatever the lighting?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The original Hawthorne lighting data do not show output rising whatever the lighting. When Steven Levitt and John List reanalyzed them, they called the famous patterns "entirely fictional".

The popular version is a neat paradox. At a factory in the 1920s, researchers brightened the lights and output rose. They dimmed the lights and output rose again. The conclusion drawn was that the workers were responding to the attention of being studied, not to the lighting.

The studies were real. They ran from 1924 to 1932 at the Hawthorne Works, a Western Electric factory in Cicero, Illinois, and included the illumination and relay-assembly experiments associated with Elton Mayo's team. The term "Hawthorne effect" came decades later, during reanalyses in the 1950s. It is usually credited to Henry Landsberger in 1958, with an earlier informal use sometimes attributed to John R. P. French.

The data tell a weaker story. The illumination records were long thought lost. Levitt and List located them and published their reanalysis as an NBER working paper in 2009 and in *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics* in 2011. They reported that the claim that every lighting change boosted output is not supported. The apparent effect largely disappears once day-of-week and pay-period cycles are controlled for, leaving only, in their words, "hints of more subtle manifestations". The original studies also rested on tiny samples and tangled confounds, among them pay incentives, changes in supervision and the replacement of workers.

The paradox spread because it was memorable and, for decades, unchecked. With the original records thought lost, the story could be retold but not tested against the numbers. It also came with a name, and a name makes an idea easy to cite. The sources describe the Hawthorne effect as a case of a memorable name outrunning its evidence.

The general idea survives. People who know they are being watched can change their behavior, which is why researchers use blinding and control groups. The founding factory data, reopened by Levitt and List in 2009, do not show the famous pattern.

**Tell someone:** The famous Hawthorne output patterns are "entirely fictional", said Levitt and List after reopening the original lighting records.

## Did Milgram show that two-thirds of people will give a lethal shock when ordered to?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** About 65 percent of participants went to the maximum 450 volts in one baseline condition. Across the more than twenty conditions Milgram ran, obedience varied widely, and archival research has questioned how many participants believed the shocks were real.

The popular version turns that number into a fact about people. Two-thirds of ordinary people, it says, will deliver a seemingly lethal shock to a stranger if someone in authority tells them to.

The experiments began in 1961 at Yale under the psychologist Stanley Milgram and were first published in 1963. Milgram launched them amid the trial of Adolf Eichmann, asking how ordinary people could take part in atrocities under orders. Each participant, cast as a teacher, was told by an experimenter in a lab coat to shock a learner for every wrong answer, raising the voltage each time. The learner was an actor and received no shocks. The generator was labeled up to 450 volts and marked as dangerous. When teachers hesitated, the experimenter used fixed prods, from "please continue" to "you have no other choice, you must go on". Yale psychology seniors he polled beforehand predicted that only a tiny fraction would reach the maximum.

The 65 percent belongs to one condition. In variants that changed how close the teacher was to the learner or to the authority, obedience rates differed widely. Archival research, notably by Gina Perry, has argued that only about half of participants fully believed the shocks were real, that experimenters sometimes went off the fixed script, and that results may have been presented selectively. These are Perry's arguments. The record treats them as part of a live dispute, not as a settled finding.

The single figure spread because it is easier to carry than twenty conditions. It also arrived with moral weight. Coming after the Holocaust and the Eichmann trial, a finding that a majority would obey to the end shocked the public and the profession. A number that seems to answer a question that large tends to be repeated without its conditions.

The checked statement is this. In the baseline condition about 65 percent of participants went to 450 volts. That figure is not a fixed human rate, and how far participants believed what they were doing is disputed.

**Tell someone:** Milgram's famous 65 percent came from one baseline condition, obedience varied widely across his variants, and how many believed the shocks were real is disputed.

## Did Milgram prove we are all six handshakes apart?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Milgram did not prove that everyone is six handshakes apart. Most of the chains in his 1967 experiment never reached their target, and the "six" is a median over the minority that did.

The popular version credits "six degrees of separation" to a single experiment. Stanley Milgram, it says, showed that any two people are linked by six acquaintances.

The experiment was real. Around 1967 Milgram had people forward a folder toward a target in Boston, from acquaintance to acquaintance. The chains that were completed passed through a median of roughly five to six intermediaries. Most never arrived. When Judith Kleinfeld re-examined Milgram's data and archives in 2002, she found that few chains were completed, that the samples were small, and that selection bias was present. If people drop out at a roughly constant rate at each step, longer chains are the ones most likely to fail, so the true distance could even be larger than the completed chains suggest.

The idea, and the phrase, came from elsewhere. The Hungarian writer Frigyes Karinthy proposed it in a 1929 short story, "Chains", which posited no more than five intermediaries. John Guare's 1990 play popularized the phrase "six degrees of separation". Counting adds confusion: five intermediaries make about six links, and degrees and hops are often mixed up.

Later work put the idea on firmer ground in a different form. In 1998 Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz showed that a network can be tightly clustered and still crossed in a few steps, provided a few links reach far away. A 2012 study of the whole Facebook graph, some 721 million users, found an average distance of about 4.7 hops. That figure counts every link in a complete graph and is not directly comparable to Milgram's lossy chains.

The legend spread because a round number is easier to remember than a completion rate. "Six degrees" had a catchy phrase, a play behind it and a famous experiment to point to. The failed chains were invisible, because a chain that never arrives leaves nothing to count. The sources call it a case of a vivid number outrunning the evidence behind it.

What Milgram showed is narrower. Short paths exist between some pairs of people, and people can sometimes find them using only local knowledge of who seems closer to the target. The six is a median of the chains that finished, not a law.

**Tell someone:** Most of the chains in Milgram's small-world experiment never reached their target, and the famous six is a median of the minority that did.

## On most trials, did Asch's subjects go along with the wrong answer?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** On most trials, Asch's subjects did not go along. They gave the group's wrong answer on about a third of critical trials and stayed independent on about two-thirds.

The popular version holds that people will deny the evidence of their own eyes when a group tells them to. In the retelling, the subjects in Solomon Asch's line experiments usually caved.

The experiments ran principally around 1951 at Swarthmore College. A real participant sat with a group of confederates, typically around seven, and everyone said aloud which of three lines matched a reference line. The answer was obvious. On critical trials the confederates all gave the same wrong answer before the participant spoke. People judging alone erred less than one percent of the time, which showed the task was easy.

Two numbers came out, and they are easy to confuse. Across all critical trials, about a third of answers conformed to the wrong majority. Separately, about three-quarters of participants conformed at least once. Many never conformed at all, and some who went along aloud yielded only in public, without changing their minds.

The conditions mattered. Unanimity was crucial: a single confederate who gave the right answer sharply reduced conformity. Conformity rose with group size up to about three confederates and then leveled off. The participants were male American college students of a particular era, rates vary with culture and task, and a 1990 survey by Friend, Rafferty and Bramel found that most textbooks overstated it.

The stronger version spread partly through that pair of figures. "Three-quarters conformed at least once" slides easily into "three-quarters conformed". A story about caving also makes a sharper lesson than one about partial independence, and the sharper lesson is the one that gets repeated.

The record is clear on the proportion. Participants went along with an obviously wrong answer about a third of the time and held to what they saw about two-thirds of the time. A single dissenting ally sharply reduced even that third.

**Tell someone:** Asch's subjects held to what they saw on about two-thirds of critical trials, and one dissenting ally sharply cut conformity.

## Does waiting for a second marshmallow predict success in life?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** Early follow-ups linked a preschooler's ability to wait with later achievement. A larger 2018 replication found the link much weaker and largely explained by family background.

The popular version is a parable about willpower. Children who could wait for a second marshmallow, it says, grew into more successful adults, so self-control at four predicts success in life.

The study came from the psychologist Walter Mischel and colleagues at Stanford University around 1970. A child, typically aged between about three and a half and five and a half, was left alone with one treat and told that waiting until the experimenter returned, usually up to fifteen minutes, would earn two. Children who distracted themselves, or pictured the marshmallow as a cloud rather than food, tended to wait longer. In follow-ups, notably a 1990 study by Shoda, Mischel and Peake, preschool wait times were correlated with later measures such as SAT scores and parental ratings.

The limits are on record. The original follow-up samples were small and drawn from a relatively privileged Stanford-community population. In 2018 Watts, Duncan and Quan re-ran the design with about 900 children and controls for background, and the association shrank substantially. A child's willingness to wait can also reflect trust that the promised reward will arrive, and the reliability of the child's surroundings, not only self-control. The test records one moment in one artificial situation.

The verdict is DISPUTED rather than FOLKLORE because the record does not say the link is zero. It says the effect is much weaker and largely explained by background, so the strong claim is heavily qualified rather than refuted.

The strong version spread because it confirmed something many people already believed. It gave scientific weight to the intuition that resisting temptation pays, and it became a staple of popular writing on willpower, parenting and character. A small, privileged sample produced a clean story, and clean stories travel further than their samples.

The checked statement is modest. Waiting for a second marshmallow was associated with later achievement in small early samples. In a larger, more diverse study, most of that association reflected family background.

**Tell someone:** Waiting for a second marshmallow tracked later achievement in small early samples, but a larger 2018 study found the link largely explained by family background.

## Did the Robbers Cave study have a failed first attempt?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Before the famous 1954 Robbers Cave study, Muzafer Sherif ran a pilot in 1953 that failed. The boys resisted the hostility the researchers tried to induce, and suspected the staff.

The version usually taught is clean. Two groups of boys at a summer camp became rivals through competition, then reconciled by working together on shared goals.

That version is accurate as far as it goes. In 1954 Muzafer Sherif and Carolyn Wood Sherif, with colleagues, brought twenty-two boys aged eleven and twelve to a summer camp at Robbers Cave State Park in Oklahoma. The boys did not know each other and did not know they were being studied. Split into two groups, they named themselves the Rattlers and the Eagles. A tournament with prizes only for the winners produced name-calling, flag-burning and cabin raids. Pleasant contact between the groups did not reduce the hostility. Tasks that neither group could manage alone, such as restoring the camp's water supply, freeing a stuck truck and pooling money for a film, gradually did.

The failed attempt complicates the picture. In the 1953 pilot, the boys resisted the induced hostility and suspected the staff. The sources say this failure is often omitted, and that it raises questions about how readily the effect appears. The 1954 study has further limits on record. The sample was small and uniform: white, middle-class, Protestant American boys of similar age. Their parents were not told the study's true purpose.

The tidy version spread because it has the shape of a good lesson: conflict, then reconciliation. A failed pilot interrupts that shape. A retelling that starts from the 1954 results has no reason to mention an earlier camp where the plan did not work, so each retelling inherits the clean ending.

The record is clear on the sequence. The study that became famous in 1954 followed a 1953 attempt in which the boys resisted the induced hostility, and that first attempt belongs in the evidence.

## Was the forgetting curve based on just one person?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The forgetting curve rests on one subject: Hermann Ebbinghaus, who tested himself. Its best-known modern replication, in 2015, also used essentially one subject.

The popular version treats the curve as a general law of memory, measured across many people and describing how everyone forgets.

The record shows a single, methodical experimenter. In the early 1880s Ebbinghaus memorized lists of consonant-vowel-consonant nonsense syllables, such as "WID" and "ZOF", chosen to strip away prior meaning. He measured retention by the savings method: how much time or effort he saved when relearning a list after a delay. He published the results in 1885 as *Über das Gedächtnis*, translated in 1913 as *Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology*. The curve he found falls steeply in the first hours to a day after learning, then slows and levels toward a floor rather than dropping to zero.

In 2015 Murre and Dros replicated the curve in *PLOS ONE*. They largely confirmed its shape, but again with essentially one subject. "Replicated" here means the shape reproduces, not that it was validated across a large sample. They also found a probable upward jump around the 24-hour mark, so the curve is not perfectly smooth, and its usual exponential formula is an approximation.

The material matters as much as the sample. Nonsense syllables were chosen to mean nothing. Meaningful material is encoded and forgotten differently, and emotionally vivid memories do not follow the same decay.

The large-sample version spread because the curve looks like a law. It has a clean shape, a formula and a long history, and it underwrites practical tools, from flashcards to spaced-repetition software. A replication sounds like independent confirmation by many people, and few users of the curve ask how many people were in either study.

The record is plain on the point. The forgetting curve's shape has held up twice, and both times it was measured in essentially one person learning syllables designed to mean nothing. Its robust lessons are qualitative: forgetting is fast at first and then slows, and spaced review counters it.

**Tell someone:** The forgetting curve was measured on one man, Ebbinghaus, learning nonsense syllables, and its 2015 replication again used essentially one subject.

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** No one has settled who Little Albert was or what became of him. Rival candidates have been proposed, and the record says there is no settled answer.

The popular version has a tidy ending. The infant in the famous 1920 study, it says, was later identified, and his later life is known.

The study itself is on record. In 1920 at Johns Hopkins University, the behaviorist John B. Watson and Rosalie Rayner, his graduate student, set out to show that fear could be conditioned in a human being. Albert was an infant of around nine to eleven months who at first showed no fear of a white laboratory rat. The experimenters repeatedly presented the rat while striking a steel bar with a hammer behind his head. After several pairings he cried and tried to crawl away from the rat alone. His fear appeared to spread to a rabbit, a dog, a fur coat, cotton wool and a bearded Santa Claus mask, though unevenly. The conditioning was never reversed, and Albert left the study still fearful.

The study's evidence is thin. It had one subject and no controls, and it relied on the experimenters' own judgments of the child's reactions. Later reviewers, starting with Ben Harris in 1979, found little evidence that the animals consistently evoked fear.

The checked statement is narrow. The 1920 experiment happened, and its methods and flaws are on record. Who the infant was, and what became of him, is not settled.
