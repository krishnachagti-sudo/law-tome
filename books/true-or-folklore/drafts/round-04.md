## Is the "Mount Stupid" curve from the Dunning–Kruger paper?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The "Mount Stupid" curve is not in Kruger and Dunning's 1999 paper. The record describes it as a later internet embellishment.

The popular version is a picture. Confidence is plotted against experience. It shoots up to a peak labeled Mount Stupid after a little learning, falls as the size of the subject comes into view, and climbs slowly again with real expertise. The chart is usually captioned as the Dunning–Kruger effect.

The paper measured something else. Justin Kruger and David Dunning published "Unskilled and Unaware of It" in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* in 1999. They tested people on grammar, logic and humor, then asked them to estimate how they ranked. Low performers systematically overestimated their rank. Students who scored in the bottom quarter, around the 12th percentile on average, estimated that they were around the 62nd. The paper's subject is self-estimated rank set against actual rank. Confidence over a learning career, the curve's axis, is not what the 1999 studies reported.

The mountain is not in the paper at all. The record places it later and online, and no peak, valley or "experience" axis comes from the journal article.

The curve spread because it matches a feeling many people recognize. A little knowledge can feel like a lot: after one course or one article confidence can spike, and deeper study brings doubt back as the true size of a field comes into view. A shape that matches experience feels true, and a label like Mount Stupid is easy to share. It also makes a handy weapon in arguments. The record warns against that use: the effect is not a license to call people one disagrees with stupid.

What the paper found, and how well it holds up, is the next question in this round. The answer to this one is simpler. The 1999 paper compared people's estimates of their rank with their actual rank on tests of grammar, logic and humor. The mountain came later, from the internet, not from the journal.

**Tell someone:** The "Mount Stupid" curve appears nowhere in Kruger and Dunning's 1999 paper, which compared people's estimated rank with their actual test rank.

## Do the least skilled people really overrate themselves the most?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** In 1999 Kruger and Dunning found that the lowest scorers on several tests overestimated their rank. Since 2016 several researchers have argued that much of that pattern is a statistical artifact, and a 2026 reanalysis reports that it inverts.

The popular version is blunter than the paper. The incompetent, it says, are too incompetent to know it, while experts underrate themselves. In its shortest form: stupid people think they are geniuses.

The original finding is narrower. Justin Kruger and David Dunning's 1999 paper, "Unskilled and Unaware of It", tested people on grammar, logic and humor. Students who scored in the bottom quarter, around the 12th percentile, estimated they were around the 62nd. The explanation offered was that the knowledge needed to do a task well is largely the knowledge needed to judge how well it was done, so a beginner lacks both. Even taken at face value, the record describes the effect as a modest miscalibration, not a claim that the least able think they are the best.

The critics' case is statistical. In 2016 Nuhfer and colleagues showed that random numbers can produce the same picture, low performers overestimating and high performers underestimating. In 2020 Gignac and Zajenkowski argued that the usual method is confounded by regression to the mean and the better-than-average effect, and in their own sample of 929 adults found little support for it. People with extreme scores on one measure tend to drift toward the average on any second measure, so the pattern can appear whether or not a cognitive effect exists. In 2026 Dawson and de Meza went further in *Psychological Review*. Reanalyzing large replication datasets with methods that account for randomness in both the test score and the confidence prediction, they report that the pattern disappears and then inverts. In their analysis overconfidence appeared at every ability level and was most pronounced among the highest performers.

The pop version spread because it flatters whoever repeats it. It explains other people's confident mistakes and places the speaker among the self-aware. It also has the look of science: a named effect, a journal, a chart. Regression to the mean, the critics' explanation, is invisible in a chart and hard to explain in a sentence.

What is settled is narrow. The 1999 paper reported that low scorers overestimated their rank. Whether that reflects a real cognitive effect, a statistical artifact or the reverse of the popular story is disputed. The record's advice is to read it as a caution about one's own self-assessment, not a verdict on other people.

**Tell someone:** The 1999 finding that low scorers overrated their rank is disputed, and a 2026 reanalysis reports overconfidence was most pronounced among the highest performers.

## Is the famous inverted-U stress curve in Yerkes and Dodson's paper?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The smooth inverted-U curve of stress against performance is not a plot of Yerkes and Dodson's results. Their 1908 paper was a study of mice learning under electric shocks of different strengths.

The popular version is a single bell-shaped curve. Performance rises with arousal or stress to a sweet spot, then falls, and the curve is credited to a 1908 study.

The paper says something narrower. Robert M. Yerkes and John Dillingham Dodson published "The Relation of Strength of Stimulus to Rapidity of Habit-Formation" in the *Journal of Comparative Neurology and Psychology* in 1908. Japanese "dancing mice" learned a black-or-white discrimination in which one box gave a mild shock. Stronger shocks sped learning up to a point, then slowed it. A middle strength worked best in two of the three experiments, and the best strength depended on how hard the task was; in the easiest, learning simply sped up as the shocks grew stronger. The evidence came from forty mice. Arousal and stress were never measured. Shock intensity was reinterpreted as arousal decades later.

The curve was built afterward. The paper was cited only a handful of times for half a century. Interest revived in the 1950s, when D. O. Hebb's arousal concept, from 1955, was mapped onto the 1908 result and generalized to humans. The textbook graph reproduced everywhere is essentially Hebb's. Replication has been poor: follow-ups in chicks and kittens found no slowing at higher shock, and many human studies find simply that more stress means worse performance.

The curve spread because it matches a familiar feeling. Mild nerves before an exam can sharpen focus, while overwhelming anxiety impairs recall and reasoning. A smooth curve with two old names attached looks like settled science. Popularity also stripped out the awkward detail. The task-difficulty point, the part of the original most worth keeping, is the part the popular curve usually drops. That omission wrongly implies that high arousal harms even simple tasks.

The 1908 paper measured no stress and no humans. It reported how fast mice learned under shocks of different strengths, with a peak that depended on task difficulty and appeared in two experiments of three.

**Tell someone:** Yerkes and Dodson's 1908 paper measured no stress and no humans, only how fast forty mice learned under shocks of different strengths.

## Did Maslow draw the pyramid of needs?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Maslow did not draw the pyramid. The famous diagram was created by later authors, not by Maslow himself.

The popular version is a five-tier pyramid credited to Abraham Maslow, with physiological needs at the base and self-actualization at the tip, each layer to be completed before the next.

The theory is Maslow's. He set it out in a 1943 paper, "A Theory of Human Motivation", in *Psychological Review*, and developed it in his 1954 book *Motivation and Personality*. He arranged needs in ascending levels: physiological needs such as food, water, warmth and rest; safety; love and belonging; esteem; and self-actualization, the drive to become all one is capable of becoming. He argued that more basic needs must be substantially satisfied before higher ones become strong motivators. He also allowed that the ordering is not rigid, and that people may pursue several needs at once or in a different order. In later versions he added cognitive and aesthetic needs and, above self-actualization, self-transcendence.

A pyramid hides much of that. It has five fixed layers, each resting fully on the one below, and a summit that looks like a finish line, though Maslow held that self-actualization is never fully satisfied. His own account was looser than the shape, and later had more levels.

The theory's evidence is also weaker than its fame. A 1976 review by Wahba and Bridwell found little evidence for the strict hierarchy, and later research has not consistently confirmed that lower needs must be met first. Critics also see a Western individualist bias in placing self-actualization at the top.

The pyramid spread because it is easy to remember and easy to redraw. The theory moved out of psychology into management, education, marketing and healthcare, and the pyramid became one of the most recognizable images in the social sciences, credited to Maslow wherever it went. A picture that simple comes to stand for the theory, and the qualifications in the text are left behind.

The hierarchy is Maslow's and the pyramid is not. The earliest pyramid that Bridgman, Cummings and Ballard could trace is Charles McDermid's, in *Business Horizons* in 1960. It is clear that the diagram came from later authors, and that Maslow's own ordering was less rigid than the shape suggests.

**Tell someone:** The hierarchy of needs is Maslow's, but the pyramid came from later authors, and his own ordering was less rigid than the shape.

## Did Abraham Wald draw the famous bullet-hole plane?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Abraham Wald did not draw the bomber covered in red dots. The record describes that image as a designer's later recreation, a teaching device from decades after the war.

The popular version comes with a picture. A plane seen from above is spattered with red dots where returning bombers were hit. Wald, studying it, tells the military to armor the clean areas instead.

Wald's work was real and is dated. In 1943 he wrote memoranda on estimating how vulnerable aircraft were from the damage on planes that survived. The Center for Naval Analyses reprinted the work in 1980 as "A Method of Estimating Plane Vulnerability Based on Damage of Survivors". It was dense probability, not a sketch. Its central move was to account for the planes that never came back, rather than treating the survivors as a fair sample.

The picture came from somewhere else. The designer Cameron Moll says he drew the first version around 2005 for talks on problem solving, plotting invented red dots on an aircraft outline; the widely shared image is a recreation of his. The name for the error came later too. Survivorship bias is the mistake of studying only what came through a selection process while overlooking what did not, usually because the failures have vanished from view. Naming it as a general fallacy, and attaching it to the Wald story, came considerably later than 1943, and its popularity surged after the mid-2000s.

The picture spread because it does in one glance what the memoranda do in pages of mathematics. The reasoning behind survivorship bias is abstract: the missing data are the point, and missing data are hard to show. A drawing makes the missing planes visible by showing where the holes are not. As the term grew popular, the drawing became its emblem, and the emblem was credited to the man whose reasoning it illustrates.

The reasoning is Wald's, set out in 1943 and reprinted in 1980. The red-dotted plane is not. It was drawn decades later to teach that reasoning.

**Tell someone:** Abraham Wald's 1943 memoranda were dense probability, not a sketch, and the red-dotted bomber is a designer's later recreation, drawn decades after the war.

## Did Wald really tell the military to armor where returning planes weren't hit?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The reasoning in the famous story is Wald's. In his 1943 memoranda he estimated aircraft vulnerability from the damage on returning planes, while accounting for the planes that did not return.

The story is so neat that it is easy to doubt. A statistician looks at bombers riddled with holes and says to armor the places with no holes, because planes hit there never made it home. It sounds like a parable invented to teach a point.

The record treats the anecdote as partly embellished but its core as genuine. In the Second World War, Abraham Wald reasoned that armor should go where returning planes had the fewest bullet holes, not the most, since planes hit in those places did not come back. He made that reasoning rigorous in his 1943 memoranda, which the Center for Naval Analyses reprinted in 1980 as "A Method of Estimating Plane Vulnerability Based on Damage of Survivors". It was rigorous statistics, not a slogan. It accounted for the downed planes rather than treating the survivors as a fair sample.

The embellishments are the picture and the label. The red-dotted airplane was drawn decades later. The name "survivorship bias", and its attachment to Wald, also came later.

The idea Wald formalized now reaches well beyond aircraft. Survivorship bias is the error of studying only what came through a selection process while the failures drop out of view. Funds that perform badly are closed and dropped from databases, so the surviving set overstates average returns, by about 0.9 percent a year in one 1996 study. People admire old buildings and conclude they were built to last, forgetting the flimsy ones long since demolished.

Doubt about the story spread for a fair reason: parts of it were embellished. When a story arrives with a picture drawn long after the event, it is natural to suspect that the rest was invented too. A tale this tidy also looks like the kind made up to teach a lesson. Here the suspicion overshoots.

The reasoning behind the story is on record, dated 1943 and reprinted in 1980. Wald's method rested on the planes that never came back.

**Tell someone:** Wald did reason that armor belongs where returning planes had the fewest bullet holes, since planes hit there did not come back.

Juran generalized the pattern into a broad principle of the "vital few" and attached Pareto's name to it in the first edition of his *Quality Control Handbook*, in 1951. He built the principle around quality control: rank the causes of a problem, then fix the few that matter most.

Juran later corrected the attribution himself. In "The Non-Pareto Principle; Mea Culpa" (1974), he wrote that Pareto "had made extensive studies of the unequal distribution of wealth" and that he himself had "mistakenly applied the wrong name" to the rule. The principle as a universal, Juran wrote, "was not original with Pareto," and later authors "fabricated some embellishments" about him.

Suspicion of the round numbers is half right. Eighty and twenty measure different things, a share of causes and a share of effects, so their adding to 100 is a coincidence of the famous example. A real split might be 90/10, 70/30 or 99/1. The principle is about skew, formalized as a power-law, or Pareto, distribution. It is a rule of thumb, not a law of nature, and forcing 80/20 onto a roughly even phenomenon is a category error.

The skew does turn up in practice. In 2002 Microsoft reported that fixing the roughly 20 percent of most-reported bugs removed about 80 percent of the errors and crashes users actually hit. These are the company's own figures, not a controlled study.

The land story spread because it gives an abstract rule a concrete origin. A named economist, a country and a tidy split make a better story than a general finding about income, and a citation to the *Cours* as a whole, with no page, is hard to check. Juran's borrowed name then made the link look settled.

The 80 percent of Italy's land has not been found in Pareto's writing, and Juran later called the name his own mistake. What Pareto did document was the heavy concentration of income and wealth, the kind of skew the rule now describes.

**Tell someone:** The famous 80/20 split of Italy's land has not been traced to Pareto's text; what he actually documented was the heavily unequal distribution of income and wealth.

## Was Benford's law first spotted in worn logarithm tables?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The astronomer Simon Newcomb first noticed the leading-digit law in 1881, from the observation that the early pages of logarithm tables, those for numbers starting with 1, were the most worn. Frank Benford rediscovered it in 1938.

The popular version credits Frank Benford with the discovery, as the name suggests.

The law itself is odd enough to deserve the attention. In large sets of real-world numbers that span many orders of magnitude, 1 is the leading digit about 30 percent of the time and 2 about 18 percent, and the frequencies fall away to under 5 percent for 9. The distribution follows P(d) = log₁₀(1 + 1/d).

Newcomb set out the observation in "Note on the Frequency of Use of the Different Digits in Natural Numbers", in the *American Journal of Mathematics* in 1881. The clue was physical. The early pages of the tables, covering numbers that begin with 1, had worn fastest.

The physicist Frank Benford rediscovered the pattern in his 1938 paper "The Law of Anomalous Numbers". He tested it across more than twenty thousand values, among them river surface areas, populations, physical constants and molecular weights, and their leading digits followed the curve. The law was named after him.

Benford's name stuck because his paper did the work that makes a law famous. It gathered thousands of numbers from unrelated sources, all obeying one curve. A short note about worn pages in a book of tables is easier to overlook. The record calls the case a classic instance of Stigler's law of eponymy, which has its own entry in Round 5.

The law now has a working use and a notorious misuse. Auditors, following Mark Nigrini, screen ledgers and tax figures for sharp deviations that can flag fabricated entries for a closer look. It is also wrongly applied to election vote counts, where precinct totals sit in a narrow range and the law is not expected to hold. Deviation is a prompt to look closer, never proof of manipulation.

The law carries Benford's name and his 1938 evidence. The first observation was Newcomb's, in 1881, made from the wear on books of logarithms.

**Tell someone:** Simon Newcomb first spotted Benford's law in 1881, noticing that the early pages of logarithm tables, for numbers starting with 1, were the most worn.

## Did roulette land on black 26 times in a row at Monte Carlo?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The gambler's fallacy is real, but its most famous example is unverified. No 1913 record of a run of 26 blacks at Monte Carlo has been found; the earliest known account is a 1959 popular book on probability by Darrell Huff and Irving Geis.

The popular version is precise. On 18 August 1913, at the Monte Carlo Casino, the ball landed on black 26 times in a row, and with each black the gamblers grew surer that red was due, raised their bets on red, and lost heavily.

That version has no contemporary witness. Huff and Geis tell it in *How to Take a Chance* (1959), pp. 28–29, with players who "doubled and tripled their stakes" and a run that "enriched the Casino by some millions of francs," as they put it. The writer Robin Quinn searched the Times Digital Archive, the British Newspaper Archive and Google Books, found no mention before Huff, and suspects Huff invented it as an illustration. Wikipedia repeats the date and count without a 1913 source. The error itself is also called the Monte Carlo fallacy.

Whatever happened in 1913, the reasoning in the story is wrong because a roulette wheel has no memory. Each spin keeps its original odds, whatever came before. The mistake comes from misapplying the law of large numbers to short runs. That law says the proportions of outcomes settle down over a large number of trials. It does not say that short sequences must balance out. Early deviations are diluted by volume, not canceled. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman studied the error as a consequence of the representativeness heuristic: expecting a handful of trials to look like the long run.

The fallacy has a strict scope. It applies to independent trials. Where events are not independent, as when sampling without replacement, adjusting expectations from past outcomes is rational. A long run can also reasonably make someone suspect a biased wheel. The error is only the belief that a fair, memoryless wheel must correct itself.

The story spread because it is the ideal illustration. A run of 26 feels as though it must mean something, and a room of gamblers ruined by that feeling turns an abstract error into a scene. A date and a famous casino make an example sound like a record, and each retelling borrowed the precision of the last.

The 1913 run rests on nothing earlier than a 1959 book. The error it illustrates is real: each spin of a fair wheel, including one after 26 blacks, keeps the same odds as the first.

**Tell someone:** The famous 1913 run of 26 blacks at Monte Carlo has no known record before a 1959 book, but the gambler's fallacy it illustrates is real: a fair wheel has no memory.

## Did Marilyn vos Savant's correct Monty Hall answer draw a storm of objections?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** When Marilyn vos Savant gave the correct answer to the Monty Hall problem in 1990, some ten thousand readers wrote in, close to a thousand of them with doctorates, insisting she was wrong.

The story is told as a parable of expert overconfidence. A columnist got a probability puzzle right, and thousands of readers, many with advanced degrees, told her she had got it wrong. It sounds too neat to be true.

The puzzle is named for Monty Hall, host of the game show *Let's Make a Deal*. A contestant picks one of three doors, one of which hides a car. The host, who knows where the car is, opens another door to show a goat and offers a switch. Switching wins two times in three. The first pick is right one time in three, and that does not change when a door is opened. Because the host can never open the contestant's door or the car's, whenever the first pick was wrong he is forced to leave the car behind the other closed door.

The problem was first posed by the biostatistician Steve Selvin in a 1975 letter to *The American Statistician*. It became famous through vos Savant's 1990 "Ask Marilyn" column and the storm of objections that followed. She offered an intuition pump: with a hundred doors, a contestant picks one and the host opens ninety-eight goats, leaving two doors, one of which holds almost all the probability. The puzzle fooled professional mathematicians too. Paul Erdős reportedly stayed unconvinced until he was shown a simulation.

Two things fed the objections. Intuition says that two closed doors must be fifty-fifty, and intuition misjudges conditional probability badly. The original wording also did not pin down the host's rules. The two-thirds answer holds only if the host always opens a goat door, never the contestant's, always offers the switch, and chooses at random between goats when the first pick is the car. If he opens a door at random and it happens to show a goat, switching wins only half the time. The record says this ambiguity is much of why the dispute raged.

The storm of letters is on record, and so is the answer. Under the standard host rules, vos Savant was right: switching wins two-thirds of the time.

**Tell someone:** By her own estimate, some ten thousand readers, close to a thousand with doctorates, wrote in to tell Marilyn vos Savant she was wrong, and she was right.
