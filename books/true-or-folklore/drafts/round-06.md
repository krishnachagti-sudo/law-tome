## Do Eskimo languages have far more words for snow than English?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The answer depends on what counts as a word. By roots, Eskimo languages have roughly as many snow words as English; by whole words, specialist surveys find far more. The inflated figures of fifty and a hundred are myth.

The popular version holds that Eskimo languages have a great many words for snow, and offers this as proof that the language people speak shapes what they see.

Franz Boas listed four snow words in 1911 with no count attached. Benjamin Lee Whorf's 1940 popular article made the contrast explicit, setting English's one all-inclusive snow word against several Eskimo ones. Later writers inflated the number, to fifty by 1978 and a hundred in a 1984 *New York Times* editorial. The linguist Geoffrey Pullum's 1989 essay "The Great Eskimo Vocabulary Hoax," building on Laura Martin's 1986 study, attacked those numbers and found the root count roughly comparable to English.

The question stays open because the answer turns on what is counted. Pullum's root count stands, but surveys led by Igor Krupnik count about 40 terms in Central Siberian Yupik and at least 53 in Nunavik Inuktitut, and a 2010 study supports more frozen-water roots than English.

The hypothesis the story was meant to support is real, and more modest. Linguistic relativity is associated with Edward Sapir and Whorf in the early twentieth century, though they never co-authored it or called it a hypothesis; the label came later, commonly credited to Harry Hoijer. It comes in two versions. The strong one, that language determines and limits thought, is rejected by mainstream cognitive science. The weak one, that language influences habitual thought, has some support. Russian has separate basic words for lighter and darker blue, and Russian speakers tell blues apart across that boundary faster than English speakers. The advantage vanishes under verbal, but not spatial, interference. Speakers of Guugu Yimithirr, which uses compass directions rather than left and right, keep constant track of orientation. Effect sizes are modest, and some findings, such as those on grammatical gender, have failed to replicate.

The snow story spread because it does in one image what the real evidence does slowly. A people surrounded by snow, with words to match, is easy to picture and easy to retell. Pinning it on Whorf, whose name was already on the idea, made an anecdote look like scholarship. Each retelling then borrowed authority from the last.

So the counts of fifty and a hundred are myth, and the story began with Boas, not Whorf. Whether the real total is far above English depends on counting roots or words, and specialists still argue it. The firmer evidence for the weak hypothesis concerns blues and compass points.

**Tell someone:** Specialists count about 40 snow terms in Central Siberian Yupik and at least 53 in Nunavik Inuktitut; whether that beats English is disputed.

## Does the Coriolis effect decide which way a sink drains?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** At the scale of a sink, bath or toilet, the Coriolis effect is thousands of times too weak to set the direction of draining. Water drains both ways within the same hemisphere.

The popular version says the Earth's rotation makes water swirl down drains one way in the Northern Hemisphere and the other way in the Southern.

The record shows a real effect at the wrong scale. The Coriolis effect is the apparent deflection of a moving object when its motion is described from a rotating frame such as the turning Earth. Freely moving air or water appears to curve to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern. It is not a real force pushing the object but an inertial effect of viewing motion from a spinning frame. Its sideways push is proportional to the object's speed, and its horizontal strength scales with the sine of the latitude: zero at the equator, greatest at the poles. Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis gave the mathematical expression in an 1835 paper on rotating machines, and the effect entered meteorology in the early twentieth century.

Over long distances and long times, that weak nudge adds up. It makes large low-pressure systems rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern. It helps drive the great ocean gyres, and long-range shells travel far enough that gunners must correct for it. A basin of water does none of this. Its draining is set by the basin's shape, the angle of the jet that filled it and whatever motion is left in the water. Only under extreme laboratory control, with still, symmetric tanks left for hours, has a Coriolis signature in draining water been measured.

The myth spread because it borrows real physics. Storms do turn opposite ways in the two hemispheres, and a drain vortex looks like a small storm. The step from one to the other is natural and wrong, because the effect only builds up over large scales and long times.

So the effect is real and shapes the weather. In a sink it is swamped, and water drains both ways in either hemisphere.

**Tell someone:** The Coriolis effect is thousands of times too weak to set a sink's draining direction, so water drains both ways in either hemisphere.

## Is the uncertainty principle just about measuring disturbing things?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The uncertainty principle is not merely the idea that measuring a particle disturbs it. It says a quantum state cannot have sharply defined values of both position and momentum at once, and it holds even for perfect instruments.

The popular version runs like this: a particle's position and speed cannot both be known, because looking at it knocks it off course.

The record describes something deeper than clumsy measurement. Certain pairs of quantities, called conjugate variables, cannot both be defined to arbitrary precision at the same time. For position and momentum, the spread in position times the spread in momentum is at least the reduced Planck constant divided by two. This is a feature of quantum states themselves, which do not possess sharp values of both quantities at once. No state that breaks the bound can even be prepared.

The reason is the wave nature of matter. A quantum state's description in position and its description in momentum are Fourier transforms of each other, and a function sharply peaked in one is necessarily spread out in the other. Pin a particle down in space and its momentum fans out. A wave passing through a narrow slit shows the trade: narrow the slit and the position sharpens while the spread beyond it widens. The bound applies only to such pairs. It does not say everything is uncertain.

The disturbance reading spread partly through the principle's own history. Heisenberg introduced it in a 1927 paper in *Zeitschrift für Physik* with a thought experiment about a gamma-ray microscope, a picture of looking at a particle, and gave only an order-of-magnitude relation. Earle Kennard supplied the rigorous inequality the same year, and Hermann Weyl also contributed to it. The record notes a separate relation describing how a measurement disturbs a system; conflating the two has caused lasting confusion. A picture of looking is easier to hold than a Fourier transform.

So the principle is about the states, not the instruments. The precise bound, half the reduced Planck constant, comes from Kennard's 1927 inequality, not from Heisenberg's microscope.

**Tell someone:** The uncertainty principle holds even for perfect instruments, because a quantum state cannot have sharply defined values of both position and momentum at once.

## Was Schrödinger's cat meant to make a quantum idea look absurd?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Erwin Schrödinger devised the cat in 1935 as an objection to the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics. His aim was to show the absurdity of extending quantum superposition to a large object, not to claim that a cat can be alive and dead at once.

The popular version turns the argument around: Schrödinger proposed that a cat in a sealed box really is both alive and dead until someone looks.

The record describes a reductio ad absurdum. A cat is sealed in a box with a radioactive atom, a Geiger counter, a hammer and a flask of poison. If the atom decays, the counter trips the hammer, the flask shatters and the cat dies. Radioactive decay is a quantum event, and until it is measured the atom is in a superposition of decayed and not decayed. A naive reading of the formalism therefore puts the cat in a superposition of alive and dead until the box is opened. Schrödinger found that conclusion absurd for a cat, and used it to press the measurement problem: when and how the wavefunction collapses, and what counts as an observer.

The puzzle is still open, and interpretations answer it in incompatible ways. The Copenhagen view holds that measurement yields a single outcome. In the many-worlds interpretation the cat is alive in one branch and dead in another. Decoherence, a physical process rather than an interpretation, adds that contact with the environment suppresses interference between alive and dead almost instantly, so no such superposition can be observed. On its own it does not say why one outcome occurs. On none of these does collapse require a conscious observer.

The reversed reading spread because the image outlived the argument. A cat both alive and dead is a picture that sticks after one hearing. The objection attached to it takes a paragraph, and it gets dropped in the retelling. Popular versions then added a mystical observer whose mind settles the cat's fate, which the record calls a popular distortion of the physics.

So the cat was a critique. Schrödinger built it in 1935 to make an idea look absurd, not to claim that cats live double lives.

**Tell someone:** Schrödinger's cat was a critique: he devised it in 1935 to show that extending quantum superposition to a large object is absurd.

*Sources:* Schrödinger, "Die gegenwärtige Situation in der Quantenmechanik," *Naturwissenschaften* 23 (1935). "Schrödinger's cat," *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. "Schrödinger's cat," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/schrodingers-cat/

## Did vortex shedding bring down the Tacoma Narrows Bridge?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The 1940 collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge is more accurately explained by aeroelastic flutter, a distinct, self-excited mechanism. The record says the case should not be presented as simple vortex-street resonance.

The popular version attributes the collapse purely to vortex shedding: wind peeling eddies off the bridge in a steady rhythm until the structure shook itself apart.

Vortex shedding is real, but the example is misplaced. When a fluid flows past a blunt body and the Reynolds number that characterizes the flow passes a critical value, about 47 for a circular cylinder, the wake turns unstable. Vortices roll up and break away first from one side and then from the other, never both at once, and trail downstream in a staggered double row called a Kármán vortex street. The alternating shedding pushes the body one way and then the other. The rhythm is set by the Strouhal number, which links the shedding frequency, the size of the body and the speed of the flow. If that frequency matches a natural frequency of the structure, the push can drive resonance and damaging vibration. That is why some chimneys, masts and antennas are fitted with helical strakes or dampers, and why wires hum in the wind. Theodore von Kármán analyzed the pattern, building on earlier observations by Mallock and Bénard.

Tacoma Narrows, on the record's account, belongs to a different instability. It names that mechanism as aeroelastic flutter and calls it distinct and self-excited. In flutter the wind feeds energy into the deck's own twisting faster than the structure can damp it, so the motion grows with no need for a matching rhythm in the wind. The numbers fit flutter, not vortices: the destructive twisting ran at about 0.2 hertz, while vortex shedding at that wind speed would have been near 1 hertz.

The vortex version spread because it is a true mechanism attached to the wrong case. Vortex shedding does set structures shaking, and a famous collapse in the wind makes a tempting illustration. Both stories feature wind and oscillation, which makes the substitution easy to miss and easy to repeat.

So vortex shedding is real and can wreck a chimney. The famous bridge failure of 1940 is better explained by flutter.

**Tell someone:** Vortex shedding can wreck a chimney, but the 1940 Tacoma Narrows collapse is more accurately explained by aeroelastic flutter, a distinct, self-excited mechanism.

## Does air over a wing speed up to meet the air underneath?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Air does move faster over a wing's upper surface, and the pressure there is lower. But the popular reason, that the air must hurry to rejoin the air beneath, is a misconception that NASA debunks.

The popular version is the "equal transit time" story. The upper surface of a wing is longer, so air passing over it must speed up to meet the air passing underneath at the back of the wing. Faster air means lower pressure, and the wing is lifted.

The record keeps the conclusion and rejects the reason. NASA's Glenn Research Center explicitly debunks the equal-transit-time account. Symmetric wings and inverted flight both contradict it, and it predicts far too little lift. The record does not say why the upper air speeds up; it says only that this popular justification is wrong and that Bernoulli's principle does contribute to lift.

The principle itself is sound. Daniel Bernoulli set it out in *Hydrodynamica* in 1738, from experiments with liquids, and Leonhard Euler later derived the equation in its usual form. Along a streamline, faster flow means lower pressure. It is an energy budget: the kinetic energy, pressure energy and gravitational potential energy per unit volume add to a constant, so more speed leaves less pressure. Seen through Newton's second law, it is a pressure difference that accelerates a parcel of fluid, so the faster region sits at lower pressure. The standard form holds for steady, incompressible flow without viscosity. A narrowing pipe shows it plainly: the fluid speeds up through the constriction and its pressure falls, the Venturi effect.

The wrong story spread because it ends at the right answer. Faster air and lower pressure are both true, and equal transit time supplies a tidy reason for them. An explanation that reaches the correct result by a wrong route is hard to catch, because every check of the result seems to confirm it.

So Bernoulli's principle stands, and the air over a wing is faster. The idea that it races to meet the air below is the misconception.

**Tell someone:** Air over a wing moves faster at lower pressure and Bernoulli's principle aids lift; that air is not racing to meet the air below.

## Does hot water freeze faster than cold?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** Whether hot water can freeze faster than cold is unresolved. Erasto Mpemba and Denis Osborne published the claim in 1969, a careful 2016 study by Burridge and Linden found no evidence for a meaningful effect, and the record says the dispute remains open.

The popular version is simple: hot water freezes first, and the effect carries Mpemba's name.

The real story is longer and less settled. In 1963 Mpemba, a Tanzanian student, noticed that a hot sugared-milk mix for ice cream froze faster than a cooler one. He later published with the physicist Denis Osborne, in a 1969 paper titled "Cool?" in *Physics Education*. The idea is far older. Aristotle, Francis Bacon and Descartes each remarked that previously heated water can freeze quickly, though their observations are anecdotal. So Mpemba did not discover the idea first.

The modern evidence cuts against a robust effect. Burridge and Linden's 2016 study in *Scientific Reports* timed how long samples took to cool to 0 °C, not to freeze solid, and found no evidence for a meaningful effect. Misplacing a thermometer by as little as a centimeter could produce an apparent one. There is no agreed definition of what freezing faster means, and no accepted mechanism. The candidates include evaporation, which leaves less hot water to freeze; differences in convection; dissolved gases driven out by heating; differences in supercooling; and a hot container melting frost and improving thermal contact. Each is contested, and some may be artifacts. Some narrow studies report reproducing something under specific conditions, which is why the record stops short of calling it debunked.

The claim persists because it is counterintuitive, and a surprise is easy to remember and fun to repeat. It also has a memorable origin, a schoolboy and an ice-cream mix. And it is easy to get a result either way at home, because the outcome depends on the container, the volume and exactly what counts as frozen.

So it is not an established fact that hot water freezes faster, and it is not fully debunked. The careful test of 2016 found nothing meaningful, and the question remains open.

**Tell someone:** Whether hot water can freeze faster remains unresolved; a careful 2016 study found no evidence for a meaningful Mpemba effect.

## Does a broken bone heal back stronger?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** There is no evidence, contrary to popular belief, that a healed fracture becomes stronger than the original unbroken bone. The claim is an overstatement of Wolff's law, which describes how healthy bone adapts to the loads placed on it.

The popular version holds that a bone knits back stronger at the break than it was before.

The record finds no support for that and traces where the idea comes from. Wolff's law is frequently overstated as implying that broken bones heal back stronger. What the law actually says is narrower. The German anatomist and surgeon Julius Wolff set it out in his 1892 book *Das Gesetz der Transformation der Knochen* (The Law of Bone Transformation). Healthy bone adapts its structure to the loads it carries, growing stronger under more stress and weaker under less.

The mechanism is living tissue at work. Cells embedded in bone, called osteocytes, sense strain, largely through fluid flowing in tiny channels. They signal the cells that build bone and the cells that remove it. The internal lattice of the bone is reshaped first, and the outer wall can then thicken or thin. The record gives three cases. Racquet-sport players develop measurably denser bone in the arm that holds the racquet. After a hip replacement, the metal implant carries load the femur used to bear, and studies have recorded falls of roughly 10 to 40% in bone density around the upper femur in the first two years. Astronauts on long missions lose bone density because microgravity removes the loading signal.

Wolff's own strict mathematical formulation has been discredited in its precise form, and researchers now prefer the looser term "bone functional adaptation."

The myth spreads because the true part makes the false part sound like a corollary. Bone does get stronger under stress, and a break looks like the greatest stress of all, so stronger healing feels like the same rule applied once more. The step is an inference the evidence does not support.

So bone does adapt to load. A healed fracture becoming stronger than the original bone is not something the evidence shows.

**Tell someone:** No evidence shows a healed fracture becomes stronger than the original bone; healthy bone instead adapts to the loads it carries.

## Did Thomas Young shine light through two slits?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** Thomas Young showed that light interferes, in his Bakerian Lectures around 1801 and 1803. Whether he performed a literal two-slit experiment is disputed: his demonstrations used sunlight, pinholes and narrow cards.

The popular version has Young shining light through two slits around 1801, seeing bands on a screen and proving that light is a wave.

The record keeps the result and questions the apparatus. Young argued for the wave nature of light by showing that light passing two closely spaced openings produces interference fringes. His interference work is best dated to the Bakerian Lectures of around 1801 and 1803. But the tidy two-slit setup of the textbooks is partly a later idealization, since his actual demonstrations used sunlight, pinholes and narrow cards.

The physics is the same in either form. Light passing two narrow, closely spaced openings spreads out from each, and the two waves overlap. Where crest meets crest the light is bright; where crest meets trough it cancels. The result is a row of evenly spaced fringes whose spacing depends on the wavelength and the geometry. Those fringes are the hallmark of waves.

The experiment's later fame came from its quantum version. When electrons or photons are sent through one at a time, each lands as a single dot, yet the dots build up the same fringes, as Tonomura's 1989 experiment filmed with electrons. If a detector records which slit each particle takes, the fringes disappear. That is a physical effect of the detector, not of a conscious mind watching. Richard Feynman called the phenomenon the only mystery at the heart of quantum mechanics.

The neat two-slit picture of Young spread because the quantum version made the apparatus famous, and the history was drawn to match. A clean diagram with two slits is easier to teach than pinholes and cards, so the textbook setup was projected back onto its founder.

So Young's interference work around 1801 and 1803 is secure. Whether it used two slits as pictured is not, and the record says he worked with pinholes and cards.

**Tell someone:** Thomas Young showed light interferes, but whether he used two literal slits is disputed; his demonstrations used sunlight, pinholes and narrow cards.

## Was the Doppler effect tested with trumpeters on a train?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** C. H. D. Buys Ballot tested the Doppler effect for sound in 1845, using trumpeters on a moving train. Christian Doppler had predicted the effect three years earlier, in an 1842 paper in Prague.

The popular assumption is that the trumpeters belong in a textbook, not a railway timetable: the scene sounds like an illustration invented to explain a passing siren, not an experiment anyone ran.

The record says it was run. Doppler, an Austrian physicist, predicted the effect in 1842. Buys Ballot confirmed it for sound in 1845 with his railway experiment, and Hippolyte Fizeau described the effect independently for light in 1848.

The effect is a change in the frequency an observer detects when source and observer move relative to each other. Approaching, the observed frequency is higher than the emitted one; receding, it is lower. The source's own output never changes. As a source approaches, each wave cycle starts from a point a little closer to the observer than the last, so the crests bunch up ahead and spread out behind. For sound, which travels in a medium, the effect depends separately on how the source, the observer and the air are moving, so a moving source is not equivalent to a moving observer.

The doubt comes from how well the story fits a classroom. A brass note rising as the train approaches and falling as it passes is exactly what a teacher would invent to explain an ambulance siren, so it is easy to file as an invention. The record also shows that Doppler's own grand application went wrong. He thought the effect explained the visible colors of stars, but stellar speeds are far too small for that, and the real signature is a shift of spectral lines, as Fizeau clarified. The prediction's founder got its most ambitious use wrong; the modest test with trumpeters got it right.

So the trumpeters were real. Doppler predicted the effect in 1842, and Buys Ballot confirmed it for sound on a moving train in 1845.

**Tell someone:** The trumpeters were real: in 1845 Buys Ballot tested the Doppler effect for sound on a moving train, three years after Doppler predicted it.
