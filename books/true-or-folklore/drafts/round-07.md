# Round 7: Words and phrases

Where the famous expressions really come from.

Phrases travel faster than their sources. A line is quoted, then paraphrased, then credited to whoever is most famous for the idea, and after a few retellings the paraphrase is the version everyone knows. This round checks ten familiar expressions against the earliest text the record can find: who wrote the words, who wrote only something like them, and whose name was added later.

## Does "red herring" come from training hounds with smoked fish?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The hound-training origin is likely a myth. The figurative sense of "red herring" is real, and it is traceable to the journalist William Cobbett in 1807.

The popular version explains the phrase with a scene from the hunting field. Hunters, it says, dragged a strong-smelling smoked fish across a trail, either to train their hounds or to throw them off a scent. A red herring, on this account, is whatever pulls the dogs away from the trail they should be following.

The record does not support the practice. Michael Quinion's article "The Lure of the Red Herring" traces the figurative usage to Cobbett in 1807 and treats the hound-training practice as dubious. The phrase's documented history starts with a writer using it as a figure of speech, not with any recorded kennel routine. The record's hedge matters here: it calls the fish story likely a myth, not a proven fabrication.

The meaning the phrase settled into is precise. A red herring is something brought in to distract from the question at hand. Its fault is irrelevance, not falsehood: a true point that leads a discussion away from the issue is still a red herring. Logicians file it among the fallacies of relevance, close to the Latin *ignoratio elenchi*, the irrelevant conclusion. Writers of mysteries use the same term for a deliberate false clue that steers readers toward the wrong suspect, a legitimate device rather than a fault.

The hunting story spread because it fits the meaning so neatly. An odd phrase invites an explanation, and one that matches the sense of being led off a scent feels right on first hearing. Folk etymologies often work this way: the story is built backward from the meaning, and the fit is taken as proof. Repetition does the rest.

What can be stated with confidence is narrower than the legend. The figurative "red herring" is traceable to Cobbett in 1807. The hounds and the smoked fish are, on the record's evidence, likely a myth.

**Tell someone:** The hound-training story is likely a myth; the figurative "red herring" traces to the journalist William Cobbett in 1807.

## Is the "invisible hand" the heart of Adam Smith's economics?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Adam Smith used the exact phrase "invisible hand" only three times in his works, and just once in *The Wealth of Nations*. It was not the center of his economics, and he supported many roles for government.

The popular version makes the invisible hand Smith's master idea. The free market, left alone, turns private gain into public good, and Smith built his economics on that principle.

The record shows a narrower use. In *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), Smith wrote that an individual who intends only his own gain is led "by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention," namely the interest of society. His example was a merchant who prefers domestic to foreign industry for his own security and so, without meaning to, supports the wider economy. That is its only appearance in *The Wealth of Nations*. A study in *Econ Journal Watch*, "Adam Smith's Invisible Hands," documents how sparse and varied Smith's uses of the phrase were across his works.

Smith did not advocate unregulated laissez-faire. He supported government roles in defense, justice, public works and education. He was sharply critical of merchants, and warned that people of the same trade seldom meet without conspiring against the public. The invisible-hand result also breaks down under market failures such as externalities, monopoly, public goods and imperfect information, where self-interest does not serve society.

The phrase grew in other hands. The record says later economists inflated a limited observation into a sweeping principle of market efficiency, and the phrase became the central image of free-market economics. A two-word metaphor travels more easily than a long book's qualifications. Once it stood for the whole case for markets, readers expected to find it at the center of Smith's work, and its fame came to far outstrip its frequency in his writing.

The invisible hand is Smith's, and the economic passage is real. It appears once in that book, whose author also backed public works, courts and education, and distrusted merchants.

**Tell someone:** Adam Smith used "invisible hand" just once in *The Wealth of Nations*, and he backed public works, courts and education.

## Did habeas corpus come from Magna Carta?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Habeas corpus did not come from Magna Carta. Its roots predate the charter of 1215. It grew out of English common law over centuries, and the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679 codified it.

The popular version gives the writ a single birth certificate. Magna Carta, in 1215, created the right to make a jailer justify an imprisonment before a court.

The record treats that as a simplification. Magna Carta is often cited as a precursor, and often called the origin, but the writ traces to earlier English practice. Its beginnings are older and more diffuse than the charter story suggests. The protection developed through common law over centuries, until the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679 codified it. Long known as the Great Writ of liberty, it is also protected in the United States, where the Constitution's Suspension Clause forbids suspending it except in cases of rebellion or invasion when public safety requires.

What the writ does is also narrower than its reputation. It orders whoever holds a prisoner to bring that person before a court and justify the detention; the Latin name refers to producing the body for examination. It tests only whether the detention is lawful, not whether the prisoner is guilty. It is a procedural remedy, not a guarantee of release, since a lawful detention will be upheld. It can also be suspended in extreme circumstances. Abraham Lincoln's suspension during the American Civil War is the famous and controversial case, and the writ's reach over wartime and immigration detention has been repeatedly contested.

The charter story spread because it is tidy. A right with a date and a founding document is easier to teach than one that built up through courts over centuries. Because Magna Carta is so often cited as a precursor, the step from precursor to source is a short one, and retellings take it.

The record's account is less neat and better supported. The writ's roots are older than Magna Carta; it grew through English common law and was codified by the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679.

**Tell someone:** Habeas corpus has roots older than Magna Carta; it grew through English common law and was codified by the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679.

## Did Rousseau coin "the noble savage"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Jean-Jacques Rousseau did not coin "the noble savage." The phrase does not appear in his writings and was pinned on him later.

The popular version makes the phrase Rousseau's own summary of his view: people in a state of nature were noble, and civilization ruined them.

The record shows why the label was easy to attach, and why it is still wrong. Rousseau belongs with the great social contract theorists, who reasoned about government by imagining people without one. They drew opposite portraits. Thomas Hobbes, in *Leviathan* (1651), pictured that condition as a war of every man against every man, in which life is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short. John Locke saw relative peace under natural law, troubled chiefly by the lack of an impartial judge. Rousseau imagined natural humans as simple, healthy and essentially good, corrupted only later by society.

That is close to the label, but it is not the same thing. Rousseau treated the state of nature as a conjecture, a condition that perhaps never existed, not as a report on any real people. The record says that attaching the exact phrase to him distorts his more qualified view. The words themselves are not his.

The label spread because it compresses. Set beside Hobbes's brutish war, Rousseau's picture of good natural humans is easy to reduce to a two-word tag, and a tag can stand in for a long argument. Once it circulated with his name beside it, repetition gave it the look of a quotation. The record does not say who first pinned it on him, or when.

The fact is short. Rousseau wrote about natural goodness and the corrupting effect of society, and he hedged it as a thought experiment. "Noble savage" is a phrase other people used about him, not one he used himself.

The concept he described is distinct from his other famous idea. Fog is the uncertainty of information: incomplete, delayed, contradictory or false reports about one's own forces, the enemy and the battlefield. Friction is the accumulated practical difficulty of carrying anything out. Modern sensors have not lifted the fog either. ## Did "the prisoner's dilemma" begin without any prisoners?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The game came first and the prisoners second. Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher devised it at the RAND Corporation in 1950 as a table of payoffs. The mathematician Albert Tucker added the story of two prisoners later that year, to explain it to an audience of psychologists at Stanford.

The popular version is that the dilemma began as a story about two suspects held in separate cells, each offered a lighter sentence for betraying the other.

The record starts with the mathematics. RAND had recruited academics after the Second World War to think about strategy scientifically, and game theory was new. Flood and Dresher wanted to know whether players in a game without binding agreements, where one side's gain is not simply the other's loss, would reach what is now called a Nash equilibrium. In early 1950 they had two colleagues play their game a hundred times in a row. The players often cooperated, more than the theory predicted.

The payoffs carry the whole idea. Betraying a cooperator pays best, mutual cooperation next, mutual betrayal worse, and being the one betrayed worst. Betrayal therefore pays more whatever the other player does, so both betray, and each ends up worse off than if both had cooperated.

Tucker's contribution was the story, with prison sentences as the payoffs. The name is usually credited to him too, but the earliest use of "prisoner's dilemma" in print is Luce and Raiffa's 1957 textbook, and the original handout for Tucker's talk did not carry the phrase.

The story spread because it is easier to retell than a table. Two suspects, a bargain and a betrayal turn four numbers into a plot with a moral, and the same plot fits arms races and price wars. A good illustration can outlive its occasion until it seems to be the origin.

So the order is documented. The game came from Flood and Dresher at RAND in 1950, and the prisoners arrived afterward, as Tucker's way of explaining it.

**Tell someone:** The prisoner's dilemma began in 1950 as a RAND payoff game by Flood and Dresher; Albert Tucker added the prisoners later that year to explain it.

The popular version treats the figure as a law of history. Three in four power transitions end in war, so a rising China and a ruling United States are probably headed for one.

The phrase is older than the count: in a 1980 lecture the novelist Herman Wouk asked how to break out of a "Thucydidean trap" in the American and Soviet rivalry. Allison revived it in a 2012 *Financial Times* article, developed it in *The Atlantic* in 2015 and in the 2017 book *Destined for War*. He anchored it in Thucydides's line that it was the rise of Athens and the fear this instilled in Sparta that made war inevitable. The case file, published by Harvard's Belfer Center, lists the sixteen rivalries.

The claim is narrower than the slogan. Allison does not say war is inevitable. He credits the four peaceful cases to imaginative statecraft, and avoiding war is the point of his argument.

The dispute is about the count. Critics among historians and international-relations scholars argue that the sixteen cases were not chosen by a rigorous, repeatable rule, so the ratio may reflect the selection. The coding of war or no war, and of which side was rising, is often ambiguous. Critics also call the idea single-cause and say it misreads Thucydides, whose account of the war's causes is many-sided. And even granting the tally, correlation is not causation.

The figure spread because a clean ratio under an ancient name sounds measured. A number turns a judgment into what looks like a finding, and the China question makes it urgent.

So the count is accurate as a count. Whether twelve of sixteen is evidence of a trap is still argued over.

## Did Richard Dawkins coin the word "meme"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Richard Dawkins coined the word "meme" in his 1976 book *The Selfish Gene*, as the name for a unit of cultural transmission.

The popular assumption runs the other way. The word is so common in everyday talk that it is easy to take it for slang that grew up on its own, with no author and no date.

The record gives both. The meme appears in the same 1976 book that set out the selfish gene, the gene-centered view of evolution, for a general readership. Dawkins built on earlier work by W. D. Hamilton, George C. Williams (notably his 1966 *Adaptation and Natural Selection*) and Robert Trivers. In that view natural selection acts at the level of genes, the replicators that copy themselves across generations, and organisms are the survival machines, or vehicles, that carry them. "Selfish" is a metaphor for a gene's tendency to promote its own replication, not a claim that animals or people are innately selfish.

The meme was his analogy for culture. It is a unit of cultural transmission, like the gene, and subject to its own selection.

The gene-centered view itself remains contested. Critics argue that selection acts on whole organisms or groups, and Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin attacked it as reductionist. Many argue that gene-level and individual-level accounts often describe the same process in different terms. The word "meme" is not part of that dispute. Its source is a single, dated book.

The assumption that it has no author follows a common pattern. Words that pass into everyday use tend to shed their origins, and the more ordinary a word becomes, the less often anyone asks who made it.

This one has a maker and a year. Dawkins coined "meme" in *The Selfish Gene* in 1976.
