# Round 8: Jokes that became laws

Satire, office humor and asides that hardened into rules.

Some laws began as a wink. A satirist needed a rule to hang an essay on, a mathematician left a boast in a margin, colleagues traded remarks over lunch. The rule outlived the occasion, and often the joke was the first thing forgotten. This round asks which famous laws were meant seriously, which were not, and how much of the original humor the record lets survive.

## Did Parkinson's law begin as a joke in *The Economist*?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Parkinson's law began as satire. The naval historian C. Northcote Parkinson opened a satirical essay in *The Economist* on 19 November 1955 with the "commonplace observation" that made his name: work "expands so as to fill the time available for its completion."

The popular version treats it as a management finding, a rule distilled from research into how organizations work.

The record shows a satirist with a target. Parkinson's evidence was the British Admiralty, whose bureaucracy kept growing as the fleet shrank. Officials, he argued, make work for one another regardless of the work to be done. The essay was later expanded into a 1957 book, *Parkinson's Law, or The Pursuit of Progress*, which carried the joke further. He offered a bureaucratic corollary: an organization's staff grows by a roughly fixed percentage each year, which he estimated at five to seven, independent of the workload. A later financial version held that expenditure rises to meet income, so that money, like time, is consumed by whatever is available.

The figures gave the satire the look of a study. Numbers, a named institution and a crisp general rule are the furniture of research, and satire that borrows them is easily read straight. The opening line also describes something most office workers recognize. An hour-long meeting fills the hour; a fifteen-minute slot covers the same ground. A law that matches daily experience does not need its source checked, and the satirical frame fell away in the retelling.

Read as a serious rule, it also gets stretched. The record is careful about what it does not say. It is not proof that every deadline should be brutal or that rushed work is as good. Past a point, compression destroys quality, and creative or research work needs unpredictable slack. The useful reading is narrower: generous time is not free, because it quietly recruits busywork.

The record is clear on the origin. Parkinson's law is a line from a satirical essay in *The Economist*, 19 November 1955, about an Admiralty that grew while its fleet got smaller.

**Tell someone:** Parkinson's law began as a 1955 satirical essay in *The Economist* about an Admiralty whose bureaucracy grew as its fleet shrank.

## Was the Peter Principle written as satire?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The Peter Principle was pitched as satire. The educator Laurence J. Peter and the playwright Raymond Hull set it out in their 1969 book: "In a hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to his level of incompetence." It landed as serious management theory.

The popular version treats the principle as a finding of management research, a measured law of how organizations promote people.

The record shows a different pedigree. The book's subtitle was *Why Things Always Go Wrong*. Its co-author was a playwright. The mechanism it described is simple: promotion rewards being good at the job a person already has, not the job they are moving into, so people keep rising until they reach a role they are bad at, and there they stay. Peter and Hull added a corollary of their own: "In time, every post tends to be occupied by an employee who is incompetent to carry out its duties."

Others extended it in the same spirit. Scott Adams's Dilbert Principle, from the 1990s, held that companies deliberately promote their least competent workers into management, to limit the damage they can do to the product. Archibald Putt's version for technical organizations runs: "Technology is dominated by two types of people. Those who understand what they do not manage, and those who manage what they do not understand." A small literature of corollaries grew from a book meant to amuse.

The satire was taken seriously because the mechanism is real. The record's examples are familiar: the best salesperson made sales manager, the strongest engineer moved into a job about politics and people, the gifted researcher buried in budgets. Where promotion rewards current performance and the next role differs in kind, the effect bites. A joke that describes a real failure mode is easy to read as a finding.

The record also marks its limits. It is a critique of one promotion mechanism, not a claim that everyone ends up incompetent. Plenty of people keep growing into larger roles, and organizations that promote on demonstrated fitness for the next job escape it.

The origin stands. Peter and Hull wrote the principle in 1969 as satire, and readers turned it into management theory.

**Tell someone:** The Peter Principle was pitched as satire, written in 1969 by an educator and a playwright under the subtitle *Why Things Always Go Wrong*.

*Sources:* Peter & Hull, *The Peter Principle: Why Things Always Go Wrong* (1969). "Peter principle," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-peter-principle/

## Was there a real committee that argued over a bike shed?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** No real meeting lies behind the story. The reactor, the bicycle shed and the refreshment budget are C. Northcote Parkinson's invented example, from his 1957 book *Parkinson's Law, or The Pursuit of Progress*.

The popular version reports it as something that happened: a committee approved a nuclear reactor in minutes and then argued for far longer about a bike shed.

The record places the scene in an essay titled "High Finance, or the Point of Vanishing Interest." Parkinson's finance committee approves a £10 million atomic reactor in about two and a half minutes. It argues for three-quarters of an hour over a £350 bicycle shed. It then spends over an hour on a refreshment budget of £21 a year, the smallest sum drawing the longest fight. Everyone understands a shed, and no member wants to admit he cannot follow the reactor. From this Parkinson drew his law of triviality: the time a committee spends on an item is inversely proportional to the money involved.

Parkinson wrote as a satirist. The record calls the committee and its figures a fictional illustration rather than data, and the "inverse proportion" phrasing rhetorical. It is also a separate idea from his better-known law that work expands to fill the time available, though both come from the same book.

The name arrived much later. In 1999 the FreeBSD developer Poul-Henning Kamp, exasperated by an endless mailing-list thread, sent an email headed "A bike shed (any colour will do)…" on 2 October. It popularized "bikeshedding" for teams that agonize over trivia while real design decisions pass unexamined.

The scene passes for a true story because it is built like one. Exact sums and exact timings read like the minutes of a meeting, and satire often borrows the look of a report. Once the scene was retold apart from its book, the figures were what remained, and they sounded like evidence.

The committee never met. The pattern it was invented to show is what the record's everyday examples describe: a large budget nodded through in minutes, then a long debate about a logo's color or the office snacks.

**Tell someone:** The committee that approved a reactor in minutes and argued over a bike shed never met; C. Northcote Parkinson invented it in 1957.

## Did Brooks admit his own law was an oversimplification?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Fred Brooks, who coined Brooks's law in *The Mythical Man-Month* (1975), freely admitted that the law oversimplifies a real tendency: he introduced it with the words "Oversimplifying outrageously."

The popular version treats it as an iron law of software: never add people to a late project, because it will only get later.

The record shows an author more careful than his slogan. The law reads: "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later." Brooks drew it from his experience managing the development of IBM's OS/360, and he gave two mechanisms. New people need time to learn the work, and the existing team must teach them. Communication overhead also grows faster than headcount. A team of n people has n(n−1)/2 pairwise links to keep in sync, so doubling a team from five to ten takes the paths from 10 to 45, and a team of 50 carries 1,225. His analogy for work that cannot be divided: "The bearing of a child takes nine months, no matter how many women are assigned."

The limits are just as clear. Adding people early, before a project is late, can help. A project may be "late" only because its schedule was fantasy. Skilled developers who already know the codebase avoid most of the ramp-up penalty. For divisible tasks, more hands produce more output. The law bites hardest on tightly coupled work under heavy communication load, and strong modularity and clear ownership shrink it.

The iron-law reading spread because the sentence is short and the experience behind it is common. A team that has watched a rescue hire slow everything down needs no further proof. The caveats live in the surrounding chapters, and caveats rarely travel with a slogan.

The author's own verdict is on the record. Brooks stated his law in 1975, explained when it applies, and introduced it with "Oversimplifying outrageously."

**Tell someone:** Fred Brooks introduced his own law, that adding manpower to a late software project makes it later, with the words "Oversimplifying outrageously."

## Did Clarke add "Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic" years later?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Arthur C. Clarke's most famous law came last. He stated his First and Second Laws in 1962 and added the Third, "Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic," to the revised 1973 edition of the same book, having first published it in a 1968 letter to *Science*.

The popular version treats the three laws as a set, written together.

The record shows them arriving over a decade. The first two appeared in Clarke's essay "Hazards of Prophecy: The Failure of Imagination," in *Profiles of the Future* (1962). The First Law: "When a distinguished but elderly scientist states that something is possible, he is almost certainly right. When he states that something is impossible, he is very probably wrong." The Second: "The only way of discovering the limits of the possible is to venture a little way past them into the impossible." His examples were experts who called things impossible. The astronomer Simon Newcomb argued that heavier-than-air flight was impossible; the Wright brothers flew in 1903. Lord Rutherford dismissed the prospect of practical atomic energy.

The Third Law came later, and Clarke was frank about why he stopped there. In a footnote he wrote that "as three laws were good enough for Newton, I have modestly decided to stop there."

The record is clear that none of the three is a law in the scientific sense. They are aphorisms, with no predictive formalism, and the word "law" is playful. The Third is about the onlooker, not the machine: an observer who lacks the mechanism cannot tell engineered cause and effect from the inexplicable. It does not say technology is magic. An inversion, "Any sufficiently advanced magic is indistinguishable from technology," is usually credited to Larry Niven, though the attribution is contested.

The idea that the laws were written together comes from their form. A numbered set of three looks designed as a unit, and the most quoted member is the one people assume came first. Newton's three laws, which Clarke himself invoked, set the template.

The dates settle it. The First and Second Laws are from 1962. The Third appeared in 1968 and joined them in the book in 1973.

**Tell someone:** Clarke's First and Second Laws date from 1962; the magic one came last, in a 1968 letter to *Science*, joining the book in 1973.

## Has toast landing butter-side down been studied seriously?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** In 1995 the physicist Robert Matthews published "Tumbling toast, Murphy's Law and the fundamental constants" in the *European Journal of Physics*. It won a 1996 Ig Nobel Prize, and it argued that toast does tend to land butter-side down, for mundane physical reasons.

The popular version treats falling toast as a joke about bad luck with no science behind it, the emblem of Sod's law.

The law itself is a joke, and an old one. Sod's law is a British colloquialism of obscure, undated origin, used where North Americans say "Murphy's law." Its name comes from the "unlucky sod" to whom misfortune happens: if something can go wrong, it will, and at the worst possible time. The record explains most of it as selective memory. People remember the rain on the day they forgot an umbrella and forget the dry days. The other queue seems faster because a lagging queue is the one people notice. Richard Dawkins dismissed the idea that events "know" what a person wants.

The toast is the exception. Matthews's paper is described in the record as tongue-in-cheek but genuine physics. Dropped from a typical table height, a slice rotates only about half a turn before it lands. A slice that starts butter-up therefore tends to arrive butter-down. Physics, not superstition, tips it over, which makes the law's most famous example the one with a real deterministic basis.

The assumption that nobody had studied it is understandable. The toast is the mascot of a law built on bias, so it is natural to assume it is bias too. A physics paper about breakfast also sounds like a joke, and this one was written tongue-in-cheek.

The record holds both facts. Sod's law is mostly a trick of memory. Toast landing butter-side down is not, and a 1995 paper in the *European Journal of Physics* worked out why.

**Tell someone:** A 1995 physics paper argued that toast dropped from table height rotates only about half a turn, so it tends to land butter-side down.

## Did *Harvard Business Review* reject Conway's law?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** By Melvin Conway's own account, *Harvard Business Review* rejected his paper on the grounds that he had not proved his thesis. "How Do Committees Invent?" appeared instead in *Datamation* in April 1968.

The popular assumption is that a law now quoted so widely in management and software circles must have arrived through a leading management journal.

The record gives it a humbler start. Conway's own wording was that organizations "are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations." After the *Datamation* paper, participants at the 1968 National Symposium on Modular Programming gave the idea the name "Conway's Law." Fred Brooks later cited and popularized it in *The Mythical Man-Month* (1975).

The rejection looks less foolish with time, not more. *HBR*'s objection was that the thesis was unproved, and for decades it was. The empirical support arrived much later. In 2012 MacCormack, Baldwin and Rusnak compared software products of near-identical function, some built by tightly coupled commercial firms and some by loosely coupled open-source communities. The products' modularity mirrored the coupling of the organizations that made them.

The law has since become a method. The "inverse Conway maneuver," coined by Jonny LeRoy and Matt Simons in 2010, runs it backward: to get a target architecture, first reshape the teams to match it. The record still calls the law a strong tendency, not an iron rule. A small, co-located team can build a well-factored system without visible mirroring, and reorganizing teams will not instantly refactor a rigid legacy system.

The prestigious-journal assumption reads a law's later fame back into its birth. Ideas that end up central tend to be imagined as having started at the center. This one started with a rejection.

The record is plain on the facts. *Harvard Business Review* turned the paper down as unproved, *Datamation* published it in April 1968, and the evidence that it was right came decades later.

**Tell someone:** By Melvin Conway's own account, *Harvard Business Review* rejected the paper behind his law as unproved; the evidence that it was right came decades later.

## Did Fermat have a proof too big for the margin?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The margin note is real; the proof almost certainly was not. Around 1637 Pierre de Fermat wrote in his copy of Diophantus's *Arithmetica* that he had a marvelous proof the margin was too narrow to contain, but the record judges he almost certainly had no valid general proof. The accepted proof, by Andrew Wiles with Richard Taylor, was published in 1995 and rests on twentieth-century mathematics.

The popular version takes Fermat at his word. He had a short, elegant proof, and the world lost it to a narrow margin.

The note is documented. Fermat's claim was that no three positive whole numbers satisfy a to the n plus b to the n equals c to the n for any power above two. For the power two there are endless answers, such as 3, 4 and 5; above two, he said, there are none. His son published the note in 1670, after Fermat's death.

The proof is not. Fermat did prove the case of the fourth power, by his method of infinite descent. He posed the cases of three and four as challenges to other mathematicians but never the general one, and he never mentioned the marvelous proof again. Leonhard Euler published a proof for the cube in his *Algebra* of 1770, and others extended the range case by case. No elementary proof has ever been found. Wiles announced his in 1993; a gap was found and repaired with Taylor, ending a search of 358 years.

The margin story spread because it is the most famous aside in mathematics. A boast with a cliffhanger invites everyone to try, and a lost proof makes a better legend than a mistaken one. The record's reading is plainer: the method that finally worked, proving that certain curves are modular, was far beyond any tool Fermat had. The MacTutor history suggests he may well have realized his proof was wrong.

So the note is real, c. 1637, and the proof is not part of the result. The record says Fermat almost certainly did not have one.

**Tell someone:** Fermat's margin note claiming a "marvelous proof" is real, but he almost certainly had none; the accepted proof, published in 1995, needed twentieth-century mathematics.

## Did Kelly Johnson coin "Keep it simple, stupid"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The phrase is attributed to Kelly Johnson, lead engineer at Lockheed's Skunk Works, but the attribution is not airtight. A 1938 *Minneapolis Star* article used the variant "keep it short and simple," before Johnson's association with the phrase, and the earliest dated record of the acronym itself is a U.S. Navy "Project KISS" in 1960.

The popular version credits Johnson alone, the engineer who coined KISS.

The record supports a strong association and no clean first use. The phrase is linked to Johnson's reported instruction that the aircraft his engineers designed be repairable in the field by an average mechanic with a few basic tools. An aircraft only its designers can service is worse than a simpler one anybody can, because a fleet is judged on availability, not peak performance. The Navy documented its Project KISS in 1960, and the term was in general use by 1970. The 1938 newspaper variant sits awkwardly in front of all of this.

The punctuation matters too. By one account Johnson wrote the phrase without a comma, so "stupid" is not an insult aimed at the reader. The instruction is to keep the thing simple, and foolproof. The famous version, comma included, turns a design rule into a scolding.

The principle has a real weakness, which the record states plainly: "simple" is not defined. Two engineers who agree on KISS will disagree about which design is simpler, both honestly. A problem with essential complexity cannot be made simple, only made to look simple in one place and worse in another, and "we kept it simple" is a common post-mortem finding.

The attribution to Johnson spread because it makes the better story. A famous engineer at Lockheed's Skunk Works, with a field-repair anecdote attached, is easier to retell than a newspaper variant and a Navy project record. Stories about origins favor a person over a paper trail.

The safe statement is the hedged one. KISS is attributed to Kelly Johnson, the Navy recorded it in 1960, and a 1938 variant predates his association with it. Who said it first is not settled.

**Tell someone:** KISS is attributed to Kelly Johnson, but a 1938 newspaper already used "keep it short and simple," so who said it first is not settled.

## Did Fermi pose the Fermi paradox over lunch?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The question began as a lunchtime remark by the physicist Enrico Fermi at Los Alamos in 1950, with his colleagues Teller, Konopinski and York. Firsthand accounts differ on his exact words, and the paradox's name and rigorous form came later, from others.

The popular version has Fermi at lunch asking, "Where is everybody?" and launching the paradox on the spot.

The record confirms the lunch and loosens the rest. The remark was informal. Fermi did not coin the name or write the paradox up. The 1950 exchange is reconstructed from firsthand accounts that disagree about what he said, so "Where is everybody?" is the usual rendering rather than a verified quotation. The question was first put into print by Carl Sagan. Michael Hart gave it rigorous form in 1975, and Frank Tipler followed.

Hart's version made the tension sharp. A civilization, he argued, could spread across the galaxy in far less time than the galaxy's age, so the absence of any civilization reaching Earth suggests none exists. The paradox sets that argument against the numbers: hundreds of billions of stars, an abundance of planets, and a great span of time in which intelligent life should have arisen and spread or signaled.

It is an argument, not a law, and it proves nothing about whether aliens exist. Its force depends on the premise that intelligent life should be common, which rests on the unknown terms of the Drake equation. Proposed resolutions range from Robin Hanson's Great Filter, some improbable step between a lifeless planet and a galactic civilization, to the Rare Earth view that complex life is uncommon, to the possibility that civilizations exist but cannot be detected. There is no consensus.

The popular version is mostly right, and its errors are of a familiar kind. A single quip from a famous physicist is a better story than a lunch remembered differently by those present, followed by decades of work by others. The name then credits Fermi with an argument he never wrote down.

The record supports the lunch. Fermi raised the question at Los Alamos in 1950; the exact words are unknown, and the paradox as an argument belongs to later writers, starting with Hart in 1975.

**Tell someone:** Fermi did raise the question over lunch at Los Alamos in 1950, though firsthand accounts differ on his exact words.
