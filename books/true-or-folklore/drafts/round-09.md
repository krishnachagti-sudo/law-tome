# Round 9: Discovery stories

Who found what, and how the credit was shared out.

Scientific credit is handed out in stories, and a story wants one hero. The questions in this round ask who took a famous photograph, who first saw a pattern, who wrote a result down first and how they wrote it. Some answers move credit to a less famous name. Some confirm a detail that sounds invented. A few cannot be settled from the surviving record.

## Was Photo 51 taken by Raymond Gosling?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Raymond Gosling, a doctoral student, took Photo 51 at King's College London in May 1952, while working under Rosalind Franklin's supervision. The credit belongs to both of them.

The popular version gives the photograph to Franklin alone. She is the person the image is most closely associated with, and the short telling of the DNA story has no room for her student.

The record is more exact. Photo 51 is an X-ray diffraction image of the hydrated, B-form of DNA. Gosling made it with Franklin's carefully prepared, humidity-controlled DNA fibers, exposing the sample to X-rays for a total of 62 hours, by Franklin's notebook. The film recorded dark spots arranged in an X. A helix produces exactly that cross when it scatters X-rays, so the X was direct evidence that DNA is helical. The spacing of the spots carried numbers too: the width of the helix and the distance over which it repeats.

Those clues fed into the model of the double helix that James Watson and Francis Crick built in 1953, along with data from Franklin's unpublished reports. The image did not hand over the whole structure on its own. The model also needed chemical knowledge, the base-pairing insight and other data, including Franklin's own measurements. How the photograph reached Watson is the next question in this round.

The shorter version spread because the photograph became a symbol. It stands for an argument about how Franklin's data and her credit were handled, and a symbol of overlooked credit tends to gather all of that credit to the person it represents. A supervisor's name also travels further than a student's, in science as elsewhere. The record makes the point directly: saying simply that Franklin took the photograph omits Gosling's role.

So the attribution has two names in it. Gosling set up the exposure on 2 May 1952. He did it with Franklin's fibers and under her supervision, and the image is most associated with her experimental technique. Both statements are true, and leaving out either one misstates who made Photo 51.

**Tell someone:** Photo 51 was taken by Raymond Gosling, a doctoral student, in May 1952, using Rosalind Franklin's fibers and working under her supervision.

## Did Watson and Crick steal Rosalind Franklin's data?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The record documents that Maurice Wilkins showed Photo 51 to James Watson without Franklin's knowledge, and that her data shaped the 1953 model. Recent scholarship argues that this was not a simple story of theft.

The popular version is a heist. Watson and Crick took Franklin's photograph behind her back and won the Nobel Prize on it.

Part of that is in the record. Wilkins showed the image to Watson without Franklin's knowledge or permission. The X-shaped pattern, and the measurements its spots encoded, fed into the double-helix model that Watson and Crick built in 1953. They also used data from Franklin's unpublished reports. Watson, Crick and Wilkins shared the 1962 Nobel Prize. Franklin had died in 1958, and the prize is not awarded posthumously.

Other parts do not hold as told. The photograph did not reveal the structure by itself. Building the model also needed chemical knowledge, the base-pairing insight and other data, including Franklin's own measurements. And the ethical account is being revisited. In a 2023 article in *Nature*, Cobb and Comfort argue that Franklin was an equal contributor who understood the significance of her data. They also argue that there was cooperation as well as rivalry between the two teams. On their reading, the familiar story of a victim whose data was stolen leaves too much out.

The heist version spread because it has a clear wrong and a clear victim. Franklin received far less recognition in the popular story than the three men who shared the prize, and her death before the award left her outside it. A story about lost credit is easier to tell as a theft than as a mix of cooperation, rivalry and an image shown without consent. A single photograph also makes a better symbol than a stack of unpublished reports.

What the record supports is narrower than the legend. Wilkins showed Photo 51 to Watson without Franklin's knowledge, and her data went into the 1953 model, which raises lasting questions about how her data and her credit were handled. Whether that amounts to theft is a judgment, not a finding. The latest scholarship the record cites, Cobb and Comfort in 2023, argues that it was a more complex history in which Franklin was an equal contributor.

**Tell someone:** Wilkins showed Photo 51 to Watson without Franklin's knowledge; recent scholarship argues the full history was more complex, with Franklin an equal contributor.

## Did Philippa Foot invent the trolley problem?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Philippa Foot introduced the original case in 1967, in a paper called "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect." She did not name it. Judith Jarvis Thomson called it the trolley problem and added its best-known variants in 1976 and 1985.

The popular version comes in two forms. One treats the puzzle as an old one, too old to trace. The other treats it as a modern psychology test.

The record gives it a clear birth. In the basic case a runaway trolley will kill five people on the track. In Foot's version its driver can steer onto a side track, where it would kill one; in the version most retold, a bystander pulls a lever to divert it. Most people judge that diverting it is permitted. Foot's case appeared in a paper about the doctrine of double effect, the principle that harm intended as a means is worse than equal harm merely foreseen as a side effect.

Thomson named the problem and sharpened it. In her footbridge variant, the only way to stop the trolley is to push a large stranger off a bridge into its path. Most people judge this forbidden, though it too trades one life for five. In her transplant case, a surgeon could save five patients by taking the organs of one healthy visitor, and almost everyone judges this monstrous.

The psychology came later. In 2001 Joshua Greene's team brought brain imaging to the cases, reporting in *Science* that personal dilemmas such as the footbridge engage emotional brain systems while impersonal ones engage deliberative systems. Those findings are influential but contested, and they have faced replication and interpretation challenges.

Both popular versions have a reason. The puzzle strips a moral choice down to a count of lives, so it reads like folk wisdom rather than an argument from a journal. It also feels like a laboratory test because it has become one: it shaped experimental moral psychology and public discussion of machine ethics, from medical triage to self-driving cars. And the name hides the author. The label that stuck is Thomson's, while the case is Foot's.

The trolley problem has a datable origin. Foot set out the case in 1967, Thomson named and extended it in 1976 and 1985, and Greene's team took it into the brain scanner in 2001.

**Tell someone:** Philippa Foot set out the trolley case in 1967, but the name belongs to Judith Jarvis Thomson, who also added the footbridge variant.

## Was Wegener the first to notice the continents fit together?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Alfred Wegener was not the first. Ortelius and others remarked on the jigsaw fit of the continents centuries before him. Wegener's originality, first put to geologists in a 1912 lecture, lay in combining that fit with fossil, rock and climate evidence.

The popular version has Wegener looking at a map, seeing South America nest into Africa and spotting the jigsaw.

The fit is real. The eastern bulge of South America sits neatly in the western curve of Africa, the match that most strikingly suggests the two were once joined. But the record says plainly that it had been noticed centuries earlier. A related claim that Francis Bacon had the idea of drift is, in the record's words, "a persistent overstatement."

What Wegener added was a case built from several independent lines of evidence. The German meteorologist and geophysicist set it out in lectures in 1912 and in his 1915 book *The Origin of Continents and Oceans*. Fossils of the freshwater reptile Mesosaurus and the seed fern Glossopteris turn up on continents now divided by oceans they could not have crossed. Rock formations and mountain belts line up when the continents are reassembled. Glacial deposits lie in warm regions, and coal, formed in lush climates, lies near the poles. Together they pointed to a single former landmass, which he called Pangaea.

What he lacked was a cause. He pictured continents plowing through the ocean crust, pushed by a pole-fleeing force and by tides. Calculation showed those forces were far too weak, and geophysicists led by figures such as Harold Jeffreys had rejected the idea by around 1930. It was vindicated in the 1950s and 1960s through paleomagnetism and seafloor spreading, and absorbed into plate tectonics.

The myth spread because the fit is the one piece of evidence anyone can see. A story that credits Wegener with noticing it gives him the part that was not his and drops the part that was. His career also has the shape of a legend: a meteorologist challenging geologists, rejected for about half a century and then proved right. A hero in that story tends to be handed every observation in it.

The fit was old news by 1912. Wegener's achievement was the synthesis: coastlines, fossils, rocks and ancient climates read together as one argument that the continents had moved.

**Tell someone:** Ortelius and others remarked on the jigsaw fit of the continents centuries before Wegener; his real feat was adding fossils, rocks and ancient climates.

## Was umami discovered in a seaweed broth?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** In 1908 the Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda isolated glutamate from kombu seaweed broth, coined the word umami and developed MSG as a seasoning. Umami is now widely accepted as the fifth basic taste.

The popular version is skeptical. On this telling, umami is a marketing invention: a word made up to sell a seasoning, not a taste in its own right.

The record says otherwise. Ikeda was the first to identify umami scientifically, and he took its name from a Japanese word meaning, roughly, pleasant savory taste. The taste comes from glutamate, an amino acid, together with certain nucleotides, chiefly inosinate and guanylate. Combined, they show a striking synergy: the savory intensity is far greater than the two alone would give. That is why Japanese dashi, made from kombu, rich in glutamate, and bonito flakes, rich in inosinate, tastes so deep.

Umami is not only MSG. Glutamate occurs naturally in aged cheeses such as Parmesan, in tomatoes, mushrooms and broths, and in soy sauce and miso. The tongue detects it with dedicated taste receptors, including receptors built from taste receptor type 1 proteins and related glutamate receptors. That detection of glutamate as a quality separate from sweet, sour, salty and bitter is what makes umami a basic taste.

The case was not settled quickly. Recognition took most of a century after Ikeda's 1908 work, and the exact identity and workings of the umami receptors are still an area of ongoing research.

The skeptical version spread for understandable reasons. The same chemist named the taste and developed the seasoning that delivers it, which looks like a sales pitch. The long wait for acceptance gave doubt time to settle in. And past fears about MSG, which the record says are unfounded, cast suspicion on anything tied to it.

The order of events runs the other way from the cynical story. Ikeda found glutamate in kombu broth first and named the taste it produced. The seasoning came after, and the receptors found since show that umami is a taste the tongue registers on its own.

**Tell someone:** Umami was found in kombu seaweed broth: Kikunae Ikeda isolated glutamate there in 1908 and named the taste before developing MSG.

## Did Mendeleev invent the periodic table alone?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Dmitri Mendeleev published his periodic table in 1869, but he did not conceive it alone. John Newlands had proposed a related law of octaves in 1865, and Julius Lothar Meyer worked out a similar arrangement independently at about the same time.

The popular version gives the table to one man in one year: Mendeleev, 1869.

The record shares the credit out. Mendeleev ordered the roughly sixty known elements by increasing atomic weight and started new rows so that elements with similar chemistry fell into the same columns. That revealed the periodic law: properties repeat at regular intervals when the elements are listed by weight. But Newlands had already proposed a related law, and Lothar Meyer had reached a similar table on his own.

Mendeleev is credited chiefly for three things, according to the record. He published a full system. He pressed the periodic law harder than anyone. And he made bold predictions that came true. Where an element placed strictly by weight would have landed in the wrong column, he left a gap and predicted what would fill it. His eka-aluminium, eka-boron and eka-silicon were later found as gallium, scandium and germanium. Gallium, found in 1875, matched his forecast closely. He also adjusted some accepted atomic weights so that elements fell into their chemically proper places.

His table was not the last word either. Ordering by weight produced anomalies, such as tellurium and iodine, where the chemically correct order reverses the order of weights. Henry Moseley resolved them in 1913 by showing that elements should be ordered by atomic number. Mendeleev also did not know why the properties repeat; electron shells were understood later.

The single-inventor story spread because the predictions make a better tale than the priority record. A gap in a table, filled years later by a new element that matches the forecast, is a moment people remember. A law of octaves and a parallel table drawn up elsewhere are background. Naming an achievement after one person then makes it sound as though one person did it.

The periodic system had several authors in the 1860s. Mendeleev earned the name by publishing the fullest version and predicting elements before they were found. The modern order, by atomic number, came from Moseley in 1913.

**Tell someone:** Mendeleev had company: Newlands proposed a related law of octaves in 1865, and Lothar Meyer reached a similar table independently at about the same time.

## Were Mendel's pea results too good to be true?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** In 1936 Fisher began a statistical debate over whether Gregor Mendel's reported ratios fit too closely to be chance. The record calls the question unresolved, not a proven charge of fabrication.

The popular version is short: Mendel fudged his data.

The record starts with the experiments. Mendel bred pea plants between 1856 and 1863 and published the results as "Experiments on Plant Hybridization" in 1866. He found that heredity is carried by discrete factors, now called genes, rather than by a blending of parental traits. Crossing plants that differ in one trait gives a uniform first generation and a ratio of three dominant to one recessive in the next. Following two unlinked traits gives nine to three to three to one. The work was largely ignored until about 1900, when Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak rediscovered it.

Fisher's question was about the numbers, not the rules. It asked whether Mendel's reported counts sat closer to the ideal ratios than chance would usually allow. The record says the debate has run since 1936 and remains open. It does not record a finding that Mendel invented results.

The laws themselves have held up as the foundation of classical genetics, within known limits. Independent assortment fails for linked genes. Incomplete dominance, codominance and epistasis change the ratios while the alleles still separate normally. Mitochondrial inheritance and genomic imprinting break one rule or another.

The fraud version spread because a suspicion is easier to repeat than a statistical argument. "Too good to be true" fits in one sentence; an analysis of how far reported ratios should scatter does not. The target makes it memorable too: an accusation against the founder of genetics is a better story than a quarrel about variance. And a debate that stays open for decades is easily rounded off to a verdict, in either direction.

The record supports the open question and nothing stronger. Since Fisher in 1936, statisticians have argued over whether Mendel's ratios fit too well. That argument is unresolved, and it is not a proven charge that he fabricated his data.

**Tell someone:** Since Fisher in 1936, statisticians have argued over whether Mendel's pea ratios fit too well, and no one has settled it either way.

## Did Gauss guess the prime number theorem as a teenager?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The record reports that Gauss, as a teenager in the 1790s, conjectured the prime number theorem from tables of primes. It was proved only in 1896, about a century later.

The popular version remembers the theorem as a nineteenth-century proof, the work of the mathematicians who finally settled it.

The theorem says the number of primes up to a given size is roughly that size divided by its natural logarithm. Primes thin out as numbers grow, but smoothly. Near a million, whose natural logarithm is about fourteen, roughly one number in fourteen is prime. Near a billion, whose logarithm is about twenty-one, roughly one in twenty-one is.

According to the record, Gauss reached this pattern by looking at tables of primes, as a teenager, in the 1790s. Legendre reached it around the same time, in about 1797 or 1798. Neither could prove it. The proof came in 1896, when Jacques Hadamard and Charles-Jean de la Vallée Poussin each settled it, independently, using complex analysis of the Riemann zeta function. Erdős and Selberg found an elementary proof in 1948 and 1949.

Part of the problem is still open. Beyond small numbers, the simple estimate undercounts the primes, and a refined one, the logarithmic integral, tracks the true count far more closely. How small the error can be is tied to the Riemann hypothesis, which is unproven.

The guess is less famous than the proof for a plain reason. A proof is an event with a date and authors. A conjecture read off a table is a private observation, and in this case it had two owners, since Legendre made it too. The teenage detail also sounds like the kind of flourish that gathers around famous mathematicians, so it is easy to dismiss as legend.

This one is in the record. Gauss guessed the law from prime tables in the 1790s, while still a teenager, and it took a century of mathematics and the zeta function to prove that he was right.

**Tell someone:** Gauss reportedly guessed the prime number theorem from prime tables as a teenager in the 1790s, about a century before its 1896 proof.

## Did Hooke publish his law as an anagram?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Robert Hooke published his law of springs in 1676 as the anagram "ceiiinosssttuu." In 1678 he revealed the answer: "ut tensio, sic vis," as the extension, so the force.

The popular version assumes nothing of the sort. A law taught as a single line of algebra is presumed to have been published plainly, like any other result.

The record gives the sequence. Hooke, an English natural philosopher, said he had known the law since 1660. He published it sixteen years later in scrambled form, and two years after that he gave the solution. The fourteen letters of the anagram are the letters of the Latin phrase, sorted into alphabetical order, with u standing for v as printers then used it.

The record does not say why Hooke chose to hide the law. Any reason offered here would be invented, so none is.

What he hid was simple. Pull a spring twice as hard and it stretches twice as far. Force and deformation rise together in fixed proportion, a straight line whose slope is the spring's stiffness. That is what lets a spring balance read a weight off a length. In its general form, stress is proportional to strain, with an elastic modulus such as Young's modulus as the constant, and it is the starting point for the theory of how solids deform.

The law has firm edges. It holds only within the elastic limit, for small deformations. Beyond that, materials yield, deform permanently and then break. Some materials are not linear even at small stretches: rubber is only roughly linear for small pulls and responds nonlinearly further out. The record calls the law a first-order approximation with a well-defined domain, not a law derived from first principles.

The assumption of plain publication is natural. Textbooks present results stripped of how they first appeared, so a tidy equation looks as though it arrived tidy. An anagram is also the kind of detail that sounds like a later joke rather than a printed fact.

It was printed. Hooke's law first appeared in 1676 as fourteen scrambled letters, and the Latin sentence they spell, "ut tensio, sic vis," followed in 1678.

**Tell someone:** Hooke published his spring law in 1676 as the anagram "ceiiinosssttuu," and only revealed in 1678 that it spelled "ut tensio, sic vis."

*Sources:* Hooke, *Lectures de Potentia Restitutiva, or of Spring* (1678). "Hooke's law," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/hookes-law/

## Was the 1919 eclipse a clean proof of Einstein?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The 1919 eclipse measurements matched Einstein's prediction, and modern reanalyses largely uphold the result. But the proof was not clean: blurred plates from one telescope were discarded, and historians have debated whether that was justified.

The popular version is a single triumphant photograph. Eddington's plates proved relativity beyond doubt.

The record is more careful. On 29 May 1919 two expeditions, organized by Frank Watson Dyson and Arthur Eddington, observed a total solar eclipse: one at Sobral in Brazil and one, with Eddington, on the island of Príncipe off West Africa. They photographed stars of the Hyades cluster near the darkened Sun and compared their positions with photographs of the same stars taken months earlier at night. Newtonian gravity predicted that starlight grazing the Sun would bend by about 0.87 arcseconds. General relativity predicted about twice that, 1.75 arcseconds. The measurements came out near Einstein's value. Announced in November 1919, the result made Einstein world-famous almost overnight.

The trouble lies in what was left out. The main telescope at Sobral produced blurred, defocused images, apparently because the Sun's heat distorted the instrument. That dataset gave a deflection closer to the Newtonian value, and it was dropped from the final conclusion. The shifts that counted came chiefly from a backup telescope at Sobral and from the Príncipe plates. Critics from the 1980s onward argued that Eddington may have selectively excluded data that favored Einstein, and that the error bars were large relative to the effect.

Modern reanalyses of the surviving plates have generally vindicated the original conclusion. They found a real instrumental problem that justified excluding the blurred plates. The conclusion has not been overturned.

The clean version spread because the announcement was a public event built around a hero. A newspaper can report that a famous theory passed a famous test. It cannot easily report error bars or a discarded set of plates. The opposite story, that Eddington simply picked the answer he wanted, spread for a similar reason: a scandal is also a good shape.

The safe statement sits between them. The 1919 eclipse gave the first major observational confirmation of general relativity, and its result has survived later checking. How its data were handled was contested, and the debate is part of the record.

**Tell someone:** The 1919 eclipse results matched Einstein and have largely survived reanalysis, though blurred plates from one Sobral telescope were set aside, a choice historians debated.
