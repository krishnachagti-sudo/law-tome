## Did a cobra bounty in Delhi breed more cobras?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** No record shows that anyone in Delhi bred cobras for a bounty. The idea the story named rests on firmer evidence, which is the next story in this round.

The popular version is short. British officials in colonial Delhi paid a reward for dead cobras, and local people started breeding cobras to collect it. The German economist Horst Siebert liked the story enough to name an idea after it. His 2001 book *Der Kobra-Effekt* describes how a reward offered to solve a problem can end up paying people to manufacture it.

The trail behind the story is thin. A 2025 review by the Friends of Snakes Society traced it to an 1873 newspaper report on a bounty in the Madras Presidency, not Delhi. That report said only that it "was alleged" that some natives bred cobras for the reward. The review found no contemporary record of a breeding operation or of anyone prosecuted for one.

The story spread because it is the ideal shape for a lesson. It has a plan, a twist and a moral that fits in one sentence. It is also set far enough away, and long enough ago, that few retellers went looking for the source. Each version cited the one before, and repetition did the work that evidence should have done.

The idea itself holds up, and its best evidence is a bounty on rats paid in French colonial Hanoi, which is the subject of the next question in this round. Later cases follow the same pattern. Mexico City's 1989 driving ban took each car off the road one weekday a week by license plate. Households bought a second car, often older and dirtier, with a different plate; the economist Lucas Davis found no gain in air quality.

So the Cobra Effect is real and the cobras are folklore. A reward tied to something people can produce will eventually be met by people producing it.

**Tell someone:** The Delhi cobra story traces to an 1873 Madras report saying only that breeding "was alleged"; an economist named an effect after it.

## Did a French bounty on rat tails in Hanoi backfire?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** In 1902 the French colonial administration in Hanoi paid a bounty for each rat killed, with a severed tail as proof. Rat-catchers began cutting the tails off live rats and letting them go to breed. The case is documented in a 2003 paper by M. G. Vann in *French Colonial History*.

The popular version is the same story, which is the surprise. Paying for tails produced rats without tails, and rats without tails went on producing rats. It sounds too neat to be true, and it shares a shape with the Delhi cobra story, which the record treats as folklore.

The evidence backs the rat version. Vann's paper, "Of Rats, Rice, and Race: The Great Hanoi Rat Massacre, an Episode in French Colonial History," appeared in *French Colonial History* in 2003. The Law Tome record calls the Hanoi bounty the documented case behind the cobra effect. Rat-catchers cut the tails off live rats and released them, so the animals could breed and supply more tails to sell.

The mechanism is plain once it is named. A bounty pays for a measurable result on the assumption that the result can only come from doing the wanted thing. Here the result was a tail, and a tail did not require a dead rat. When the thing being counted can be produced instead of removed, the reward funds its production.

The same setup has recurred. Wells Fargo set aggressive cross-selling targets, and employees opened millions of accounts that customers never authorized; the bank paid US$185 million in penalties in 2016 and fired thousands of staff. DigitalOcean's 2020 Hacktoberfest rewarded a set number of GitHub pull requests, and after a viral how-to, maintainers were buried in spam submissions.

The limits matter. The effect is a warning about one design flaw, not a claim that incentives backfire in general; most work as intended. It needs a proxy that the people collecting the reward can manufacture, and a sponsor who does not control its supply. Schemes that pay for verified outcomes and check where the proof came from avoid it.

The rat story is less famous than the cobra because the cobra got the name. Siebert's 2001 book took its title from the snake story, so the better-sourced case became the footnote. Its resemblance to a famous piece of folklore then made it look like folklore too.

It is not. Hanoi's 1902 rat bounty is the documented example of a reward that bred the problem it paid to end.

**Tell someone:** Hanoi's 1902 rat bounty took tails as proof, so rat-catchers cut the tails off live rats and released them to breed.

## Did more efficient steam engines make Britain burn more coal?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** In *The Coal Question* (1865), the economist William Stanley Jevons observed that England's coal use soared after more efficient steam engines expanded coal's industrial uses.

The popular version is an assumption rather than a story. A more efficient engine burns less coal for each job, so a country with better engines should burn less coal.

Jevons said the opposite, and bluntly: "It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth." His case was James Watt's more efficient steam engines. They lowered the cost of coal-powered work, which pushed coal into far more industries and raised total British coal consumption in the nineteenth century.

The mechanism is now called the rebound effect. An efficiency gain lowers the price of each unit of service, so people use more of it. If demand responds strongly enough, the extra use outweighs the saving per unit and total consumption rises. A rebound above 100 percent is called backfire, and that is the full Jevons paradox. At the scale of a whole economy, the Khazzoom–Brookes postulate, named by Harry Saunders in 1992, adds that efficiency raises incomes and growth, which lifts demand further.

The record keeps the claim narrow. In many cases the rebound is only partial, so efficiency still saves something. Fuel-efficient cars lower the cost per mile and encourage more driving, but usually not enough to erase the gain. Lighting may be a stronger case: a Sandia study led by Jeff Tsao projected that cheaper solid-state lighting could raise lighting consumption roughly tenfold by 2030. Economists still debate how large and how common rebound is, and evidence for economy-wide backfire is genuine but contested.

The efficiency assumption persists because it is true where people look. Per unit, a better engine does burn less. The rebound happens across a whole economy, out of sight of any single machine, as new uses appear that the old cost ruled out. Jevons named the mistake in his own phrase: a confusion of ideas.

For coal in nineteenth-century Britain, the record supports Jevons. Better engines made coal cheaper to use, and the country burned more of it.

**Tell someone:** More efficient steam engines made coal cheaper to use, so Britain found new uses and burned more of it, as Jevons observed in 1865.

## Did Irish famine potatoes sell more as they got dearer?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The first real-world evidence of Giffen behavior comes from rice in Hunan, in a 2008 study by Jensen and Miller, not from Ireland. The famine potato, long the textbook example, has been discredited: Rosen's 1999 study of Irish price and quantity data concluded that 1840s potatoes were not Giffen goods.

The popular version is the textbook one: during the famine, potatoes were the classic case of a good bought more as its price rose.

A Giffen good breaks the basic law of demand. Its consumption rises when its price rises, giving it an upward-sloping demand curve over some range. The idea is named after the Scottish economist Sir Robert Giffen, to whom Alfred Marshall attributed it in his *Principles of Economics*. Marshall's own example, as the record gives it, was bread: a rise in its price could drain poor families' budgets so far that they cut back on meat and bought still more bread.

Three conditions must hold at once. The good must be inferior, bought less as people grow richer. It must take a large share of a poor household's budget. And it must lack close substitutes. When its price rises, the household is poorer in real terms, cannot afford the costlier foods it would otherwise buy, and fills the gap with more of the staple.

For a long time no real-world case was firmly documented, and some economists doubted any existed. Jensen and Miller's field study, published in the *American Economic Review* in 2008, found strong evidence of Giffen behavior for rice among poor households in Hunan, and weaker evidence for wheat in Gansu. They present it as the first real-world evidence of such behavior. The same paper says the potato example "has also been discredited (Rosen, 1999)," adds that aggregate potato consumption could hardly have risen when blight destroyed the crop, and traces the potato version to the 1964 edition of Samuelson's textbook.

The potato story spread because it fits the conditions on paper. A famine supplies a staple, poor households and few alternatives, all in a setting most readers already know. Textbooks needed an example, and for decades there was no documented one to give, so a plausible historical case filled the space and was repeated from book to book.

The potato case does not survive the data. Giffen goods are real but rare, and their clearest documented case is rice in Hunan, reported by Jensen and Miller in 2008.

**Tell someone:** The textbook Giffen good, Irish famine potatoes, has been discredited; the first real-world evidence came from rice among poor households in Hunan.

## Did broken-windows policing cause New York's 1990s crime drop?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The claim that broken-windows policing caused New York's crime decline is disputed and confounded. Crime fell across most American cities in the 1990s, including cities that never adopted the approach, and a 2006 analysis by Harcourt and Ludwig found little causal support for the crime-reduction claim.

The popular version credits the windows. New York fixed the small things, arrested fare-dodgers and cleaned off graffiti, and the city became safe.

The theory came first. James Q. Wilson and George Kelling set it out in "Broken Windows," in *The Atlantic Monthly* in 1982. Visible disorder, they argued, signals that a place is unwatched, which invites more disorder and eventually serious crime. They drew on Philip Zimbardo's abandoned-car experiments. A car left without plates was stripped within a day in the Bronx but sat untouched for a week in Palo Alto, until Zimbardo smashed it himself and passersby joined in.

In 1990s New York, order-maintenance policing under Bratton and Giuliani targeted fare evasion and graffiti. Proponents credited it with the city's crime decline.

The record treats that credit as unproven. Crime fell nationally in the 1990s, amid the end of the crack epidemic, economic growth, more police and other changes, including in cities that never used the policy. Harcourt and Ludwig's analysis of a five-city housing experiment found that families moved to neighborhoods with less disorder did not commit fewer crimes.

One narrow part of the theory has support. In field experiments published in *Science* in 2008, Keizer, Lindenberg and Steg found that visible graffiti and litter increased other rule-breaking, including littering and petty theft. That supports disorder spreading disorder. It does not show escalation to serious crime, or that a policing program prevents it. A rival account from Sampson and Raudenbush holds that disorder and crime may both stem from weak neighborhood cohesion. Kelling's own version also stressed officer discretion, unlike the harder zero-tolerance policing often applied in its name.

The simple version spread because the timing lines up. One city, one visible policy and one falling crime rate sit side by side, and coincidence in time is easily read as cause. A national decline is hard to see from inside a single city, and the image of one broken window is easy to remember.

The record supports a narrower claim. Minor disorder breeds more minor disorder. Whether broken-windows policing caused New York's 1990s crime drop is disputed, because crime fell in places that never tried it.

**Tell someone:** Crime fell across most American cities in the 1990s, including ones that never tried broken-windows policing, so its credit for New York's drop remains disputed.

## Did Nixon really have a "madman theory"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Nixon did put the strategy into practice: the National Security Archive documents the secret JCS Readiness Test of October 1969 as his test of the madman approach. The caveat is the label. The name "madman theory" and its founding quote rest on one source, H. R. Haldeman's 1978 memoir *The Ends of Power*.

The popular version has Nixon telling aides he wanted the enemy to think he was crazy enough to use the bomb.

The record traces that to Haldeman, Nixon's chief of staff. By Haldeman's account, Nixon said during a walk on a beach that he wanted the North Vietnamese to believe he might do anything to end the war. They should think he was obsessed and had his hand on the nuclear button, so that they would beg for peace. This is a reported conversation, known from one participant's memoir, and Nixon later told the historian Joan Hoff that he did not remember using the term.

The strategy itself has documentary support beyond the memoir. Burr and Kimball's National Security Archive briefing book (2002) sets out the evidence for the October 1969 readiness test. It cites Haldeman's diary for 17 October 1969 on Nixon's "signal-type activity going around the world to try to jar Soviets + NVN." The same year Nixon and Kissinger drew up an escalation plan called Duck Hook, later aborted. These moves are widely judged to have failed to coerce Hanoi or Moscow.

The idea itself is much older than Nixon. Machiavelli praised Lucius Junius Brutus for feigning folly to survive and overthrow the Tarquins, calling it wise to simulate madness at the right time. Its Cold War theory belongs to Thomas Schelling and Herman Kahn. Schelling showed that a bargainer can gain by worsening his own options, and by a threat that leaves something to chance. The rationality of irrationality, associated with Kahn, holds that a suicidal threat is credible only if the threatener is believed irrational. Schelling did not name or endorse Nixon's version.

The Nixon story spread because it has an insider, a setting and an image. A chief of staff's memoir carries authority, and a president's hand on the nuclear button is hard to forget. It also gave a short name to a strategy that scholars had described in abstract terms. "Madman" never meant mental illness; it meant a performance staged for leverage.

The label "madman theory" rests on Haldeman's 1978 account, and Nixon said he did not remember using it. The strategy does not rest on the label. The logic came from Schelling, Kahn and Machiavelli, and Nixon tested it in October 1969, without success.

**Tell someone:** Nixon tested a madman strategy with a secret military readiness test in October 1969; only the label "madman theory" rests on Haldeman's memoir.

## Did the domino theory get its name at a 1954 press conference?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** President Dwight D. Eisenhower's press conference on Indochina on April 7, 1954, made the falling-dominoes image famous, but it did not clearly give the theory its name. He introduced it as "what you would call the 'falling domino' principle," which suggests the phrase was already current, and the reasoning behind it was older, explicit in the 1947 Truman Doctrine.

The popular version gives the theory a single birthday: Eisenhower coined the name at a press conference in 1954.

The press conference is real and dated. Eisenhower said that if the first domino in a row is knocked over, the last will fall quickly, and used the picture to explain why Indochina mattered to the whole region. His own framing, "what you would call," points to a phrase others were already using, and the columnists Joseph and Stewart Alsop reportedly claimed to have coined "domino theory" themselves. The record says he popularized the image, which is not the same as naming it.

The idea itself came first. The reasoning behind the 1947 Truman Doctrine, that abandoning Greece and Turkey would let communism spread onward, is the same logic without the dominoes. After 1954 the theory became a central justification for deepening American military involvement in Vietnam, and it shaped US strategy across four administrations.

Its prediction was then tested. In 1975 Cambodia and Laos did become communist along with South Vietnam, a limited local toppling. The forecast collapse of Thailand, Indonesia and beyond did not happen, and several of those states grew stronger. Critics fault the theory for treating different nations as interchangeable pieces and for ignoring the nationalism that set communist states against one another, as with China and Vietnam. Singapore's Lee Kuan Yew offered a partial defense: US intervention, however costly, bought time for the non-communist states of the region to stabilize. The record calls the theory largely discredited but still debated.

The single-birthday version spread because a president, a date and a vivid picture make a cleaner origin than a phrase drifting through columns and briefings. A transcript is easy to cite, and the earlier uses it hints at are not. The theory also has two starting points, the 1947 logic and the 1954 image, and the famous one tends to absorb the credit.

The reasoning dates from 1947, and the name was apparently already current when Eisenhower spoke. What he supplied on April 7, 1954, was the image that made the phrase famous.

## Did the Sydney Opera House open a decade late and vastly over budget?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The Sydney Opera House was estimated to open in 1963 at a cost of a few million pounds. A scaled-down version opened in 1973 at A$102 million. The exact figures vary by source.

The popular version holds the Opera House up as the classic runaway project. Famous stories of waste tend to grow in the retelling, so it is fair to suspect this one has too.

It has not grown much. The record calls the building the archetypal runaway public project. Its opening came ten years after the estimate, and the finished building was smaller than planned. The figures vary by source, and by whether they are stated in the money of the day or adjusted for inflation, so they are best kept as ranges: a few million at the start, more than a hundred million at the end.

The Opera House is the best-known case of the planning fallacy, a term coined by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1979. It is the tendency to underestimate how long a task will take, even when similar past tasks have overrun. In a 1994 study by Buehler, Griffin and Ross, students predicted they would finish their honors theses in about 34 days on average, and in about 49 if everything went as badly as possible. They took about 55, and fewer than a third finished within their own estimate.

Large projects show the same pattern in money. In 2002 Flyvbjerg, Holm and Buhl studied 258 transport projects worth some $90 billion. Actual costs averaged about 28 percent above estimate, and roughly nine in ten were underestimated. Flyvbjerg argues that for megaprojects the cause is often not honest optimism but strategic misrepresentation: promoters low-ball costs to win approval. The record treats that as a rival explanation, not a settled one.

The story spread because the building is famous and the gap is easy to state: a decade and a large multiple of the budget. The variation between sources is real, and it invites doubt. But it concerns the precise numbers, not the shape of the story.

The popular version holds. Planned for 1963 at a few million pounds, the Sydney Opera House opened in scaled-down form in 1973 at A$102 million.

## Did Hardin later admit the tragedy of the commons was missing a word?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Garrett Hardin later conceded that his error had been to omit one word: "unmanaged." His tragedy describes an unmanaged, open-access resource, not every shared one.

The popular version treats the tragedy as an iron law: any resource held in common will be ruined unless it is privatized or controlled by the state.

The argument first appeared as "The Tragedy of the Commons" in *Science* in 1968, given as a presidential address to the Pacific Division of the AAAS. Its logic rests on herders sharing a pasture. Adding one more cow gives a herder its full benefit, while the cost of overgrazing is divided among everyone, so each private calculation favors adding more. That works where no one can be kept out and where one person's use leaves less for others.

Hardin himself later conceded that his error was omitting the word "unmanaged." Historical commons were in fact regulated by custom and law to prevent overgrazing. The model fits an open-access resource with no rules, which is a different thing.

Elinor Ostrom's fieldwork then showed what managed commons can do. In *Governing the Commons* (1990), she documented communities that govern shared resources successfully through their own institutions, without privatizing or nationalizing them. She won the 2009 Nobel Prize. The depletion mechanism is real where a resource is truly open: the record points to overfished cod on the Grand Banks and to the atmosphere as a sink for greenhouse gases. What the record rejects is the claim that ruin is inevitable.

The iron-law reading spread because the essay was short, prominent and memorable, and the qualifier that limits it is a single word, easy to drop. The concession came thirty years later, in a 1998 essay, so it never traveled with the original. The name itself also invites the misreading. It borrows the word "commons" from exactly the kind of regulated pasture to which the model does not apply.

Hardin's own correction is the safe statement. The tragedy belongs to the unmanaged commons, and Ostrom showed that managed ones need not end in ruin.

**Tell someone:** Garrett Hardin later conceded his famous essay left out one word, "unmanaged," and Elinor Ostrom documented communities that manage shared resources successfully.

## Did Garrett Hardin invent the overgrazed-pasture parable?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The overgrazed-pasture parable came from William Forster Lloyd in 1833. Garrett Hardin named the idea and popularized it in 1968, 135 years later.

The popular version gives the herders to Hardin. His 1968 essay in *Science*, on this telling, introduced the picture of herders overgrazing a common pasture.

The record splits the credit. Hardin's paper, "The Tragedy of the Commons," appeared in *Science* 162 in 1968 and was given as a presidential address to the Pacific Division of the AAAS. It gave the idea its name and its wide audience. But the parable itself came earlier, from Lloyd in 1833. Lloyd's version appeared in *Two Lectures on the Checks to Population*, and Hardin's essay credits it by name.

The parable is simple, which helps explain why it outlived its author. A pasture is open to every herder. Each extra cow brings its owner the full benefit, while the cost of the grass it eats is shared among all. So every herder keeps adding cows, and the pasture is ruined. It needs two conditions: no one can be kept out, and one animal's grazing leaves less for the rest.

The idea has since been tested against real commons. Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) challenged the claim that such ruin is inevitable, by documenting communities that manage shared resources well. The record presents Hardin's 1968 essay as the version that named and spread the parable, and Lloyd's 1833 version as its source.

The misattribution spread because names stick to whoever makes an idea famous. Hardin supplied the phrase people repeat, "the tragedy of the commons," in a prominent journal, and the frame went on to shape debates over pollution, conservation and property rights. A memorable title tends to absorb the history behind it. It is the same drift met in the round on names that mislead: the person remembered is the one who popularized the idea, not the one who first told it.

The parable is older than its name. Lloyd described the overgrazed pasture in 1833, and Hardin gave it the title it carries in 1968.

**Tell someone:** The overgrazed-pasture parable came from William Forster Lloyd in 1833; Garrett Hardin gave it its famous name 135 years later.
