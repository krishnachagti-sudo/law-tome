# Round 1: Famous scenes

The stories everyone has heard. A warm-up.

These are the scenes told in classrooms and at dinner tables: a bath, an apple, a tower, an equation, a wartime speech. Each has a version almost everyone knows and a record that can be checked. Some of the famous details come from the people involved. Others were added long afterward by people who were not there. Guess first, then turn the page.

## Did Archimedes shout "Eureka!" in the bath?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Archimedes stated the law of buoyancy around 250 BC, in his treatise *On Floating Bodies*. The bath, the crown and the cry of "Eureka!" are almost certainly a later embellishment.

The popular version is a single moment. Archimedes stepped into his bath, saw the water rise, and realized how to settle a problem about a crown. He ran out shouting "Eureka!"

The record separates the principle from the anecdote. The principle is in Archimedes' own work. *On Floating Bodies* states that a body in a fluid is buoyed up by the weight of the fluid it displaces, and its Proposition 5 gives the corollary for floating bodies: a floating object settles until it displaces exactly its own weight. That is why a steel hull floats and why a balloon rises when it displaces more than its own weight of air. Within its assumptions the principle is exact.

The anecdote has a different author. It was first told by Vitruvius, writing some two centuries after Archimedes. The method Vitruvius describes, judging the crown by the water it pushes aside, is crude, and too imprecise to have worked. Archimedes more likely used hydrostatic weighing.

The story spread because it gives an abstract law a face and a date. A rule about fluid pressure is hard to picture; a man running from his bath is not. It also fits a popular idea of discovery as one flash of insight rather than patient measurement. Once Vitruvius had written it down, each retelling had a source to point to, and nobody needed to ask how a writer two centuries later could know what was said in a bathroom.

The physics stands on its own. Archimedes' principle is one of the oldest quantitative results in physics, and the earliest source for it is his own treatise of around 250 BC. The anecdote is not evidence for it. The earliest source for the bath is Vitruvius.

**Tell someone:** Archimedes did state the law of buoyancy, but the bath and the "Eureka!" almost certainly come from Vitruvius, writing some two centuries later.

*Sources:* Vitruvius, *De architectura*, book IX, preface. "Fact or Fiction?: Archimedes Coined the Term 'Eureka!' in the Bath," *Scientific American*. Rorres, "The Golden Crown," NYU. "Archimedes' principle," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/archimedes-principle/

## Did an apple fall on Newton's head?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** There is an apple in Newton's own account of his thinking about gravity. It does not hit him on the head. That detail is a later embellishment.

The popular version is a piece of slapstick. An apple dropped onto Newton's head, and the idea of gravity arrived with the bump.

The record is plainer. Newton published his law of universal gravitation in *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica* in 1687, a book whose printing Edmond Halley paid for. The law says that any two particles of matter attract each other with a force that grows with the product of their masses and falls with the square of the distance between them. Robert Hooke had suggested an inverse-square attraction in letters, but he could not derive the orbits of the planets from it. The mathematical derivation was Newton's own.

The apple story itself, according to *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, traces back to Newton's own later recollection. That recollection includes an apple. It does not include an apple striking him.

The bump spread because it improves the story as a story. A falling apple seen from a distance is a thought. An apple landing on a scientist's head is an event, with a victim and a punchline, and it makes discovery look like something that happens to a person by accident. Each retelling had a reason to keep the joke and none to check it.

The apple is also a fair symbol of what the law did. Newton's rule covers an apple falling to the ground and the Moon orbiting the Earth with the same mathematics, which is why the law is often called the first great unification in physics. The symbol worked without any help from a bruise.

So the fruit belongs to Newton and the bump belongs to his retellers. The law is in the *Principia* of 1687. The apple comes from Newton's own later account, and in that account it stays off his head.

**Tell someone:** According to *Britannica*, Newton's own recollection includes an apple but no bump; the hit on the head was a later embellishment.

*Sources:* Stukeley, *Memoirs of Sir Isaac Newton's Life* (1752). "Newton's law of gravitation," *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. "Newton's law of universal gravitation," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/newtons-law-of-universal-gravitation/

## Did Newton himself tell the apple story?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The apple story is not a fable invented by Newton's admirers. According to *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, it traces back to Newton's own later recollection. Only the apple hitting his head was added afterward.

The popular version here is a skeptic's one. The apple, it says, is pure myth: a tidy tale invented by later admirers to dress his work in a moment of genius.

The record does not support the skeptic. The account of an apple comes from Newton himself, in his own later recollection. What the record marks as embellishment is narrower: the detail of the apple striking him. That detail is not in Newton's account.

The law the apple is attached to is fully documented. Newton published it in 1687, in *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica*. It states that every mass attracts every other mass, with a force that scales with the product of the masses and falls with the square of the distance between them. Newton showed that this rule, combined with his laws of motion, produces Kepler's three laws of planetary motion. Later work kept testing it. Galle found Neptune in 1846 within about a degree of where Le Verrier's Newtonian prediction placed it.

The myth of a wholly invented apple spread for a reason that runs opposite to most myths in this book. The head-bump is so plainly comic that it invites suspicion, and people who rightly doubted the bump threw out the apple with it. A debunking can overshoot. Once "the apple story is a myth" became a known correction, it traveled as easily as the original, and with as little checking.

The safe statement keeps both halves. Newton told of an apple; nobody needed to invent it for him. The bump on the head is the later addition. The law itself rests on the earliest dated source in the record, the *Principia* of 1687, and the apple rests on Newton's own word.

**Tell someone:** Newton himself told of the apple, according to *Britannica*; only the bump on the head was added later.

*Sources:* Stukeley, *Memoirs of Sir Isaac Newton's Life* (1752). "Newton's law of gravitation," *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. "Newton's law of universal gravitation," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/newtons-law-of-universal-gravitation/

## Did Galileo drop balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The tower drop is likely a legend. It is not in Galileo's own writings. His documented work on falling bodies used balls rolling down ramps, with notes from around 1603 to 1604 and results published in 1638.

The popular version is a public demonstration. Galileo climbed the Leaning Tower of Pisa, let go of a heavy weight and a light one together, and proved that both fall at the same rate.

The record traces the tower story to a biography by Galileo's student Viviani, written decades later. Because it is absent from Galileo's own writings and comes from that late source, it is widely regarded as legendary.

What Galileo did document was cleverer. The clocks of his day could not time a free fall, so he slowed the fall down. He rolled a polished ball down a gently sloping grooved ramp and timed each descent with a water clock, marking equal intervals by equal weights of water. In successive equal times the ball covered distances in the ratio one, three, five, seven. The total distance grew as the square of the time, which is the mark of constant acceleration. The acceleration was the same whatever the ball's weight. Without air resistance, all bodies fall at the same rate. These results overturned the old Aristotelian teaching that heavier objects fall proportionally faster.

The tower story spread because it does in one afternoon what the real work did over years. A drop from a famous building is visible, dramatic and easy to retell. Ramps, water clocks and ratios of odd numbers are not. The legend also has the right moral, an old authority refuted in a single visible test, which made it useful to anyone telling the story of modern science.

The documented record is the ramp. Galileo's working notes date to around 1603 to 1604, and he published the results in *Two New Sciences* in 1638. That book, not the tower, is the earliest dated source for his law of falling bodies.

**Tell someone:** The tower drop is likely a legend; Galileo's documented work on falling bodies rolled balls down ramps and timed them with a water clock.

*Sources:* "Galileo's Leaning Tower of Pisa experiment," Wikipedia. "Two New Sciences," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/galileos-inclined-plane/

## Did Pythagoras discover the Pythagorean theorem?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The relation that carries Pythagoras's name was known and used long before him. A Babylonian tablet from around 1800 BC lists its number triples, and Pythagoras lived from about 570 to 495 BC.

The popular version is simple. Pythagoras of Samos discovered that in a right triangle the square on the longest side equals the sum of the squares on the other two, and the theorem is named after him for that reason.

The record puts the relation much earlier and in several places. Babylonian tablets such as Plimpton 322, from around 1800 BC, record Pythagorean triples. The relation also appears in the Indian *Sulba Sutras* and the Chinese *Zhoubi Suanjing*. Their dates are less certain: the *Sulba Sutras* are placed between the 8th and 5th centuries BC, and the surviving *Zhoubi Suanjing* dates from around the 1st century BC, though its contents are thought to be older. The simplest triple, three, four and five, has been used since antiquity to lay out right angles, and builders still check a corner that way.

As for Pythagoras himself, the record is cautious. *Encyclopaedia Britannica* calls the attribution to him uncertain, and the record adds that it is doubtful he personally proved the theorem.

The myth spread because names on theorems work like labels, and labels get read as credits. Once a result is called "Pythagorean," the natural assumption is that Pythagoras found it. A name is repeated every time the theorem is taught; the older tablets and texts are not. Repetition turned a label into a history.

The theorem itself is not in doubt. Within flat, Euclidean geometry it holds without exception. Its limits are of scope: it covers right triangles only, and for any other triangle the law of cosines applies, with Pythagoras's relation as its special case. What is in doubt is the credit. The earliest dated source in the record for the relation is a Babylonian tablet of around 1800 BC, more than a thousand years before Pythagoras was born.

**Tell someone:** A Babylonian tablet from around 1800 BC already lists Pythagorean triples, more than a thousand years before Pythagoras was born.

*Sources:* "Pythagorean theorem," *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. "Pythagorean theorem," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-pythagorean-theorem/

## Did Einstein write E = mc² in his 1905 paper?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Einstein's 1905 paper contains the idea but not the famous formula. It states in a single sentence that a body which gives off energy as radiation loses mass in proportion.

The popular version has Einstein setting down E = mc² in one short line in 1905, the equation appearing fully formed in the paper that announced it.

The paper exists and can be read in translation. It is a short follow-up to his special relativity paper of the same year, titled "Does the Inertia of a Body Depend Upon Its Energy Content?" Einstein's derivation ends in a sentence about a body radiating energy and losing mass: if it gives off energy L, its mass diminishes by L/V², with V for the speed of light. That is not the compact formula now known everywhere.

The record is clear that the idea was his in a way that mattered. Others before him, Poincaré and Hasenöhrl, had linked mass and energy only for electromagnetic cases. Einstein was the first to state the relation as a general principle that applies to all energy. A compressed spring, a heated object and a chemical reaction all change mass by their energy divided by the speed of light squared. The change is far too small to weigh. Nuclear reactions are where it becomes large enough to notice.

The myth spread because the formula became an icon, and icons get read back into their origins. Readers who meet E = mc² everywhere assume it must appear in the founding document, much as people assume a famous quotation has a famous speaker. The popular tie to atomic bombs is a second misreading the record corrects: the equivalence is not specifically about nuclear physics at all.

The statement survives the check; the typography does not. The earliest dated source is Einstein's 1905 paper, and in it the mass-energy relation is set out in a sentence, not as E = mc². The record does not say when the compact form first appeared in print.

**Tell someone:** Look for E = mc² in Einstein's 1905 paper and it isn't there: he set out the mass-energy relation in a sentence.

*Sources:* Einstein, "Does the Inertia of a Body Depend Upon Its Energy Content?" (1905), English translation. "Mass-energy equivalence," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/mass-energy-equivalence/

## Did Einstein win his Nobel Prize for something other than relativity?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Einstein's Nobel Prize, the physics prize designated for 1921, was for his work on the photoelectric effect. NobelPrize.org gives the citation as "for his services to Theoretical Physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect." Relativity is not what the prize named.

The popular version assumes the obvious. Einstein is famous for relativity, so the Nobel committee must have given him the prize for relativity.

The record tells a longer story about light. Heinrich Hertz first noticed in 1887 that light striking a material can knock electrons out of its surface. Philipp Lenard studied the effect quantitatively. Its features defeated classical wave theory. Below a threshold frequency no electrons come out, however bright the light. Above it, the energy of each electron depends on the light's frequency, and brightness changes only how many electrons emerge.

In 1905 Einstein explained this in *Annalen der Physik* with the light-quantum hypothesis and an equation relating electron energy to frequency. Robert Millikan confirmed the frequency relation around 1916. By the American Physical Society's account, Millikan spent about a decade testing Einstein's equation, convinced it had to be wrong, and his own measurements confirmed it and pinned down Planck's constant, though he kept doubting the photon behind it.

The myth spread because fame and prizes are assumed to line up. Relativity is the work most people connect with Einstein, and E = mc² is the formula they know. The photoelectric effect is a laboratory result about electrons and metal surfaces. It is easy to assume the most famous work won the most famous prize.

The record adds one caution in the other direction. The effect is popularly overcredited as proof that light comes in quanta. It motivated the idea powerfully, but on its own it is not airtight; the decisive evidence for photons came later, from other experiments.

The fact holds. The earliest source is Einstein's 1905 paper on light quanta, and the prize designated for 1921 named his law of the photoelectric effect, not relativity.

**Tell someone:** Einstein's Nobel was not for relativity: the prize designated for 1921 named his law of the photoelectric effect.

*Sources:* Einstein, *Annalen der Physik* 322(6) (1905). The Nobel Prize in Physics 1921, NobelPrize.org. "Millikan's Measurement of Planck's Constant," APS Physics. "Robert Millikan," APS Historic Sites. "Photoelectric effect," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-photoelectric-effect/

## Did Gordon Moore say chips double every 18 months?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Gordon Moore never gave 18 months. His 1965 article predicted that the number of components on a chip would double roughly every year, and in 1975 he revised that to every two years.

The popular version is that Moore's law says computing power doubles every 18 months.

The record goes back to the article. On 19 April 1965 Moore, then head of research and development at Fairchild Semiconductor and later a co-founder of Intel, published "Cramming more components onto integrated circuits" in *Electronics* magazine. His rate was a doubling roughly every year. In 1975 he slowed it to every two years. The name "Moore's law" is usually credited to the Caltech professor Carver Mead.

The 18 months came from someone else. Intel's David House combined the two-year doubling in transistors with rising performance and projected that chip performance would double every 18 months. Moore said he never used the figure.

The myth spread because House's figure answered the question most people were asking. Moore's law is about transistor count and cost, an economic trend with no physical principle behind it. A claim about speed was easier to use and repeat than a claim about component density. The record notes that the popular reading of ever-doubling "speed" mixes Moore's trend up with a separate one, Dennard scaling, under which shrinking transistors kept power density roughly constant. Dennard scaling broke down around 2005 to 2006, ending the free gains in clock speed.

The two-year version held for more than forty years, from a few thousand transistors per chip in the early 1970s to billions. Its pace has slowed since about 2010.

So the law is Moore's and the 18 months is House's. The earliest dated source is Moore's own article of 19 April 1965, which said about once a year. His 1975 revision said two years.

**Tell someone:** Gordon Moore predicted a doubling roughly every year in 1965 and every two years in 1975; the 18 months was David House's.

*Sources:* Moore, "Cramming more components onto integrated circuits," *Electronics* 38(8) (19 April 1965). "Moore's law," Wikipedia. Computer History Museum, "1965: 'Moore's Law' Predicts the Future of Integrated Circuits." Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/moores-law/

## Did Churchill promise "blood, sweat and tears"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** In May 1940 Churchill offered "blood, toil, tears and sweat." That is four items, not three, and "toil" is the word that dropped out.

The popular version is the three-word phrase almost everyone quotes: Churchill, in May 1940, offering the nation blood, sweat and tears.

The record states his actual words and their date, May 1940, and files the shorter version as a misquotation. It lists the misquote alongside another piece of shaky lore attached to the same idea: the Latin tag *omne trium perfectum*, "everything that comes in threes is perfect." That motto is popularly cited as an ancient proverb, but it is not attested in the classical Latin corpus in reliable references, and is better treated as a modern slogan.

Both belong to the rule of three, the principle that a trio of words, phrases or examples is more satisfying and memorable than other numbers. The usual explanation is that three is the smallest number of items that can set up a recognizable pattern. A triad is short, has rhythm, and feels complete. The device runs from classical rhetoric, where three parallel clauses are called a tricolon, to Caesar's *veni, vidi, vici*, the three rights of the US Declaration of Independence and the closing phrase of Lincoln's Gettysburg Address.

That is the most likely reason the misquote spread. Memory and retelling favor the shape that feels finished. Four items are one too many for the pattern the rule describes, so the phrase was trimmed to the form that sounds right. The record warns that a well-made group of three can lend an idea an unearned air of completeness. Here it did more: it rewrote a famous line to fit the pattern.

The words on the record are Churchill's own. In May 1940 he promised "blood, toil, tears and sweat." The three-word version is what the rule of three made of them.

**Tell someone:** Churchill offered "blood, toil, tears and sweat" in May 1940: four items, and "toil" is the word that dropped out.

*Sources:* Hansard, HC Deb 13 May 1940. "Rule of three (writing)," Wikipedia. "Tricolon," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-rule-of-three/

## Did Edward Murphy say "Anything that can go wrong will go wrong"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** A maxim like it came out of an Air Force rocket-sled project around 1949, and Murphy's name stuck to it. Who first said it, and in what words, is not settled. The earliest firm printing under Murphy's name, from 1952, credits an unnamed physicist.

The popular version has an engineer called Murphy, annoyed by a botched job, coining the law on the spot.

The record is more tangled. Around 1949 Edward A. Murphy Jr., an aerospace engineer, worked on project MX981, a series of rocket-sled deceleration tests at what became Edwards Air Force Base. In one run, strain-gauge transducers wired to a harness returned zero readings because they had been installed the wrong way round. A part that could be fitted two ways had been fitted the wrong way. The project's lead, Colonel John Stapp, then popularized the maxim at a press conference. He framed it as a strength: the team's habit of designing against every failure it could imagine.

The wording is where the trail breaks. Murphy's son and his colleagues later recalled materially different phrasings, and the accounts of the 1949 events are anecdotal. The earliest firm appearance in print of Murphy's law by name, as "If anything can go wrong, it will," is in Anne Roe's 1952 book *The Making of a Scientist*. Roe credits it to a physicist she does not name. Similar sayings without Murphy's name were in print as early as 1877.

The gloomy reading won because the law is so easy to confirm. Across enough events some failures are statistically certain, which the statistician David Hand calls the law of truly large numbers. People remember the toast that landed butter-side down and forget the far more numerous times nothing went wrong. That turned a working engineer's rule into a cosmic joke.

The safe statement is narrower than the legend. A rule about failure came out of the MX981 tests around 1949 and carries Murphy's name. Its first wording is contested, and its first firm appearance in print under his name is 1952. What it meant to the people who used it was practical: if a part can be fitted the wrong way, design it so it can't be.

**Tell someone:** Who first said Murphy's law is disputed; its earliest firm printing under his name, in 1952, credits an unnamed physicist.

*Sources:* Spark, *A History of Murphy's Law* (2006). "The Real-Life Murphy and How 'Murphy's Law' Came to Be," Military.com. Roe, *The Making of a Scientist* (1952). Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/murphys-law/

# Round 2: Who really said it?

Lines pinned on the wrong person, or on nobody in particular.

A good line rarely stays with the person who wrote it. It drifts toward a more famous name, loses a word, or gains a sharper one. Each saying here comes with a name: a medieval friar, a psychologist, an economist, a science-fiction writer and several famous wits, and one label comes with the two psychologists who named it. The question is whether the name and the words belong together. Guess first, then turn the page.

## Did William of Ockham write "Entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The famous Latin sentence is nowhere in Ockham's surviving works. Its earliest close version was written by John Punch in 1639, nearly three centuries after Ockham died. Ockham argued for the same principle, in other words.

The popular version is that the medieval friar William of Ockham wrote *Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*, "entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity," and so gave his name to the razor.

The record starts with Ockham himself. William of Ockham (c. 1287 to 1347) was an English Franciscan who argued repeatedly for parsimony. One of his own statements is in his *Summa Logicae*, part I, chapter 12. The phrasing the record quotes from him is *Pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate*: "plurality should not be posited without necessity."

The famous sentence came later. The Irish Franciscan John Punch wrote a close version, *non sunt multiplicanda entia sine necessitate*, in a 1639 commentary on Duns Scotus, and called it a common axiom of the Scholastics. The name came later still. Libert Froidmont coined *novacula occami*, Occam's razor, in 1649. And the idea is older than all of them: Aristotle and Ptolemy both stated versions.

The misattribution spread because the sentence and the name fit together so neatly. Punch's line is crisper than Ockham's own, and Punch presented it as a shared saying rather than his own invention, so it traveled without an author. Ten years later Froidmont's coinage supplied one. A sentence without a writer attached itself to the nearest famous name, and the razor has been quoted in words close to Punch's under Ockham's name ever since.

None of this weakens the principle, which is a tie-breaker between explanations that both fit the facts: prefer the one that assumes less. It changes only the credit. Ockham's own wording is in the *Summa Logicae*. A close version of the famous Latin sentence first appears in Punch's commentary of 1639, and the name in Froidmont in 1649.

**Tell someone:** The famous Latin line of Occam's razor is not in Ockham's works; John Punch wrote a close version in 1639, nearly three centuries after Ockham died.

*Sources:* Ockham, *Summa Logicae*, part I, ch. 12. "Occam's razor," Wikipedia. Baker, "Simplicity," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (rev. 2022). Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/occams-razor/

## Did Hebb say "neurons that fire together wire together"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Donald Hebb set out the rule in 1949. The slogan is not his. It came in 1992, usually credited to Carla Shatz as a mnemonic, and crediting it to Hebb is, in the record's words, the most common error about the rule.

The popular version puts the rhyme in Hebb's mouth, as his own summary of how learning works in the brain.

The record goes back to the book. The Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb stated his rule in *The Organization of Behavior* in 1949. His version is careful and causal: when one neuron repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing another, the connection between them is strengthened. Hebb also proposed that this strengthening welds groups of neurons into working units, which he called cell assemblies.

The slogan flattens that. "Fire together" suggests that firing at the same moment is enough. Hebb's wording is about one cell helping to fire the next. Later research backed the causal reading. Under spike-timing-dependent plasticity, a signal arriving shortly before the receiving cell fires strengthens the connection, and the reverse order weakens it.

The record corrects two other credits that drift to Hebb. He did not discover long-term potentiation, the physiological effect consistent with his rule; Bliss and Lømo found it in 1973. And the idea was not wholly his: earlier associationist proposals came first. His contribution was the crisp synthesis and the cell-assembly framework.

The slogan spread because it was built to. A mnemonic is designed to be remembered, and a rhyme is easy to remember. Once it was in circulation, it was natural to attach it to the scientist whose rule it summarized rather than to the scientist who wrote the summary. The shorter and catchier a line is, the faster it loses its author.

The rule is Hebb's, set out in *The Organization of Behavior* in 1949. The rhyme is usually credited to Carla Shatz, who printed it in 1992; Siegrid Löwel and Wolf Singer printed a similar line the same year.

**Tell someone:** Hebb set out his rule in 1949, but the rhyme "fire together, wire together" came in 1992 and is usually credited to Carla Shatz.

*Sources:* Hebb, *The Organization of Behavior* (1949; reissued 2005). "Donald Olding Hebb," Canadian Association for Neuroscience. "Hebbian theory," ScienceDirect Topics. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/hebbs-rule/

## Did Mark Twain say "If all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** There is no evidence that Mark Twain said it. The idea was named by the philosopher Abraham Kaplan in 1964, after a talk in 1962. Its best-known hammer-and-nail form is Abraham Maslow's, from 1966.

The popular version is a Twain quip about the habit of using the one tool a person knows for every job.

The record traces a different line. Kaplan named and formalized the idea as the law of the instrument in *The Conduct of Inquiry* (1964), having aired it in a 1962 talk. His image was a small boy who, given a hammer, finds that everything he meets needs pounding. Two years later Abraham Maslow gave the most famous version in *The Psychology of Science* (1966). That is why the bias is often called Maslow's hammer. Maslow also imagined a machine built to wash cars that treats everything fed into it as a car to be washed.

The record adds two cautions. First, Maslow did not coin the idea; "Maslow's hammer" credits his phrasing, not the invention. Second, the familiar line about hammers and nails is a paraphrase, not a word-for-word quotation from either man. The Twain attribution is the most common misattribution of all, and there is no evidence for it.

The saying drifted to Twain for a reason that recurs throughout this round. A line with no obvious author tends to settle on a famous wit. The paraphrase helped: once the wording had moved away from Kaplan's and Maslow's own, nothing in the line itself pointed back to them. A quip that sounds like Twain gets credited to Twain.

The point of the law survives the mix-up. It warns against applying a familiar method where it does not fit, not against reuse or expertise. The earliest dated source in the record is Kaplan's 1962 talk, set in print in 1964. Maslow's version followed in 1966. Twain has no place in the record.

**Tell someone:** There is no evidence Twain said it: Abraham Kaplan named the law of the instrument in 1964, and Maslow's hammer-and-nail version followed in 1966.

## Did Goodhart say "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The famous sentence is Marilyn Strathern's, from 1997. Charles Goodhart's original point, in 1975, was narrower and about monetary policy.

The popular version credits the one-liner to Goodhart himself, as the summary of his law in his own words.

The record has both texts. In 1975 the economist Charles Goodhart wrote a paper on British monetary policy, "Problems of Monetary Management: the U.K. Experience." His point was that any statistical regularity the authorities leaned on would collapse once they used it for control. The record calls it a monetary cousin of the Lucas critique.

The sentence everyone quotes appeared 22 years later, in a different field. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, writing about audit culture in British universities in "'Improving ratings': audit in the British University system" (*European Review*, 1997), generalized Goodhart's point into "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." She was rephrasing a 1996 gloss on Goodhart's law by Keith Hoskin.

The general form is the one that traveled, because it applies everywhere. Rank schools on test scores and teachers teach to the test. Hold emergency departments to a waiting-time target and some meet it by reclassifying patients. Reward engineers for lines of code and the code bloats. A rule about monetary statistics reaches economists; a rule about every target reaches everyone. In 2018 David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant sorted the ways it bites into four types: regressional, extremal, causal and adversarial.

The misattribution spread because the generalization kept the eponym. Strathern's sentence generalized Goodhart's law and traveled under his name, and a law is assumed to be quoted in its namesake's words. Each repetition under his name made the original 1975 paper less likely to be read, and the narrower point it made less likely to be noticed.

So the law is Goodhart's and the sentence is Strathern's. The earliest dated source is Goodhart's 1975 paper on monetary management. The famous wording first appears in Strathern's article of 1997.

**Tell someone:** The famous line about measures becoming targets is Marilyn Strathern's, from 1997; Goodhart's 1975 point was narrower and about monetary policy.

## Did Napoleon say "Never attribute to malice what can be explained by stupidity"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The line is not Napoleon's. Quote Investigator, the source the record cites for its dated history of the saying, debunks the Napoleon attribution. The earliest known printing of the razor is from 1980.

The popular version attaches the warning against blaming malice for what stupidity explains to Napoleon.

The record dates the saying. Its first known appearance in print is Arthur Bloch's *Murphy's Law Book Two* (1980), page 52, under the title "Hanlon's razor." Before that come antecedents. The closest is in Robert A. Heinlein's 1941 novella "Logic of Empire": "You have attributed conditions to villainy that simply result from stupidity." Lines without owners drift toward famous names, and a remark about malice and incompetence sounds weightier from a famous historical figure. The attribution gave a modern adage an air of age and authority that its real history, a 1980 humor book, does not.

The record is clear on what is known. The razor's first known printing is 1980, in Bloch's book. Heinlein had a close version in 1941 and Goethe a version in 1774. Napoleon is not in the chain.

## Did Godwin say whoever mentions Hitler first loses?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Mike Godwin's 1990 law says only that a long online argument is likely to produce a comparison to Hitler or the Nazis. The rule that whoever makes the comparison loses was added later by the Usenet community, not by Godwin.

The popular version treats the "loser" rule as part of Godwin's law: whoever invokes Hitler ends the argument and loses it.

The record goes back to 1990. Godwin, an American attorney, coined the law that year on early internet forums, chiefly Usenet. In its usual form it says that as an online discussion grows longer, the probability of a comparison to Hitler or the Nazis approaches one. Godwin has described it as a deliberate experiment in memetics: a counter-meme, seeded to discourage the reflexive Hitler comparisons he felt trivialized the Holocaust. He documented the method in a 1994 *Wired* article, "Meme, Counter-meme."

The loser rule came from the community that adopted the law. A Usenet tradition, recorded in the hacker Jargon File, added that once someone invokes the Nazis the thread is over and the person who made the comparison has lost. Godwin resists it. He has said the law should start a conversation, not end one, and that it was never meant to be predictive. After the 2017 rally in Charlottesville he publicly set the law aside for marchers carrying swastikas, saying that comparing them to Nazis was fair.

The corollary spread because it turned an observation into a weapon. Godwin's version describes a pattern. The loser rule gives anyone in an argument a way to win it, by declaring the other side out of bounds. A rule that ends arguments is more useful in an argument than a remark about how arguments tend to go, so it was repeated more. The record calls citing the law to shut down a well-founded comparison its most common misuse.

The law itself is narrow. The earliest dated source in the record is Godwin's 1990 coinage, with his own account in *Wired* in 1994. The loser rule is folk tradition added afterward.

**Tell someone:** Godwin's 1990 law says only that a long online argument is likely to produce a Hitler comparison; the loser rule came later, from Usenet.

## Did Sturgeon say "Ninety percent of everything is crap"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Theodore Sturgeon's printed word was "crud," not "crap." His *Venture Science Fiction* column first printed it in September 1957. In March 1958 the column gave it as "Ninety percent of everything is crud." He also called it his Revelation, not his law.

The popular version quotes Sturgeon's law in a blunter word: "Ninety percent of everything is crap."

The record has a timeline. Theodore Sturgeon, a science-fiction author, aired the idea in a lecture around 1951 and at the 1953 World Science Fiction Convention in Philadelphia. He first put it in print in his *Venture Science Fiction* book column in September 1957, and in March 1958 set it out formally, "Ninety percent of everything is crud," with two corollaries.

The context was a defense. Critics were judging all of science fiction by its pulpiest work. Sturgeon's answer was that most science fiction is indeed poor, because most of every genre is, and that the best of it stands with the best writing anywhere. The saying was a case for judging a field by its finest work rather than its most abundant.

There is a second slip in the popular version. Sturgeon called the 90% line his Revelation. His actual law was a different sentence: "Nothing is always absolutely so," a warning against absolutes. Popular usage, and the *Oxford English Dictionary*, folded the Revelation into the law, and that is now the standard sense.

The record does not say when "crap" replaced "crud." The general pattern is familiar: a quotation that travels by word of mouth tends to get sharper, and a blunter word makes a stronger line. The same drift turned the Revelation into the law, because a named law is easier to cite than a revelation.

The "90%" was never a measurement, as the record notes, and the word was never "crap." The earliest printing is Sturgeon's column of September 1957, and like the March 1958 version it says crud.

**Tell someone:** In print in 1957 and 1958, Sturgeon wrote that ninety percent of everything is "crud," and he called it his Revelation.

## Did Twain or Einstein say "A man with one watch knows what time it is"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Neither did. The saying first appears without a name in the *San Diego Union* in 1930. The attributions to Mark Twain and Albert Einstein are unsupported, and no documented Segal wrote it either.

The popular version credits the line about the man with one watch and the man with two to Twain or to Einstein.

The record follows the line through three stages. It first appears, anonymously, in the *San Diego Union* in 1930: "a man with one watch knows what time it is, and a man with two watches could never be sure." In 1961 a newspaper columnist attributed it to Lee Segall, a broadcaster in Dallas. It then picked up the label "Segal's law," spelled with one l, through Arthur Bloch's *Murphy's Law* books. The record calls the eponym unidentified: no documented person named Segal is its author.

The sailor's version, quoted by Fred Brooks, is: "Never go to sea with two chronometers; take one or three." With three, a majority vote shows which one is wrong. A sharp line with no author attached tends to be given one, and the owners it finds are the most quotable names available. Einstein adds a further pull for a saying about time and measurement. ## Did the researchers who named it call it "impostor syndrome"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The two psychologists who named it, Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes, called it "the impostor phenomenon" in a 1978 paper. "Syndrome" is popular usage, not their term, and the experience appears in neither of the two main diagnostic manuals.

The popular version is that impostor syndrome is a recognized condition, described under that name by the researchers who first identified it.

The record starts with the paper itself: "The Impostor Phenomenon in High Achieving Women: Dynamics and Therapeutic Intervention," published in *Psychotherapy: Theory, Research & Practice*. Clance and Imes drew on clinical work with high-achieving professional women who believed their success came from luck, timing or a fooled assessor, whatever their record showed. They described a loop. Over-prepare, or put the work off and then rush it; succeed either way; then credit the effort or the charm rather than the ability.

The difference in wording matters. A phenomenon names an experience a person has; a syndrome suggests a disorder. The record keeps to the first. The experience is in neither DSM-5 nor ICD-11, so its prevalence and its best treatment are still unsettled, and a 2019 systematic review of 62 studies found that none had tested whether any treatment works. Later research also found the experience across genders and professions, not only among the women of the first study.

"Syndrome" spread because it sounds like medicine. A clinical label makes a private feeling seem official and shared, and sharing was much of what people wanted from the name: those who thought they were uniquely fraudulent learned the feeling was common. The term became one of the most searched psychological labels in working life. The record adds that popular use has drifted toward ordinary, proportionate nerves about new work, which is not what the 1978 paper described.

So the word on the original page is "phenomenon." Clance and Imes coined that term in 1978, and the "syndrome" most people now say came from popular usage, not from the researchers.

**Tell someone:** The psychologists who named it in 1978 called it the "impostor phenomenon"; "impostor syndrome" is popular usage, and it is not a diagnosis.

# Round 3: Famous experiments, retold

What the textbook version leaves out.

Psychology's most famous experiments reach most people as stories: a few lines, a striking number, a lesson about human nature. The stories are shorter than the studies. A percentage loses the condition that produced it, and pilot runs, script changes and single subjects drop out of the retelling. Each question in this round asks whether the version people repeat matches what the record shows.

## Did 38 witnesses watch Kitty Genovese die and do nothing?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The record finds no basis for the story that 38 witnesses watched Kitty Genovese die and did nothing. At least one witness shouted at her attacker.

The popular version is short and bleak. Kitty Genovese was murdered in 1964 while 38 people watched, and not one of them acted. It is often told as proof that a crowd will leave a person to die.

The record does not support it. In a 2007 paper in *American Psychologist*, Manning, Levine and Collins documented the exaggerations in the account of her murder. The number 38 has no basis, and at least one witness shouted at the attacker. Kitty Genovese's death was real. The image of a silent crowd watching it is not supported by the evidence.

The case, as misreported, did prompt real research. In two experiments in 1968, John Darley and Bibb Latané formalized what became known as the bystander effect. People who believed they alone could hear someone having a seizure over an intercom almost all responded quickly. Those who thought several others were listening responded less often and more slowly. The effect holds in the laboratory under narrow conditions: ambiguous, non-dangerous emergencies among strangers.

Outside the laboratory the picture is kinder. A 2020 analysis by Philpot and colleagues of 219 real public conflicts recorded on CCTV found that in nine cases in ten at least one bystander intervened, and that more bystanders meant more help. In clearly dangerous emergencies the effect weakens or reverses.

The false version spread because it was vivid and because it became attached to a real finding. A single shocking case with a precise number is easy to remember and easy to repeat, and each retelling of the science carried the anecdote along with it. The effect even acquired a colloquial name, the Genovese syndrome, which tied her name to a partly mythologized event. Once a story becomes the standard illustration of a result, few people go back to check it.

The bystander effect is real in narrow laboratory conditions, and in real public conflicts intervention is the norm. The earliest source in the record to set out the corrections to the Genovese story is Manning, Levine and Collins, 2007. The record, drawing on it, finds no basis for the 38, and at least one witness acted.

**Tell someone:** The record finds no basis for 38 silent witnesses to Kitty Genovese's murder, and at least one witness shouted at her attacker.

*Sources:* Manning, Levine & Collins, "The Kitty Genovese murder and the social psychology of helping," *American Psychologist* 62(6) (2007). Philpot et al., "Would I be helped? ## Did the Stanford prison guards turn cruel on their own?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The guards did not become cruel on their own. Archival recordings and later scholarship show that the experimenters, including the warden, instructed and pressured them to be tough.

The popular version runs as follows. In 1971 ordinary students at Stanford were given guard uniforms and authority over fellow students playing prisoners, and within days they turned abusive without being told to. The lesson drawn is that a bad situation can make anyone cruel.

The basic events are on record. The study was led by the psychologist Philip Zimbardo in 1971. Twenty-four screened male student volunteers were randomly assigned to be guards or prisoners in a mock prison in the basement of the psychology building. It was planned for two weeks and stopped after six days, as guards grew abusive and prisoners showed extreme distress. Zimbardo argued that the situation and the roles, not the participants' dispositions, drove the cruelty.

The later evidence points elsewhere. Archival recordings and later scholarship, notably by Thibault Le Texier, show that the experimenters actively instructed and pressured the guards to be tough; Le Texier concluded that the abuse was substantially coached rather than spontaneous. The volunteers answered an advertisement for a study of prison life, which the sources say attracted people higher in aggression and authoritarianism. A key prisoner later said his breakdown was acting, and many guards said they were consciously playing a role. The study had no control condition, and critics have said it never went through normal peer review, which Zimbardo disputed. The sources also record serious ethical problems, including participants who effectively could not leave.

The popular version spread because it offered a simple, dramatic answer to a hard question. The study was widely taught, and it was invoked in discussions of prisons, abuse and atrocities such as Abu Ghraib. A story that seems to explain real cruelty is easy to repeat and hard to give up. The sources now treat the study as an example of a compelling narrative outrunning weak evidence.

The defensible statement is narrower than the legend. The study did end early amid abuse and distress. The claim that the guards' cruelty arose from the situation alone is Zimbardo's interpretation, and the record pairs it with archival evidence that the guards were coached.

**Tell someone:** Archival recordings show the Stanford prison guards were instructed and pressured to be tough, which Thibault Le Texier argues made the abuse substantially coached.

## Did Hawthorne factory output rise whatever the lighting?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The original Hawthorne lighting data do not show output rising whatever the lighting. When Steven Levitt and John List reanalyzed them, they called the famous patterns "entirely fictional".

The popular version is a neat paradox. At a factory in the 1920s, researchers brightened the lights and output rose. They dimmed the lights and output rose again. The conclusion drawn was that the workers were responding to the attention of being studied, not to the lighting.

The studies were real. They ran from 1924 to 1932 at the Hawthorne Works, a Western Electric factory in Cicero, Illinois, and included the illumination and relay-assembly experiments associated with Elton Mayo's team. The term "Hawthorne effect" came decades later, during reanalyses in the 1950s. It is usually credited to Henry Landsberger in 1958, with an earlier informal use sometimes attributed to John R. P. French.

The data tell a weaker story. The illumination records were long thought lost. Levitt and List located them and published their reanalysis as an NBER working paper in 2009 and in *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics* in 2011. They reported that the claim that every lighting change boosted output is not supported. The apparent effect largely disappears once day-of-week and pay-period cycles are controlled for, leaving only, in their words, "hints of more subtle manifestations". The original studies also rested on tiny samples and tangled confounds, among them pay incentives, changes in supervision and the replacement of workers.

The paradox spread because it was memorable and, for decades, unchecked. With the original records thought lost, the story could be retold but not tested against the numbers. It also came with a name, and a name makes an idea easy to cite. The sources describe the Hawthorne effect as a case of a memorable name outrunning its evidence.

The general idea survives. People who know they are being watched can change their behavior, which is why researchers use blinding and control groups. The founding factory data, reopened by Levitt and List in 2009, do not show the famous pattern.

**Tell someone:** When Levitt and List reopened the original Hawthorne lighting records, they called the famous output patterns "entirely fictional".

## Did 65% of Milgram's volunteers give the maximum shock?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** About 65 percent of participants went to the maximum 450 volts in one baseline condition. Across the more than twenty conditions Milgram ran, obedience varied widely, and archival research has questioned how many participants believed the shocks were real.

The popular version turns that number into a fact about people. Two-thirds of ordinary people, it says, will deliver a seemingly lethal shock to a stranger if someone in authority tells them to.

The experiments began in 1961 at Yale under the psychologist Stanley Milgram and were first published in 1963. Milgram launched them amid the trial of Adolf Eichmann, asking how ordinary people could take part in atrocities under orders. Each participant, cast as a teacher, was told by an experimenter in a lab coat to shock a learner for every wrong answer, raising the voltage each time. The learner was an actor and received no shocks. The generator was labeled up to 450 volts and marked as dangerous. When teachers hesitated, the experimenter used fixed prods, from "please continue" to "you have no other choice, you must go on". Yale psychology seniors he polled beforehand predicted that only a tiny fraction would reach the maximum.

The 65 percent belongs to one condition. In variants that changed how close the teacher was to the learner or to the authority, obedience rates differed widely. Archival research, notably by Gina Perry, has argued that only about half of participants fully believed the shocks were real, that experimenters sometimes went off the fixed script, and that results may have been presented selectively. These are Perry's arguments. The record treats them as part of a live dispute, not as a settled finding.

The single figure spread because it is easier to carry than twenty conditions. It also arrived with moral weight. Coming after the Holocaust and the Eichmann trial, a finding that a majority would obey to the end shocked the public and the profession. A number that seems to answer a question that large tends to be repeated without its conditions.

The checked statement is this. In the baseline condition about 65 percent of participants went to 450 volts. That figure is not a fixed human rate, and how far participants believed what they were doing is disputed.

**Tell someone:** Milgram's famous 65 percent came from one baseline condition, obedience varied widely across his variants, and how many believed the shocks were real is disputed.

## Did Milgram prove we are all six handshakes apart?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Milgram did not prove that everyone is six handshakes apart. Most of the chains in his 1967 experiment never reached their target, and the "six" is a median over the minority that did.

The popular version credits "six degrees of separation" to a single experiment. Stanley Milgram, it says, showed that any two people are linked by six acquaintances.

The experiment was real. Around 1967 Milgram had people forward a folder toward a target in Boston, from acquaintance to acquaintance. The chains that were completed passed through a median of roughly five to six intermediaries. Most never arrived. When Judith Kleinfeld re-examined Milgram's data and archives in 2002, she found that few chains were completed, that the samples were small, and that selection bias was present. If people drop out at a roughly constant rate at each step, longer chains are the ones most likely to fail, so the true distance could even be larger than the completed chains suggest.

The idea, and the phrase, came from elsewhere. The Hungarian writer Frigyes Karinthy proposed it in a 1929 short story, "Chains", which posited no more than five intermediaries. John Guare's 1990 play popularized the phrase "six degrees of separation". Counting adds confusion: five intermediaries make about six links, and degrees and hops are often mixed up.

Later work put the idea on firmer ground in a different form. In 1998 Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz showed that a network can be tightly clustered and still crossed in a few steps, provided a few links reach far away. A 2012 study of the whole Facebook graph, some 721 million users, found an average distance of about 4.7 hops. That figure counts every link in a complete graph and is not directly comparable to Milgram's lossy chains.

The legend spread because a round number is easier to remember than a completion rate. "Six degrees" had a catchy phrase, a play behind it and a famous experiment to point to. The failed chains were invisible, because a chain that never arrives leaves nothing to count. The sources call it a case of a vivid number outrunning the evidence behind it.

What Milgram showed is narrower. Short paths exist between some pairs of people, and people can sometimes find them using only local knowledge of who seems closer to the target. The six is a median of the chains that finished, not a law.

**Tell someone:** Most of the chains in Milgram's small-world experiment never reached their target, and the famous six is a median of the minority that did.

Kleinfeld, "The Small World Problem," *Society* 39(2) (2002). Watts & Strogatz, *Nature* 393 (1998). "Small-world experiment," Wikipedia. ## On most trials, did Asch's subjects go along with the wrong answer?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** On most trials, Asch's subjects did not go along. They gave the group's wrong answer on about a third of critical trials and stayed independent on about two-thirds.

The popular version holds that people will deny the evidence of their own eyes when a group tells them to. In the retelling, the subjects in Solomon Asch's line experiments usually caved.

The experiments ran principally around 1951 at Swarthmore College. A real participant sat with a group of confederates, typically around seven, and everyone said aloud which of three lines matched a reference line. The answer was obvious. On critical trials the confederates all gave the same wrong answer before the participant spoke. People judging alone erred less than one percent of the time, which showed the task was easy.

Two numbers came out, and they are easy to confuse. Across all critical trials, about a third of answers conformed to the wrong majority. Separately, about three-quarters of participants conformed at least once. Many never conformed at all, and some who went along aloud yielded only in public, without changing their minds.

The conditions mattered. Unanimity was crucial: a single confederate who gave the right answer sharply reduced conformity. Conformity rose with group size up to about three confederates and then leveled off. The participants were male American college students of a particular era, rates vary with culture and task, and a 1990 survey by Friend, Rafferty and Bramel found that most textbooks overstated it.

The stronger version spread partly through that pair of figures. "Three-quarters conformed at least once" slides easily into "three-quarters conformed". A story about caving also makes a sharper lesson than one about partial independence, and the sharper lesson is the one that gets repeated.

The record is clear on the proportion. Participants went along with an obviously wrong answer about a third of the time and held to what they saw about two-thirds of the time. A single dissenting ally sharply reduced even that third.

**Tell someone:** Asch's subjects held to what they saw on about two-thirds of critical trials, and one dissenting ally sharply cut conformity.

## Does waiting for a second marshmallow predict success in life?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** Early follow-ups linked a preschooler's ability to wait with later achievement. A larger 2018 replication found the link much weaker and largely explained by family background.

The popular version is a parable about willpower. Children who could wait for a second marshmallow, it says, grew into more successful adults, so self-control at four predicts success in life.

The study came from the psychologist Walter Mischel and colleagues at Stanford University around 1970. A child, typically aged between about three and a half and five and a half, was left alone with one treat and told that waiting until the experimenter returned, usually up to fifteen minutes, would earn two. Children who distracted themselves, or pictured the marshmallow as a cloud rather than food, tended to wait longer. In follow-ups, notably a 1990 study by Shoda, Mischel and Peake, preschool wait times were correlated with later measures such as SAT scores and parental ratings.

The limits are on record. The original follow-up samples were small and drawn from a relatively privileged Stanford-community population. In 2018 Watts, Duncan and Quan re-ran the design with about 900 children and controls for background, and the association shrank substantially. A child's willingness to wait can also reflect trust that the promised reward will arrive, and the reliability of the child's surroundings, not only self-control. The test records one moment in one artificial situation.

The verdict is DISPUTED rather than FOLKLORE because the record does not say the link is zero. It says the effect is much weaker and largely explained by background, so the strong claim is heavily qualified rather than refuted.

The strong version spread because it confirmed something many people already believed. It gave scientific weight to the intuition that resisting temptation pays, and it became a staple of popular writing on willpower, parenting and character. A small, privileged sample produced a clean story, and clean stories travel further than their samples.

The checked statement is modest. Waiting for a second marshmallow was associated with later achievement in small early samples. In a larger, more diverse study, most of that association reflected family background.

**Tell someone:** Waiting for a second marshmallow tracked later achievement in small early samples, but a larger 2018 study found the link largely explained by family background.

## Did the Robbers Cave study have a failed first attempt?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Before the famous 1954 Robbers Cave study, Muzafer Sherif ran a pilot in 1953 that failed. The boys resisted the hostility the researchers tried to induce, and suspected the staff.

The version usually taught is clean. Two groups of boys at a summer camp became rivals through competition, then reconciled by working together on shared goals.

That version is accurate as far as it goes. In 1954 Muzafer Sherif and Carolyn Wood Sherif, with colleagues, brought twenty-two boys aged eleven and twelve to a summer camp at Robbers Cave State Park in Oklahoma. The boys did not know each other and did not know they were being studied. Split into two groups, they named themselves the Rattlers and the Eagles. A tournament with prizes only for the winners produced name-calling, flag-burning and cabin raids. Pleasant contact between the groups did not reduce the hostility. Tasks that neither group could manage alone, such as restoring the camp's water supply, freeing a stuck truck and pooling money for a film, gradually did.

The failed attempt complicates the picture. In the 1953 pilot, the boys resisted the induced hostility and suspected the staff. The sources say this failure is often omitted, and that it raises questions about how readily the effect appears. The 1954 study has further limits on record. The researchers arranged the competition and, by some accounts, notably the psychologist Gina Perry's, manipulated events to provoke conflict, so the hostility was partly engineered. The sample was small and uniform: white, middle-class, Protestant American boys of similar age. Their parents were not told the study's true purpose.

The tidy version spread because it has the shape of a good lesson: conflict, then reconciliation. A failed pilot interrupts that shape. A retelling that starts from the 1954 results has no reason to mention an earlier camp where the plan did not work, so each retelling inherits the clean ending.

The record is clear on the sequence. The study that became famous in 1954 followed a 1953 attempt in which the boys resisted the induced hostility, and that first attempt belongs in the evidence.

**Tell someone:** Before the famous 1954 Robbers Cave camp, a 1953 pilot failed when the boys resisted the induced hostility and suspected the staff.

## Was the forgetting curve based on just one person?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The forgetting curve rests on one subject: Hermann Ebbinghaus, who tested himself. Its best-known modern replication, in 2015, also used essentially one subject.

The popular version treats the curve as a general law of memory, measured across many people and describing how everyone forgets.

The record shows a single, methodical experimenter. In the early 1880s Ebbinghaus memorized lists of consonant-vowel-consonant nonsense syllables, such as "WID" and "ZOF", chosen to strip away prior meaning. He measured retention by the savings method: how much time or effort he saved when relearning a list after a delay. He published the results in 1885 as *Über das Gedächtnis*, translated in 1913 as *Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology*. The curve he found falls steeply in the first hours to a day after learning, then slows and levels toward a floor rather than dropping to zero.

In 2015 Murre and Dros replicated the curve in *PLOS ONE*. They largely confirmed its shape, but again with essentially one subject. "Replicated" here means the shape reproduces, not that it was validated across a large sample. They also found a probable upward jump around the 24-hour mark, so the curve is not perfectly smooth, and its usual exponential formula is an approximation.

The material matters as much as the sample. Nonsense syllables were chosen to mean nothing. Meaningful material is encoded and forgotten differently, and emotionally vivid memories do not follow the same decay.

The large-sample version spread because the curve looks like a law. It has a clean shape, a formula and a long history, and it underwrites practical tools, from flashcards to spaced-repetition software. A replication sounds like independent confirmation by many people, and few users of the curve ask how many people were in either study.

The record is plain on the point. The forgetting curve's shape has held up twice, and both times it was measured in essentially one person learning syllables designed to mean nothing. Its robust lessons are qualitative: forgetting is fast at first and then slows, and spaced review counters it.

**Tell someone:** The forgetting curve was measured on one man, Ebbinghaus, learning nonsense syllables, and its 2015 replication again used essentially one subject.

*Sources:* Ebbinghaus, *Über das Gedächtnis* (1885). Murre & Dros, "Replication and Analysis of Ebbinghaus' Forgetting Curve," *PLOS ONE* 10(7) (2015). "Forgetting curve," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/ebbinghaus-forgetting-curve/

## Do we know what became of Little Albert?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** No one has settled who Little Albert was or what became of him. Rival candidates have been proposed, and the record says there is no settled answer.

The popular version has a tidy ending. The infant in the famous 1920 study, it says, was later identified, and his later life is known.

The study itself is on record. In 1920 at Johns Hopkins University, the behaviorist John B. Watson and Rosalie Rayner, his graduate student, set out to show that fear could be conditioned in a human being. Albert was an infant of around nine to eleven months who at first showed no fear of a white laboratory rat. The experimenters repeatedly presented the rat while striking a steel bar with a hammer behind his head. After several pairings he cried and tried to crawl away from the rat alone. His fear appeared to spread to a rabbit, a dog, a fur coat, cotton wool and a bearded Santa Claus mask, though unevenly. The conditioning was never reversed, and Albert left the study still fearful.

The study's evidence is thin. It had one subject and no controls, and it relied on the experimenters' own judgments of the child's reactions. Later reviewers, starting with Ben Harris in 1979, found little evidence that the animals consistently evoked fear.

The question of who he was remains open. Two candidates have been proposed, Douglas Merritte and William Barger. The record calls the identity and fate of Little Albert genuinely disputed, and says claims about who he was or what became of him should be treated as uncertain.

The tidy version spread because a famous story invites an ending. A named child with a known fate turns a disturbing experiment into a complete narrative. When more than one candidate exists, each identification can be retold on its own as the answer, without the rival that contests it.

The checked statement is narrow. The 1920 experiment happened, and its methods and flaws are on record. Who the infant was, and what became of him, is not settled.

**Tell someone:** Who Little Albert was, and what became of him, is still unsettled; rival candidates have been proposed, and none has won out.

## Is the "Mount Stupid" curve from the Dunning–Kruger paper?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The "Mount Stupid" curve is not in Kruger and Dunning's 1999 paper. The record describes it as a later internet embellishment.

The popular version is a picture. Confidence is plotted against experience. It shoots up to a peak labeled Mount Stupid after a little learning, falls as the size of the subject comes into view, and climbs slowly again with real expertise. The chart is usually captioned as the Dunning–Kruger effect.

The paper measured something else. Justin Kruger and David Dunning published "Unskilled and Unaware of It" in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* in 1999. They tested people on grammar, logic and humor, then asked them to estimate how they ranked. Low performers systematically overestimated their rank. Students who scored in the bottom quarter, around the 12th percentile on average, estimated that they were around the 62nd. The paper's subject is self-estimated rank set against actual rank. Confidence over a learning career, the curve's axis, is not what the 1999 studies reported.

The mountain is not in the paper at all. The record places it later and online, and no peak, valley or "experience" axis comes from the journal article.

The curve spread because it matches a feeling many people recognize. A little knowledge can feel like a lot: after one course or one article confidence can spike, and deeper study brings doubt back as the true size of a field comes into view. A shape that matches experience feels true, and a label like Mount Stupid is easy to share. It also makes a handy weapon in arguments. The record warns against that use: the effect is not a license to call people one disagrees with stupid.

What the paper found, and how well it holds up, is the next question in this round. The answer to this one is simpler. The 1999 paper compared people's estimates of their rank with their actual rank on tests of grammar, logic and humor. The mountain came later, from the internet, not from the journal.

**Tell someone:** The "Mount Stupid" curve appears nowhere in Kruger and Dunning's 1999 paper, which compared people's estimated rank with their actual test rank.

## Do the least skilled people really overrate themselves the most?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** In 1999 Kruger and Dunning found that the lowest scorers on several tests overestimated their rank. Since 2016 several researchers have argued that much of that pattern is a statistical artifact, and a 2026 reanalysis reports that it inverts.

The popular version is blunter than the paper. The incompetent, it says, are too incompetent to know it, while experts underrate themselves. In its shortest form: stupid people think they are geniuses.

The original finding is narrower. Justin Kruger and David Dunning's 1999 paper, "Unskilled and Unaware of It", tested people on grammar, logic and humor. Students who scored in the bottom quarter, around the 12th percentile, estimated they were around the 62nd. The explanation offered was that the knowledge needed to do a task well is largely the knowledge needed to judge how well it was done, so a beginner lacks both. Even taken at face value, the record describes the effect as a modest miscalibration, not a claim that the least able think they are the best.

The critics' case is statistical. In 2016 Nuhfer and colleagues showed that random numbers can produce the same picture, low performers overestimating and high performers underestimating. In 2020 Gignac and Zajenkowski argued that the usual method is confounded by regression to the mean and the better-than-average effect, and in their own sample of 929 adults found little support for it. People with extreme scores on one measure tend to drift toward the average on any second measure, so the pattern can appear whether or not a cognitive effect exists. In 2026 Dawson and de Meza went further in *Psychological Review*. Reanalyzing large replication datasets with methods that account for randomness in both the test score and the confidence prediction, they report that the pattern disappears and then inverts. In their analysis overconfidence appeared at every ability level and was most pronounced among the highest performers.

The pop version spread because it flatters whoever repeats it. It explains other people's confident mistakes and places the speaker among the self-aware. It also has the look of science: a named effect, a journal, a chart. Regression to the mean, the critics' explanation, is invisible in a chart and hard to explain in a sentence.

What is settled is narrow. The 1999 paper reported that low scorers overestimated their rank. Whether that reflects a real cognitive effect, a statistical artifact or the reverse of the popular story is disputed. The record's advice is to read it as a caution about one's own self-assessment, not a verdict on other people.

**Tell someone:** The 1999 finding that low scorers overrated their rank is disputed, and a 2026 reanalysis reports overconfidence was most pronounced among the highest performers.

## Is the famous inverted-U stress curve in Yerkes and Dodson's paper?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The smooth inverted-U curve of stress against performance is not a plot of Yerkes and Dodson's results. Their 1908 paper was a study of mice learning under electric shocks of different strengths.

The popular version is a single bell-shaped curve. Performance rises with arousal or stress to a sweet spot, then falls, and the curve is credited to a 1908 study.

The paper says something narrower. Robert M. Yerkes and John Dillingham Dodson published "The Relation of Strength of Stimulus to Rapidity of Habit-Formation" in the *Journal of Comparative Neurology and Psychology* in 1908. Japanese "dancing mice" learned a black-or-white discrimination in which one box gave a mild shock. Stronger shocks sped learning up to a point, then slowed it. A middle strength worked best in two of the three experiments, and the best strength depended on how hard the task was; in the easiest, learning simply sped up as the shocks grew stronger. The evidence came from forty mice. Arousal and stress were never measured. Shock intensity was reinterpreted as arousal decades later.

The curve was built afterward. The paper was cited only a handful of times for half a century. Interest revived in the 1950s, when D. O. Hebb's arousal concept, from 1955, was mapped onto the 1908 result and generalized to humans. The textbook graph reproduced everywhere is essentially Hebb's. Replication has been poor: follow-ups in chicks and kittens found no slowing at higher shock, and many human studies find simply that more stress means worse performance.

The curve spread because it matches a familiar feeling. Mild nerves before an exam can sharpen focus, while overwhelming anxiety impairs recall and reasoning. A smooth curve with two old names attached looks like settled science. Popularity also stripped out the awkward detail. The task-difficulty point, the part of the original most worth keeping, is the part the popular curve usually drops. That omission wrongly implies that high arousal harms even simple tasks.

The 1908 paper measured no stress and no humans. It reported how fast mice learned under shocks of different strengths, with a peak that depended on task difficulty and appeared in two experiments of three.

**Tell someone:** Yerkes and Dodson's 1908 paper measured no stress and no humans, only how fast forty mice learned under shocks of different strengths.

## Did Maslow draw the pyramid of needs?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Maslow did not draw the pyramid. The famous diagram was created by later authors, not by Maslow himself.

The popular version is a five-tier pyramid credited to Abraham Maslow, with physiological needs at the base and self-actualization at the tip, each layer to be completed before the next.

The theory is Maslow's. He set it out in a 1943 paper, "A Theory of Human Motivation", in *Psychological Review*, and developed it in his 1954 book *Motivation and Personality*. He arranged needs in ascending levels: physiological needs such as food, water, warmth and rest; safety; love and belonging; esteem; and self-actualization, the drive to become all one is capable of becoming. He argued that more basic needs must be substantially satisfied before higher ones become strong motivators. He also allowed that the ordering is not rigid, and that people may pursue several needs at once or in a different order. In later versions he added cognitive and aesthetic needs and, above self-actualization, self-transcendence.

A pyramid hides much of that. It has five fixed layers, each resting fully on the one below, and a summit that looks like a finish line, though Maslow held that self-actualization is never fully satisfied. His own account was looser than the shape, and later had more levels.

The theory's evidence is also weaker than its fame. A 1976 review by Wahba and Bridwell found little evidence for the strict hierarchy, and later research has not consistently confirmed that lower needs must be met first. Critics also see a Western individualist bias in placing self-actualization at the top.

The pyramid spread because it is easy to remember and easy to redraw. The theory moved out of psychology into management, education, marketing and healthcare, and the pyramid became one of the most recognizable images in the social sciences, credited to Maslow wherever it went. A picture that simple comes to stand for the theory, and the qualifications in the text are left behind.

The hierarchy is Maslow's and the pyramid is not. The earliest pyramid that Bridgman, Cummings and Ballard could trace is Charles McDermid's, in *Business Horizons* in 1960. It is clear that the diagram came from later authors, and that Maslow's own ordering was less rigid than the shape suggests.

**Tell someone:** The hierarchy of needs is Maslow's, but the pyramid came from later authors, and his own ordering was less rigid than the shape.

## Did Abraham Wald draw the famous bullet-hole plane?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Abraham Wald did not draw the bomber covered in red dots. The record describes that image as a designer's later recreation, a teaching device from decades after the war.

The popular version comes with a picture. A plane seen from above is spattered with red dots where returning bombers were hit. Wald, studying it, tells the military to armor the clean areas instead.

Wald's work was real and is dated. In 1943 he wrote memoranda on estimating how vulnerable aircraft were from the damage on planes that survived. ## Did Wald really tell the military to armor where returning planes weren't hit?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The reasoning in the famous story is Wald's. In his 1943 memoranda he estimated aircraft vulnerability from the damage on returning planes, while accounting for the planes that did not return.

The story is so neat that it is easy to doubt. A statistician looks at bombers riddled with holes and says to armor the places with no holes, because planes hit there never made it home. It sounds like a parable invented to teach a point.

The record treats the anecdote as partly embellished but its core as genuine. In the Second World War, Abraham Wald reasoned that armor should go where returning planes had the fewest bullet holes, not the most, since planes hit in those places did not come back. He made that reasoning rigorous in his 1943 memoranda, which the Center for Naval Analyses reprinted in 1980 as "A Method of Estimating Plane Vulnerability Based on Damage of Survivors". It was rigorous statistics, not a slogan. It accounted for the downed planes rather than treating the survivors as a fair sample.

The embellishments are the picture and the label. The red-dotted airplane was drawn decades later. The name "survivorship bias", and its attachment to Wald, also came later.

The idea Wald formalized now reaches well beyond aircraft. Survivorship bias is the error of studying only what came through a selection process while the failures drop out of view. Funds that perform badly are closed and dropped from databases, so the surviving set overstates average returns, by about 0.9 percent a year in one 1996 study. People admire old buildings and conclude they were built to last, forgetting the flimsy ones long since demolished.

Doubt about the story spread for a fair reason: parts of it were embellished. When a story arrives with a picture drawn long after the event, it is natural to suspect that the rest was invented too. A tale this tidy also looks like the kind made up to teach a lesson. Here the suspicion overshoots.

The reasoning behind the story is on record, dated 1943 and reprinted in 1980. Wald's method rested on the planes that never came back.

**Tell someone:** Wald did reason that armor belongs where returning planes had the fewest bullet holes, since planes hit there did not come back.

## Did Pareto really find that 20% of people held 80% of Italy's land?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** What Vilfredo Pareto did study, in his *Cours d'économie politique* of 1896, was the heavily unequal distribution of income and wealth. The claim that he found 20 percent of the population holding 80 percent of Italy's land has not been traced to his text.

The popular version gives the 80/20 rule a precise birthplace: Pareto counted Italy's landowners and found that a fifth of them held four-fifths of the land.

The record traces the rule's name to Pareto, an economist, and to the *Cours*. The land figure is another matter. It is usually cited to the *Cours* as a whole, with no page, and no passage giving it has been found. The management theorist Joseph M. Juran generalized the pattern into a broad principle of the "vital few" and attached Pareto's name to it in the first edition of his *Quality Control Handbook*, in 1951. He built the principle around quality control: rank the causes of a problem, then fix the few that matter most.

Juran later corrected the attribution himself. In "The Non-Pareto Principle; Mea Culpa" (1974), he wrote that Pareto "had made extensive studies of the unequal distribution of wealth" and that he himself had "mistakenly applied the wrong name" to the rule. The principle as a universal, Juran wrote, "was not original with Pareto," and later authors "fabricated some embellishments" about him.

Suspicion of the round numbers is half right. Eighty and twenty measure different things, a share of causes and a share of effects, so their adding to 100 is a coincidence of the famous example. A real split might be 90/10, 70/30 or 99/1. The principle is about skew, formalized as a power-law, or Pareto, distribution. It is a rule of thumb, not a law of nature, and forcing 80/20 onto a roughly even phenomenon is a category error.

The skew does turn up in practice. In 2002 Microsoft reported that fixing the roughly 20 percent of most-reported bugs removed about 80 percent of the errors and crashes users actually hit. These are the company's own figures, not a controlled study.

The land story spread because it gives an abstract rule a concrete origin. A named economist, a country and a tidy split make a better story than a general finding about income, and a citation to the *Cours* as a whole, with no page, is hard to check. Juran's borrowed name then made the link look settled.

The 80 percent of Italy's land has not been found in Pareto's writing, and Juran later called the name his own mistake. What Pareto did document was the heavy concentration of income and wealth, the kind of skew the rule now describes.

**Tell someone:** The famous 80/20 split of Italy's land has not been traced to Pareto's text; what he actually documented was the heavily unequal distribution of income and wealth.

## Was Benford's law first spotted in worn logarithm tables?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The astronomer Simon Newcomb first noticed the leading-digit law in 1881, from the observation that the early pages of logarithm tables, those for numbers starting with 1, were the most worn. Frank Benford rediscovered it in 1938.

The popular version credits Frank Benford with the discovery, as the name suggests.

The law itself is odd enough to deserve the attention. In large sets of real-world numbers that span many orders of magnitude, 1 is the leading digit about 30 percent of the time and 2 about 18 percent, and the frequencies fall away to under 5 percent for 9. The distribution follows P(d) = log₁₀(1 + 1/d).

Newcomb set out the observation in "Note on the Frequency of Use of the Different Digits in Natural Numbers", in the *American Journal of Mathematics* in 1881. The clue was physical. The early pages of the tables, covering numbers that begin with 1, had worn fastest.

The physicist Frank Benford rediscovered the pattern in his 1938 paper "The Law of Anomalous Numbers". He tested it across more than twenty thousand values, among them river surface areas, populations, physical constants and molecular weights, and their leading digits followed the curve. The law was named after him.

Benford's name stuck because his paper did the work that makes a law famous. It gathered thousands of numbers from unrelated sources, all obeying one curve. A short note about worn pages in a book of tables is easier to overlook. The record calls the case a classic instance of Stigler's law of eponymy, which has its own entry in Round 5.

The law now has a working use and a notorious misuse. Auditors, following Mark Nigrini, screen ledgers and tax figures for sharp deviations that can flag fabricated entries for a closer look. It is also wrongly applied to election vote counts, where precinct totals sit in a narrow range and the law is not expected to hold. Deviation is a prompt to look closer, never proof of manipulation.

The law carries Benford's name and his 1938 evidence. The first observation was Newcomb's, in 1881, made from the wear on books of logarithms.

**Tell someone:** Simon Newcomb first spotted Benford's law in 1881, noticing that the early pages of logarithm tables, for numbers starting with 1, were the most worn.

## Did roulette land on black 26 times in a row at Monte Carlo?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The gambler's fallacy is real, but its most famous example is unverified. No 1913 record of a run of 26 blacks at Monte Carlo has been found; the earliest known account is a 1959 popular book on probability by Darrell Huff and Irving Geis.

The popular version is precise. On 18 August 1913, at the Monte Carlo Casino, the ball landed on black 26 times in a row, and with each black the gamblers grew surer that red was due, raised their bets on red, and lost heavily.

That version has no contemporary witness. Huff and Geis tell it in *How to Take a Chance* (1959), pp. 28–29, with players who "doubled and tripled their stakes" and a run that "enriched the Casino by some millions of francs," as they put it. The writer Robin Quinn searched the Times Digital Archive, the British Newspaper Archive and Google Books, found no mention before Huff, and suspects Huff invented it as an illustration. Wikipedia repeats the date and count without a 1913 source. The error itself is also called the Monte Carlo fallacy.

Whatever happened in 1913, the reasoning in the story is wrong because a roulette wheel has no memory. Each spin keeps its original odds, whatever came before. The mistake comes from misapplying the law of large numbers to short runs. That law says the proportions of outcomes settle down over a large number of trials. It does not say that short sequences must balance out. Early deviations are diluted by volume, not canceled. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman studied the error as a consequence of the representativeness heuristic: expecting a handful of trials to look like the long run.

The fallacy has a strict scope. It applies to independent trials. Where events are not independent, as when sampling without replacement, adjusting expectations from past outcomes is rational. A long run can also reasonably make someone suspect a biased wheel. The error is only the belief that a fair, memoryless wheel must correct itself.

The story spread because it is the ideal illustration. A run of 26 feels as though it must mean something, and a room of gamblers ruined by that feeling turns an abstract error into a scene. A date and a famous casino make an example sound like a record, and each retelling borrowed the precision of the last.

The 1913 run rests on nothing earlier than a 1959 book. The error it illustrates is real: each spin of a fair wheel, including one after 26 blacks, keeps the same odds as the first.

**Tell someone:** The famous 1913 run of 26 blacks at Monte Carlo has no known record before a 1959 book, but the gambler's fallacy it illustrates is real: a fair wheel has no memory.

*Sources:* Huff & Geis, *How to Take a Chance* (1959), pp. 28–29 (earliest known account). Quinn, "Fake News or Fact?" (blog, 2017). "Gambler's fallacy," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-gamblers-fallacy/

## Did Marilyn vos Savant's correct Monty Hall answer draw a storm of objections?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** When Marilyn vos Savant gave the correct answer to the Monty Hall problem in 1990, some ten thousand readers wrote in, close to a thousand of them with doctorates, insisting she was wrong.

The story is told as a parable of expert overconfidence. A columnist got a probability puzzle right, and thousands of readers, many with advanced degrees, told her she had got it wrong. It sounds too neat to be true.

The puzzle is named for Monty Hall, host of the game show *Let's Make a Deal*. A contestant picks one of three doors, one of which hides a car. The host, who knows where the car is, opens another door to show a goat and offers a switch. Switching wins two times in three. The first pick is right one time in three, and that does not change when a door is opened. Because the host can never open the contestant's door or the car's, whenever the first pick was wrong he is forced to leave the car behind the other closed door.

The problem was first posed by the biostatistician Steve Selvin in a 1975 letter to *The American Statistician*. It became famous through vos Savant's 1990 "Ask Marilyn" column and the storm of objections that followed. She offered an intuition pump: with a hundred doors, a contestant picks one and the host opens ninety-eight goats, leaving two doors, one of which holds almost all the probability. The puzzle fooled professional mathematicians too. Paul Erdős reportedly stayed unconvinced until he was shown a simulation.

Two things fed the objections. Intuition says that two closed doors must be fifty-fifty, and intuition misjudges conditional probability badly. The original wording also did not pin down the host's rules. The two-thirds answer holds only if the host always opens a goat door, never the contestant's, always offers the switch, and chooses at random between goats when the first pick is the car. If he opens a door at random and it happens to show a goat, switching wins only half the time. The record says this ambiguity is much of why the dispute raged.

The storm of letters is on record, and so is the answer. Under the standard host rules, vos Savant was right: switching wins two-thirds of the time.

**Tell someone:** By her own estimate, some ten thousand readers, close to a thousand with doctorates, wrote in to tell Marilyn vos Savant she was wrong, and she was right.

*Sources:* Selvin, letters to *The American Statistician* (1975). Tierney, "Behind Monty Hall's Doors: Puzzle, Debate and Answer?" *The New York Times* (1991). "The Time Everyone 'Corrected' the World's Smartest Woman," Priceonomics. "Monty Hall problem," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-monty-hall-problem/

## Did Howard Gardner propose "learning styles"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Howard Gardner's 1983 book *Frames of Mind* proposed multiple intelligences, not learning styles. The record calls learning styles a distinct and largely debunked idea that Gardner himself rejects, and in 2013 he wrote that the two should not be confused.

The popular version runs the two together: Gardner showed that some children are visual learners and others auditory, and good teaching matches each lesson to each child's style.

The record describes a different claim. Gardner, an American developmental psychologist, argued that intelligence is not one general ability captured by an IQ score but several relatively independent ones. He first named seven: linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, spatial, interpersonal and intrapersonal. He later added an eighth, naturalistic, and has discussed a possible ninth, existential, without adopting it. He reached them not by analyzing test scores but by pulling together evidence from prodigies and savants, from brain injuries that spare some abilities and damage others, and from the skills different cultures value.

An intelligence, in this account, is an ability a person has in some measure. A learning style is a claim about how a learner prefers to take material in. In a 2013 *Washington Post* piece, "'Multiple intelligences' are not 'learning styles'," Gardner said he was troubled that people credited him with the notion, and noted that the term was already in use before his theory.

The confusion spread through classrooms. The theory became widely popular in education, where it encouraged teachers to present material in several ways, which sounds much like teaching to styles. "Musical intelligence" and "auditory learner" also sound like neighbors. A famous name then lends a classroom habit the look of research. The record adds a caution about Gardner's own theory: intelligence researchers criticize it for lacking validating evidence, since scores on different mental tests consistently correlate, pointing to the general factor the theory downplays.

So Gardner's name belongs to multiple intelligences, a contested theory from 1983. Learning styles is a separate idea, and he is on record rejecting the match.

**Tell someone:** Howard Gardner's 1983 theory is multiple intelligences, not learning styles, and in 2013 he wrote that the two are not the same.

## Did Maxwell write the four Maxwell's equations?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** James Clerk Maxwell built the theory, but the four compact equations that carry his name are Oliver Heaviside's reformulation of 1884. Maxwell's own 1865 paper contained twenty equations.

The popular version holds that Maxwell wrote down the four equations taught today, in the vector form printed in textbooks.

The record separates the physics from the notation. Maxwell published his field theory in 1861–1862 and consolidated it in his 1865 paper, "A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field." He drew on Coulomb's, Ampère's and Faraday's laws and added a term of his own, the "displacement current," to make the system self-consistent. Combining the equations yields a wave equation whose calculated speed matched the known speed of light. That led Maxwell to conclude that light is an electromagnetic wave. The result is his.

The form is not. The 1865 paper wrote every directed quantity as three Cartesian components and, by Maxwell's own count, set out twenty equations in twenty unknowns; quaternion forms came later, in his 1873 *Treatise*. In 1884 Heaviside condensed them into the four vector equations taught today, publishing them in *The Electrician* from 1885, with related work by Josiah Willard Gibbs and Heinrich Hertz. Two of the four, the Gauss's laws, relate electric field lines to charge and record the absence of magnetic monopoles. Faraday's law says a changing magnetic field creates an electric field. The Ampère–Maxwell law says a changing electric field, together with current, creates a magnetic field.

The misattribution follows the name. The equations are called Maxwell's, so a reader assumes the page in the textbook is the page he wrote. The compact version is also the one that gets reproduced, and it hides the twenty-equation original behind it. A label that credits the physicist for the physics has come to be read as crediting him for the notation too.

Maxwell's claim to the theory is secure, and the earliest version of it is his own, from 1861–1865. The four equations in their familiar form date from Heaviside in 1884, nineteen years after Maxwell's twenty.

**Tell someone:** Maxwell built the theory, but his 1865 paper had twenty equations; Oliver Heaviside condensed them into the famous four in 1884.

*Sources:* Maxwell, "A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field," *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society* 155 (1865), Part III. "Maxwell's equations," Wikipedia. "History of Maxwell's equations," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/maxwells-equations/

## Did Murray Gell-Mann come up with Gell-Mann amnesia?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The novelist Michael Crichton coined and named the effect in a 2002 talk, after once discussing it with the physicist Murray Gell-Mann. Gell-Mann did not devise it, and by Crichton's own account the famous name was there to make the idea sound more important.

The popular version takes the name at face value: a physicist noticed the effect, and it was named for him.

The record points to one talk. In 2002 Crichton addressed the International Leadership Forum in La Jolla, in a talk titled "Why Speculate?" In it he described the habit the effect names. A reader spots a newspaper's errors on a subject they know, even cause and effect reversed, then turns the page and trusts the same paper on subjects they do not. Crichton named this after Gell-Mann tongue in cheek. He said he used the name because he had once discussed the effect with Gell-Mann, and that dropping a famous name lent himself and the effect more importance than they would otherwise have.

The name did the job it was given. A scientist's name makes an observation sound like a finding, and the record is clear that this one is not a finding. It is an anecdote from a persuasive talk, not a measured effect, and it does not appear in the peer-reviewed cognitive-bias literature as a validated construct. The record calls the name a deliberate joke by its coiner. A close cousin, Knoll's law of media accuracy, attributed to the editor Erwin Knoll, makes much the same point with no borrowed name: everything in the papers is true except the rare story the reader knows firsthand.

The observation has a logical gap that the record also notes. Different beats have different reporters and standards, so errors in one section do not necessarily predict errors in another. Pushed too far, the idea turns into blanket cynicism about all media, which is its own unwarranted leap. What it asks for is calibration: an error spotted in a known field should count as evidence about the source.

So the effect is Crichton's, from a single talk in 2002, and the name is a borrowing he admitted to. Gell-Mann's part, on Crichton's own account, was one conversation.

**Tell someone:** The novelist Michael Crichton coined Gell-Mann amnesia in a 2002 talk and admitted he borrowed the physicist's name, after one conversation with him, to make it sound more important.

*Sources:* Crichton, "Why Speculate?," talk to the International Leadership Forum, La Jolla (April 26, 2002), reproduced at Ritholtz, *The Big Picture*, and at gwern.net. "Gell-Mann amnesia effect," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/gell-mann-amnesia-effect/

## Did Thomas Gresham formulate Gresham's law?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Thomas Gresham did not formulate Gresham's law. Henry Dunning Macleod named it after him in 1858, and the principle had been stated long before: by Oresme, by Copernicus around 1519, and in Aristophanes' play *The Frogs* around 405 BC.

The popular version credits Gresham, a Tudor financier, with the observation that bad money drives out good.

The record gives Gresham a smaller part. Gresham (1519–1579) was a financier to the English Crown. After Henry VIII and Edward VI debased England's coinage, full-weight silver coins were hoarded, melted or shipped abroad while the debased coins circulated. Writing to Elizabeth I at her accession in 1558, Gresham blamed the debasement for carrying the realm's fine gold abroad. He reported a case; he never set it out as a general law. The name came three centuries later. Macleod attached it in *The Elements of Political Economy* (1858). The idea itself was far older. Copernicus set it out around 1519, which is why it is also called the Gresham–Copernicus law, and Oresme, around 1357, and Aristophanes had stated it earlier still.

The law is narrower than its slogan. It holds when a legal-tender rule or a fixed rate forces two monies to trade at the same face value. A rational holder then pays debts with the coin whose metal is worth less and keeps the better one. The economist Robert Mundell made the condition explicit: bad money drives out good only if they exchange for the same price. When the United States took silver out of dimes and quarters in 1965, the older 90%-silver coins were hoarded and vanished from circulation.

The misattribution spread the way eponyms do. Macleod gave the principle a name, and a name is easier to repeat than a list of predecessors. Gresham was also a fitting example: he lived through a debasement and reported what it did, which made the credit plausible on its face. The record calls the law a textbook case of Stigler's law of eponymy, the rule that discoveries are seldom named after their original discoverers.

So the earliest statement in the record is Aristophanes, around 405 BC. Gresham observed the effect, and Macleod put his name on it in 1858.

**Tell someone:** Gresham did not formulate Gresham's law; Macleod named it after him in 1858, and Aristophanes stated the principle around 405 BC.

*Sources:* Macleod, *The Elements of Political Economy* (1858). Selgin, "Gresham's Law," EH.Net Encyclopedia. "Gresham's law," Wikipedia. "Coinage Act of 1965," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/greshams-law/

## Was Hubble the first to find Hubble's law?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Georges Lemaître derived the relation between a galaxy's distance and its speed of recession, and estimated its constant, in 1927. Edwin Hubble's paper came two years later, in 1929. In 2018 the International Astronomical Union recommended renaming the law the Hubble–Lemaître law.

The popular version credits Hubble alone with discovering, in 1929, that the universe is expanding.

The record gives the two men different parts. Hubble published the relation observationally in 1929. The law states that the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it recedes, so a galaxy twice as far away recedes twice as fast. Its direct observational form is redshift: a galaxy's spectral lines shift toward the red by an amount proportional to its distance. Lemaître reached the same relation from general relativity, and estimated its constant, two years before Hubble published. The IAU's 2018 recommendation acknowledges that priority.

The record also corrects the picture of what is moving. The recession is not galaxies flying through space but the metric expansion of space itself, which stretches the distance between galaxies and stretches their light to longer, redder wavelengths. The Hubble constant is the current rate of that expansion. Nearby, local gravity wins: the Andromeda galaxy is approaching the Milky Way.

The single-name version spread because a name, once attached, repeats itself. Until the 2018 recommendation the law carried Hubble's name alone, and each retelling passed the credit on. The record files the case under a familiar pattern: the law is not named for its first discoverer. A theoretical derivation published first and an observational paper published second can both lay claim to a result, and here the name went to the second.

So the relation was first derived, with an estimate of its constant, by Lemaître in 1927. Hubble's observational paper followed in 1929, and since 2018 the IAU's recommended name carries both men.

**Tell someone:** Georges Lemaître derived Hubble's law in 1927, two years before Hubble's paper, and since 2018 the IAU recommends calling it the Hubble–Lemaître law.

*Sources:* Lemaître, "Un univers homogène de masse constante et de rayon croissant…," *Annales de la Société Scientifique de Bruxelles* (1927). IAU, "IAU members vote to recommend renaming the Hubble law as the Hubble–Lemaître law" (2018). "Hubble's law," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/hubbles-law/

## Did Arthur Laffer sketch the Laffer curve on a napkin?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** First-hand accounts of the napkin evening exist, but they disagree, Arthur Laffer says he does not remember the details, and the napkin now at the Smithsonian has disputed provenance. The firm dated anchor is a 1978 article by the journalist Jude Wanniski, who named and popularized the idea.

The popular version is set in a restaurant in 1974. Laffer, arguing against a tax increase, sketches a curve on a napkin for Dick Cheney, Donald Rumsfeld and Wanniski.

The accounts that do exist disagree. Wanniski, who arranged the meeting, told the story. Grace-Marie Turner, then a young aide who says she sat in, recalls a paper cocktail napkin and doubts Rumsfeld was at the table. Laffer has written that he does not remember the details. A cloth napkin inscribed to Rumsfeld, found among Wanniski's papers, is at the Smithsonian, but Turner judges it a later replica. What the record does date is the name: the curve was popularized under Laffer's name by Wanniski in a 1978 article. Laffer, for his part, disclaimed originality for the idea itself.

The story spread because the curve really is simple enough to draw on a napkin. Tax revenue is zero at a rate of zero, since nothing is taxed. It is zero again at a rate of 100%, since no one bothers to earn taxable income. Somewhere between the two lies a rate that raises the most. Two points on the baseline and a hump between them can be drawn in seconds. A story in which a policy argument fits on a napkin, sketched for powerful listeners, has the shape that travels: a small object, a famous room and a big consequence. The curve later became the intellectual centerpiece of 1980s supply-side policy, which gave its origin story a large audience.

The record is careful about what even the drawing shows. The curve says a revenue-maximizing peak exists, not where it is, nor which side of it any economy sits on. A napkin can carry the geometry but not the evidence about where the peak lies.

So the napkin evening rests on accounts that disagree, and the man at the center of it does not remember the details. The earliest dated source in the record is Wanniski's 1978 article, which put Laffer's name on the curve.

**Tell someone:** Eyewitness accounts of the Laffer napkin disagree, Arthur Laffer says he does not remember the details, and the firm dated anchor is Jude Wanniski's 1978 article.

## Does Laffer himself say the Laffer curve wasn't his idea?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Arthur Laffer has disclaimed originality for the curve that bears his name. He credits earlier sources, including Ibn Khaldun in the fourteenth century and John Maynard Keynes.

The popular version makes Laffer the inventor of the idea that cutting taxes can raise revenue.

The record says otherwise on both counts. The curve is named after Laffer and was popularized under that name by the journalist Jude Wanniski in a 1978 article. Laffer himself points to precedents, among them Ibn Khaldun six centuries earlier and Keynes. The idea is an inverted U. Revenue is zero at a tax rate of zero and zero again at 100%, so some rate between them raises the most. The engine is how people respond. As the marginal rate rises, they shift away from taxed work, investment and reported income toward leisure, avoidance, evasion or relocation, and the tax base shrinks. How strongly reported income reacts to the after-tax rate, the elasticity of taxable income, sets the curve's shape.

The popular version also runs two claims together. That a peak exists is, in the record's words, nearly uncontroversial. Where it lies, and which side of it an economy is on, is fiercely contested. Most economists place developed economies on the upward side, where cuts typically lose revenue. In a 2012 survey of leading economists by the IGM panel, none agreed that a US income-tax cut would raise revenue within five years. George H. W. Bush called the claim that cuts pay for themselves "voodoo economics" in 1980.

The single-inventor story spread with the policy. The curve was the intellectual centerpiece of 1980s supply-side policy, used to justify cutting the top US income-tax rate from 70% to 28%. A name attached to a famous policy argument travels with it, while the disclaimers and precedents do not.

So the curve carries Laffer's name through Wanniski, and Laffer himself credits Ibn Khaldun and Keynes with the idea. The geometry is sound. The claim that cuts raise revenue in today's rich economies is the part the evidence disputes.

**Tell someone:** Arthur Laffer has disclaimed originality for the Laffer curve, crediting earlier sources including Ibn Khaldun in the fourteenth century and John Maynard Keynes.

## Was the Overton window named only after Overton's death?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Joseph P. Overton developed the idea in the mid-1990s, but it was his colleague Joseph Lehman who named and elaborated it, after Overton died in a 2003 ultralight-aircraft crash.

The popular version assumes that Overton coined "the Overton window" himself.

The record separates the idea from the name. Overton was a senior vice-president at the Mackinac Center for Public Policy, a free-market think tank, and developed the model there in the mid-1990s. After his death in 2003, Lehman named the concept and elaborated it. The term then reached a wider public through Glenn Beck's 2010 novel *The Overton Window*.

The idea is a range. On any issue, only some positions can be advocated by mainstream politicians without being dismissed as too extreme. Positions can be ranked from unthinkable through radical, acceptable, sensible and popular to settled policy. The window is the band from acceptable to popular, and it shifts over time. It is a qualitative model, not a measured law, and there is no agreed metric for where the window sits. The Mackinac Center illustrates it with education policy, from no government involvement at one end to government providing all education at the other.

The assumption that Overton named it comes from the name itself. An eponym looks like a signature, and a concept that reached many readers through a novel bearing the name, years after the naming, carried no note about who chose it. The popular telling changed the idea as well as the credit. A dramatic version, in which activists deliberately propose extremes to drag the window, is contested and arguably overstated. Lehman, the concept's steward, emphasizes that lawmakers mainly detect and follow the window. The Mackinac Center attributes most shifts to gradual change in society's values rather than to manipulation.

So the model is Overton's, from the mid-1990s, and the name is Lehman's tribute, given after Overton's death in 2003.

**Tell someone:** Joseph Overton developed the idea, but his colleague Joseph Lehman named it the Overton window only after Overton died in 2003.

## Is the Baader–Meinhof phenomenon named after a militant group?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The nickname comes from the West German militant group. It traces to a 1994 reader submission to the *St. Paul Pioneer Press* from a writer who had heard the group's name twice in quick succession.

The popular assumption is understandable. Two surnames joined by a dash have the look of a scientific eponym, and it is easy to suppose that two researchers stand behind the effect.

The record shows a newspaper reader instead. The phenomenon is the experience of noticing something new, a word, a car model, an unusual name, and then seeming to meet it everywhere. The 1994 submission to the *Pioneer Press* gave that experience its nickname, after the writer's own run-in with the name of the militant group. The scholarly term came later. The Stanford linguist Arnold Zwicky coined "frequency illusion" in an August 2005 post on the blog Language Log, and the *Oxford English Dictionary* added the term in its March 2019 update. Zwicky also coined a sibling, the "recency illusion": the belief that something only recently noticed is itself new.

The mechanism, as the record gives it, has two parts. Selective attention means that once something is flagged as relevant, the brain filters for it and registers instances it used to ignore. Confirmation bias means each new sighting is taken as evidence that the thing has become more common, while the absences go uncounted. The base rate never moves.

The odd name stuck for a reason the record makes plain: its origin story is itself an example. A reader heard an unusual name twice in quick succession and took it for a pattern. That is the effect in miniature, and it makes a better story than a dry technical label. The record adds a caution. The construct comes from linguistics blogging and lexicography rather than a controlled experiment, and much of the evidence is anecdotal.

So the nickname does come from the militant group, by way of a 1994 newspaper reader. The scholarly name, "frequency illusion," dates from Zwicky in 2005.

**Tell someone:** The Baader–Meinhof phenomenon is named after the West German militant group, by a 1994 newspaper reader who heard its name twice in quick succession.

*Sources:* "Frequency illusion," Wikipedia. Zimmer, "'Frequency illusion' in the OED," Language Log. "What's the Baader-Meinhof Phenomenon?," HowStuffWorks. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/baader-meinhof-phenomenon/

## Is "Muphry's law" misspelled on purpose?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The misspelling is deliberate. John Bangsund, of the Society of Editors (Victoria) in Australia, coined Muphry's law in the society's March 1992 newsletter as "the editorial application of the better-known Murphy's law."

The popular version takes the name for a slip: someone misspelled Murphy's law and the error stuck.

The record shows a joke built into the title. The law states that any criticism of someone's writing will itself contain an error, and the name "Muphry" demonstrates the point in its own spelling. Bangsund's fuller statement adds corollaries. A book that thanks someone for proofreading will contain mistakes. The stronger the criticism, the greater the fault. Any book on editing will be internally inconsistent. An internet-native twin, Skitt's law, says that any post correcting an error in another post will itself contain at least one error.

The record gives two reported cases. In 2008 the author Stephen Dubner suggested that *The Economist* had printed "pasties" in error for "pastries." "Pasties" was correct, readers invoked Muphry's law, and the magazine reportedly sent him a Cornish pasty. In 2009 *The Sun* attacked Prime Minister Gordon Brown for misspelling a soldier's surname in a handwritten condolence letter; Brown blamed his handwriting. Days later the paper misspelled the same surname on its own website and had to apologize.

The belief that the name is a typo spreads for the same reason the joke works. The name only lands if it looks like a mistake, so a reader who sees "Muphry" and corrects it has acted out the law. The law's own durability has a plainer cause, which the record names: memorable confirming cases get shared, while the many error-free corrections go unremarked. It is a humorous folk adage, not a measured tendency, and its charm is that it proves itself.

So the spelling is no accident. Bangsund chose it in 1992 so that the law's name would carry its own error.

**Tell someone:** Muphry's law is misspelled on purpose: John Bangsund coined it in 1992 so the law's name would carry its own error.

## Do Eskimo languages have far more words for snow than English?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The answer depends on what counts as a word. By roots, Eskimo languages have roughly as many snow words as English; by whole words, specialist surveys find far more. The inflated figures of fifty and a hundred are myth.

The popular version holds that Eskimo languages have a great many words for snow, and offers this as proof that the language people speak shapes what they see.

Franz Boas listed four snow words in 1911 with no count attached. Benjamin Lee Whorf's 1940 popular article made the contrast explicit, setting English's one all-inclusive snow word against several Eskimo ones. Later writers inflated the number, to fifty by 1978 and a hundred in a 1984 *New York Times* editorial. The linguist Geoffrey Pullum's 1989 essay "The Great Eskimo Vocabulary Hoax," building on Laura Martin's 1986 study, attacked those numbers and found the root count roughly comparable to English.

The question stays open because the answer turns on what is counted. Pullum's root count stands, but surveys led by Igor Krupnik count about 40 terms in Central Siberian Yupik and at least 53 in Nunavik Inuktitut, and a 2010 study supports more frozen-water roots than English.

The hypothesis the story was meant to support is real, and more modest. Linguistic relativity is associated with Edward Sapir and Whorf in the early twentieth century, though they never co-authored it or called it a hypothesis; the label came later, commonly credited to Harry Hoijer. It comes in two versions. The strong one, that language determines and limits thought, is rejected by mainstream cognitive science. The weak one, that language influences habitual thought, has some support. Russian has separate basic words for lighter and darker blue, and Russian speakers tell blues apart across that boundary faster than English speakers. The advantage vanishes under verbal, but not spatial, interference. Speakers of Guugu Yimithirr, which uses compass directions rather than left and right, keep constant track of orientation. Effect sizes are modest, and some findings, such as those on grammatical gender, have failed to replicate.

The snow story spread because it does in one image what the real evidence does slowly. A people surrounded by snow, with words to match, is easy to picture and easy to retell. Pinning it on Whorf, whose name was already on the idea, made an anecdote look like scholarship. Each retelling then borrowed authority from the last.

So the counts of fifty and a hundred are myth, and the story began with Boas, not Whorf. Whether the real total is far above English depends on counting roots or words, and specialists still argue it. The firmer evidence for the weak hypothesis concerns blues and compass points.

**Tell someone:** The fifty or a hundred Eskimo snow words are myth, but specialists count about 40 snow terms in Central Siberian Yupik and at least 53 in Nunavik Inuktitut, so "far more than English" is still argued over.

## Does the Coriolis effect decide which way a sink drains?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** At the scale of a sink, bath or toilet, the Coriolis effect is thousands of times too weak to set the direction of draining. Water drains both ways within the same hemisphere.

The popular version says the Earth's rotation makes water swirl down drains one way in the Northern Hemisphere and the other way in the Southern.

The record shows a real effect at the wrong scale. The Coriolis effect is the apparent deflection of a moving object when its motion is described from a rotating frame such as the turning Earth. Freely moving air or water appears to curve to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern. It is not a real force pushing the object but an inertial effect of viewing motion from a spinning frame. Its sideways push is proportional to the object's speed, and its horizontal strength scales with the sine of the latitude: zero at the equator, greatest at the poles. Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis gave the mathematical expression in an 1835 paper on rotating machines, and the effect entered meteorology in the early twentieth century.

Over long distances and long times, that weak nudge adds up. It makes large low-pressure systems rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern. It helps drive the great ocean gyres, and long-range shells travel far enough that gunners must correct for it. A basin of water does none of this. Its draining is set by the basin's shape, the angle of the jet that filled it and whatever motion is left in the water. Only under extreme laboratory control, with still, symmetric tanks left for hours, has a Coriolis signature in draining water been measured.

The myth spread because it borrows real physics. Storms do turn opposite ways in the two hemispheres, and a drain vortex looks like a small storm. The step from one to the other is natural and wrong, because the effect only builds up over large scales and long times.

So the effect is real and shapes the weather. In a sink it is swamped, and water drains both ways in either hemisphere.

**Tell someone:** The Coriolis effect is thousands of times too weak to set a sink's draining direction, so water drains both ways in either hemisphere.

*Sources:* "Coriolis force," Wikipedia. "Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis," *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. Shapiro, "Bath-Tub Vortex," *Nature* 196 (1962). Trefethen et al., "The Bath-Tub Vortex in the Southern Hemisphere," *Nature* 207 (1965). Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/coriolis-effect/

## Is the uncertainty principle just about measuring disturbing things?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The uncertainty principle is not merely the idea that measuring a particle disturbs it. It says a quantum state cannot have sharply defined values of both position and momentum at once, and it holds even for perfect instruments.

The popular version runs like this: a particle's position and speed cannot both be known, because looking at it knocks it off course.

The record describes something deeper than clumsy measurement. Certain pairs of quantities, called conjugate variables, cannot both be defined to arbitrary precision at the same time. For position and momentum, the spread in position times the spread in momentum is at least the reduced Planck constant divided by two. This is a feature of quantum states themselves, which do not possess sharp values of both quantities at once. No state that breaks the bound can even be prepared.

The reason is the wave nature of matter. A quantum state's description in position and its description in momentum are Fourier transforms of each other, and a function sharply peaked in one is necessarily spread out in the other. Pin a particle down in space and its momentum fans out. A wave passing through a narrow slit shows the trade: narrow the slit and the position sharpens while the spread beyond it widens. The bound applies only to such pairs. It does not say everything is uncertain.

The disturbance reading spread partly through the principle's own history. Heisenberg introduced it in a 1927 paper in *Zeitschrift für Physik* with a thought experiment about a gamma-ray microscope, a picture of looking at a particle, and gave only an order-of-magnitude relation. Earle Kennard supplied the rigorous inequality the same year, and Hermann Weyl also contributed to it. The record notes a separate relation describing how a measurement disturbs a system; conflating the two has caused lasting confusion. A picture of looking is easier to hold than a Fourier transform.

So the principle is about the states, not the instruments. The precise bound, half the reduced Planck constant, comes from Kennard's 1927 inequality, not from Heisenberg's microscope.

**Tell someone:** The uncertainty principle holds even for perfect instruments, because a quantum state cannot have sharply defined values of both position and momentum at once.

*Sources:* Heisenberg, *Zeitschrift für Physik* 43 (1927). "The Uncertainty Principle," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. "Uncertainty principle," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/heisenbergs-uncertainty-principle/

## Was Schrödinger's cat meant to make a quantum idea look absurd?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Erwin Schrödinger devised the cat in 1935 as an objection to the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics. His aim was to show the absurdity of extending quantum superposition to a large object, not to claim that a cat can be alive and dead at once.

The popular version turns the argument around: Schrödinger proposed that a cat in a sealed box really is both alive and dead until someone looks.

The record describes a reductio ad absurdum. A cat is sealed in a box with a radioactive atom, a Geiger counter, a hammer and a flask of poison. If the atom decays, the counter trips the hammer, the flask shatters and the cat dies. Radioactive decay is a quantum event, and until it is measured the atom is in a superposition of decayed and not decayed. A naive reading of the formalism therefore puts the cat in a superposition of alive and dead until the box is opened. Schrödinger found that conclusion absurd for a cat, and used it to press the measurement problem: when and how the wavefunction collapses, and what counts as an observer.

The puzzle is still open, and interpretations answer it in incompatible ways. The Copenhagen view holds that measurement yields a single outcome. In the many-worlds interpretation the cat is alive in one branch and dead in another. Decoherence, a physical process rather than an interpretation, adds that contact with the environment suppresses interference between alive and dead almost instantly, so no such superposition can be observed. On its own it does not say why one outcome occurs. On none of these does collapse require a conscious observer.

The reversed reading spread because the image outlived the argument. A cat both alive and dead is a picture that sticks after one hearing. The objection attached to it takes a paragraph, and it gets dropped in the retelling. Popular versions then added a mystical observer whose mind settles the cat's fate, which the record calls a popular distortion of the physics.

So the cat was a critique. Schrödinger built it in 1935 to make an idea look absurd, not to claim that cats live double lives.

**Tell someone:** Schrödinger's cat was a critique: he devised it in 1935 to show that extending quantum superposition to a large object is absurd.

*Sources:* Schrödinger, "Die gegenwärtige Situation in der Quantenmechanik," *Naturwissenschaften* 23 (1935). "Schrödinger's cat," Wikipedia. "Schrödinger's cat," *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/schrodingers-cat/

## Did vortex shedding bring down the Tacoma Narrows Bridge?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The 1940 collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge is more accurately explained by aeroelastic flutter, a distinct, self-excited mechanism. The record says the case should not be presented as simple vortex-street resonance.

The popular version attributes the collapse purely to vortex shedding: wind peeling eddies off the bridge in a steady rhythm until the structure shook itself apart.

The record keeps the mechanism and moves the example. Vortex shedding is real. When a fluid flows past a blunt body and the Reynolds number that characterizes the flow passes a critical value, about 47 for a circular cylinder, the wake turns unstable. Vortices roll up and break away first from one side and then from the other, never both at once, and trail downstream in a staggered double row called a Kármán vortex street. The alternating shedding pushes the body one way and then the other. The rhythm is set by the Strouhal number, which links the shedding frequency, the size of the body and the speed of the flow. If that frequency matches a natural frequency of the structure, the push can drive resonance and damaging vibration. That is why some chimneys, masts and antennas are fitted with helical strakes or dampers, and why wires hum in the wind. Theodore von Kármán analyzed the pattern, building on earlier observations by Mallock and Bénard.

Tacoma Narrows, on the record's account, belongs to a different instability. It names that mechanism as aeroelastic flutter and calls it distinct and self-excited. In flutter the wind feeds energy into the deck's own twisting faster than the structure can damp it, so the motion grows with no need for a matching rhythm in the wind. The numbers fit flutter, not vortices: the destructive twisting ran at about 0.2 hertz, while vortex shedding at that wind speed would have been near 1 hertz.

The vortex version spread because it is a true mechanism attached to the wrong case. Vortex shedding does set structures shaking, and a famous collapse in the wind makes a tempting illustration. Both stories feature wind and oscillation, which makes the substitution easy to miss and easy to repeat.

So vortex shedding is real and can wreck a chimney. The famous bridge failure of 1940 is better explained by flutter.

**Tell someone:** Vortex shedding can wreck a chimney, but the 1940 Tacoma Narrows collapse is more accurately explained by aeroelastic flutter, a distinct, self-excited mechanism.

## Does air over a wing speed up to meet the air underneath?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Air does move faster over a wing's upper surface, and the pressure there is lower. But the popular reason, that the air must hurry to rejoin the air beneath, is a misconception that NASA debunks.

The popular version is the "equal transit time" story. The upper surface of a wing is longer, so air passing over it must speed up to meet the air passing underneath at the back of the wing. Faster air means lower pressure, and the wing is lifted.

The record keeps the conclusion and rejects the reason. NASA's Glenn Research Center explicitly debunks the equal-transit-time account. Symmetric wings and inverted flight both contradict it, and it predicts far too little lift. The record does not say why the upper air speeds up; it says only that this popular justification is wrong and that Bernoulli's principle does contribute to lift.

The principle itself is sound. Daniel Bernoulli set it out in *Hydrodynamica* in 1738, from experiments with liquids, and Leonhard Euler later derived the equation in its usual form. Along a streamline, faster flow means lower pressure. It is an energy budget: the kinetic energy, pressure energy and gravitational potential energy per unit volume add to a constant, so more speed leaves less pressure. Seen through Newton's second law, it is a pressure difference that accelerates a parcel of fluid, so the faster region sits at lower pressure. The standard form holds for steady, incompressible flow without viscosity. A narrowing pipe shows it plainly: the fluid speeds up through the constriction and its pressure falls, the Venturi effect.

The wrong story spread because it ends at the right answer. Faster air and lower pressure are both true, and equal transit time supplies a tidy reason for them. An explanation that reaches the correct result by a wrong route is hard to catch, because every check of the result seems to confirm it.

So Bernoulli's principle stands, and the air over a wing is faster. The idea that it races to meet the air below is the misconception.

**Tell someone:** NASA debunks the equal-transit-time story, yet air over a wing does move faster and at lower pressure, and Bernoulli's principle does contribute to lift.

*Sources:* NASA Glenn Research Center (Tom Benson), "Incorrect Lift Theory." "Bernoulli's principle," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/bernoullis-principle/

## Does hot water freeze faster than cold?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** Whether hot water can freeze faster than cold is unresolved. Erasto Mpemba and Denis Osborne published the claim in 1969, a careful 2016 study by Burridge and Linden found no evidence for a meaningful effect, and the record says the dispute remains open.

The popular version is simple: hot water freezes first, and the effect carries Mpemba's name.

The record tells a longer and less settled story. In 1963 Mpemba, a Tanzanian student, noticed that a hot sugared-milk mix for ice cream froze faster than a cooler one. He later published with the physicist Denis Osborne, in a 1969 paper titled "Cool?" in *Physics Education*. The idea is far older. Aristotle, Francis Bacon and Descartes each remarked that previously heated water can freeze quickly, though their observations are anecdotal. So Mpemba did not discover the idea first.

The modern evidence cuts against a robust effect. Burridge and Linden's 2016 study in *Scientific Reports* timed how long samples took to cool to 0 °C, not to freeze solid, and found no evidence for a meaningful effect. Misplacing a thermometer by as little as a centimeter could produce an apparent one. There is no agreed definition of what freezing faster means, and no accepted mechanism. The candidates include evaporation, which leaves less hot water to freeze; differences in convection; dissolved gases driven out by heating; differences in supercooling; and a hot container melting frost and improving thermal contact. Each is contested, and some may be artifacts. Some narrow studies report reproducing something under specific conditions, which is why the record stops short of calling it debunked.

The claim persists because it is counterintuitive, and a surprise is easy to remember and fun to repeat. It also has a memorable origin, a schoolboy and an ice-cream mix. And it is easy to get a result either way at home, because the outcome depends on the container, the volume and exactly what counts as frozen.

So it is not an established fact that hot water freezes faster, and it is not fully debunked. The careful test of 2016 found nothing meaningful, and the question remains open.

**Tell someone:** A careful 2016 study found no evidence for a meaningful Mpemba effect, but whether hot water can freeze faster remains unresolved.

*Sources:* Mpemba & Osborne, "Cool?," *Physics Education* 4 (1969). Burridge & Linden, "Questioning the Mpemba effect," *Scientific Reports* 6 (2016). "Mpemba effect," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/mpemba-effect/

## Does a broken bone heal back stronger?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** There is no evidence, contrary to popular belief, that a healed fracture becomes stronger than the original unbroken bone. The claim is an overstatement of Wolff's law, which describes how healthy bone adapts to the loads placed on it.

The popular version holds that a bone knits back stronger at the break than it was before.

The record finds no support for that and traces where the idea comes from. Wolff's law is frequently overstated as implying that broken bones heal back stronger. What the law actually says is narrower. The German anatomist and surgeon Julius Wolff set it out in his 1892 book *Das Gesetz der Transformation der Knochen* (The Law of Bone Transformation). Healthy bone adapts its structure to the loads it carries, growing stronger under more stress and weaker under less.

The mechanism is living tissue at work. Cells embedded in bone, called osteocytes, sense strain, largely through fluid flowing in tiny channels. They signal the cells that build bone and the cells that remove it. The internal lattice of the bone is reshaped first, and the outer wall can then thicken or thin. The record gives three cases. Racquet-sport players develop measurably denser bone in the arm that holds the racquet. After a hip replacement, the metal implant carries load the femur used to bear, and studies have recorded falls of roughly 10 to 40% in bone density around the upper femur in the first two years. Astronauts on long missions lose bone density because microgravity removes the loading signal.

Wolff's own strict mathematical formulation has been discredited in its precise form, and researchers now prefer the looser term "bone functional adaptation."

The myth spreads because the true part makes the false part sound like a corollary. Bone does get stronger under stress, and a break looks like the greatest stress of all, so stronger healing feels like the same rule applied once more. The step is an inference the evidence does not support.

So bone does adapt to load. A healed fracture becoming stronger than the original bone is not something the evidence shows.

**Tell someone:** No evidence shows a healed fracture becomes stronger than the original bone; healthy bone instead adapts to the loads it carries.

## Did Thomas Young shine light through two slits?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** Thomas Young showed that light interferes, in his Bakerian Lectures around 1801 and 1803. Whether he performed a literal two-slit experiment is disputed: his demonstrations used sunlight, pinholes and narrow cards.

The popular version has Young shining light through two slits around 1801, seeing bands on a screen and proving that light is a wave.

The record keeps the result and questions the apparatus. Young argued for the wave nature of light by showing that light passing two closely spaced openings produces interference fringes. His interference work is best dated to the Bakerian Lectures of around 1801 and 1803. But the tidy two-slit setup of the textbooks is partly a later idealization, since his actual demonstrations used sunlight, pinholes and narrow cards.

The physics is the same in either form. Light passing two narrow, closely spaced openings spreads out from each, and the two waves overlap. Where crest meets crest the light is bright; where crest meets trough it cancels. The result is a row of evenly spaced fringes whose spacing depends on the wavelength and the geometry. Those fringes are the hallmark of waves.

The experiment's later fame came from its quantum version. When electrons or photons are sent through one at a time, each lands as a single dot, yet the dots build up the same fringes, as Tonomura's 1989 experiment filmed with electrons. If a detector records which slit each particle takes, the fringes disappear. That is a physical effect of the detector, not of a conscious mind watching. Richard Feynman called the phenomenon the only mystery at the heart of quantum mechanics.

The neat two-slit picture of Young spread because the quantum version made the apparatus famous, and the history was drawn to match. A clean diagram with two slits is easier to teach than pinholes and cards, so the textbook setup was projected back onto its founder.

So Young's interference work around 1801 and 1803 is secure. Whether it used two slits as pictured is not, and the record says he worked with pinholes and cards.

**Tell someone:** Thomas Young showed light interferes, but whether he used two literal slits is disputed; his demonstrations used sunlight, pinholes and narrow cards.

## Was the Doppler effect tested with trumpeters on a train?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** C. H. D. Buys Ballot tested the Doppler effect for sound in 1845, using trumpeters on a moving train. Christian Doppler had predicted the effect three years earlier, in an 1842 paper in Prague.

The popular assumption is that the trumpeters belong in a textbook, not a railway timetable: the scene sounds like an illustration invented to explain a passing siren, not an experiment anyone ran.

The record says it was run. Doppler, an Austrian physicist, predicted the effect in 1842. Buys Ballot confirmed it for sound in 1845 with his railway experiment, and Hippolyte Fizeau described the effect independently for light in 1848. The record gives no further detail of the experiment, and this entry adds none.

The effect is a change in the frequency an observer detects when source and observer move relative to each other. Approaching, the observed frequency is higher than the emitted one; receding, it is lower. The source's own output never changes. As a source approaches, each wave cycle starts from a point a little closer to the observer than the last, so the crests bunch up ahead and spread out behind. For sound, which travels in a medium, the effect depends separately on how the source, the observer and the air are moving, so a moving source is not equivalent to a moving observer.

The doubt comes from how well the story fits a classroom. A brass note rising as the train approaches and falling as it passes is exactly what a teacher would invent to explain an ambulance siren, so it is easy to file as an invention. The record also shows that Doppler's own grand application went wrong. He thought the effect explained the visible colors of stars, but stellar speeds are far too small for that, and the real signature is a shift of spectral lines, as Fizeau clarified. The prediction's founder got its most ambitious use wrong; the modest test with trumpeters got it right.

So the trumpeters were real. Doppler predicted the effect in 1842, and Buys Ballot confirmed it for sound on a moving train in 1845.

**Tell someone:** The trumpeters were real: in 1845 Buys Ballot tested the Doppler effect for sound on a moving train, three years after Doppler predicted it.

# Round 7: Words and phrases

Where the famous expressions really come from.

Phrases travel faster than their sources. A line is quoted, then paraphrased, then credited to whoever is most famous for the idea, and after a few retellings the paraphrase is the version everyone knows. ## Does "red herring" come from training hounds with smoked fish?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The hound-training origin is likely a myth. The figurative sense of "red herring" is real, and it is traceable to the journalist William Cobbett in 1807.

The popular version explains the phrase with a scene from the hunting field. Hunters, it says, dragged a strong-smelling smoked fish across a trail, either to train their hounds or to throw them off a scent. A red herring, on this account, is whatever pulls the dogs away from the trail they should be following.

The record does not support the practice. Michael Quinion's article "The Lure of the Red Herring" traces the figurative usage to Cobbett in 1807 and treats the hound-training practice as dubious. The phrase's documented history starts with a writer using it as a figure of speech, not with any recorded kennel routine. The record's hedge matters here: it calls the fish story likely a myth, not a proven fabrication.

The meaning the phrase settled into is precise. A red herring is something brought in to distract from the question at hand. Its fault is irrelevance, not falsehood: a true point that leads a discussion away from the issue is still a red herring. Logicians file it among the fallacies of relevance, close to the Latin *ignoratio elenchi*, the irrelevant conclusion. Writers of mysteries use the same term for a deliberate false clue that steers readers toward the wrong suspect, a legitimate device rather than a fault.

The hunting story spread because it fits the meaning so neatly. An odd phrase invites an explanation, and one that matches the sense of being led off a scent feels right on first hearing. Folk etymologies often work this way: the story is built backward from the meaning, and the fit is taken as proof. Repetition does the rest.

What can be stated with confidence is narrower than the legend. The figurative "red herring" is traceable to Cobbett in 1807. The hounds and the smoked fish are, on the record's evidence, likely a myth.

**Tell someone:** The hound-training story is likely a myth; the figurative "red herring" traces to the journalist William Cobbett in 1807.

## Is the "invisible hand" the heart of Adam Smith's economics?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Adam Smith used the exact phrase "invisible hand" only three times in his works, and just once in *The Wealth of Nations*. It was not the center of his economics, and he supported many roles for government.

The popular version makes the invisible hand Smith's master idea. The free market, left alone, turns private gain into public good, and Smith built his economics on that principle.

The record shows a narrower use. In *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), Smith wrote that an individual who intends only his own gain is led "by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention," namely the interest of society. His example was a merchant who prefers domestic to foreign industry for his own security and so, without meaning to, supports the wider economy. That is its only appearance in *The Wealth of Nations*. A study in *Econ Journal Watch*, "Adam Smith's Invisible Hands," documents how sparse and varied Smith's uses of the phrase were across his works.

Smith did not advocate unregulated laissez-faire. He supported government roles in defense, justice, public works and education. He was sharply critical of merchants, and warned that people of the same trade seldom meet without conspiring against the public. The invisible-hand result also breaks down under market failures such as externalities, monopoly, public goods and imperfect information, where self-interest does not serve society.

The phrase grew in other hands. The record says later economists inflated a limited observation into a sweeping principle of market efficiency, and the phrase became the central image of free-market economics. A two-word metaphor travels more easily than a long book's qualifications. Once it stood for the whole case for markets, readers expected to find it at the center of Smith's work, and its fame came to far outstrip its frequency in his writing.

The invisible hand is Smith's, and the economic passage is real. It appears once in that book, whose author also backed public works, courts and education, and distrusted merchants.

**Tell someone:** Adam Smith used "invisible hand" just once in *The Wealth of Nations*, and he backed public works, courts and education.

*Sources:* Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), Book IV, Chapter 2. Minowitz, "Adam Smith's Invisible Hands," *Econ Journal Watch* (2004). "Invisible hand," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-invisible-hand/

## Did Barbra Streisand's lawsuit make the photo of her house famous?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** In 2003 Barbra Streisand sued over an aerial photograph of her Malibu home, and news of the suit drew far more viewers than the picture had ever had. Before the suit it had been downloaded six times, two of them by her own lawyers, by the count of the project that published it.

The popular version says the lawsuit, meant to hide the photo, made millions look at it. The record backs the story, with a smaller number.

The photograph was one frame in a survey of about 12,000 images of the California coastline, taken by the California Coastal Records Project to document erosion. Streisand sued the photographer for violation of privacy. The sum is usually given as $50 million, though contemporary reports varied. Once the suit was news, more than 420,000 people visited the site within a month. The case was dismissed, and she was ordered to pay the photographer's legal costs. The download and visitor figures are the widely cited ones, and they come from the project itself.

The name came two years later. On 5 January 2005 Mike Masnick wrote on Techdirt about a Florida resort's demand that the site urinal.net remove a photo of one of its urinals. "Let's call it the Streisand Effect," he proposed. In her 2023 memoir Streisand called the suit a mistake in retrospect and said her concern had been her name attached to the photo.

The story spread because it is a near-perfect fable: a picture almost nobody had seen, a famous plaintiff, and a lawsuit that became the advertisement. The record adds a caution the fable leaves out. The effect names the case in which suppression itself becomes the story, not a rule that suppression always fails. Most takedowns succeed quietly and are never noticed, so the remembered cases suffer from survivorship bias.

So the core is documented. A 2003 suit over one frame in a coastal survey, six downloads before it, more than 420,000 visitors in the month after, and a name coined by Masnick in 2005.

## Did habeas corpus come from Magna Carta?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Habeas corpus did not come from Magna Carta. Its roots predate the charter of 1215. It grew out of English common law over centuries, and the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679 codified it.

The popular version gives the writ a single birth certificate. Magna Carta, in 1215, created the right to make a jailer justify an imprisonment before a court.

The record treats that as a simplification. Magna Carta is often cited as a precursor, and often called the origin, but the writ traces to earlier English practice. Its beginnings are older and more diffuse than the charter story suggests. The protection developed through common law over centuries, until the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679 codified it. Long known as the Great Writ of liberty, it is also protected in the United States, where the Constitution's Suspension Clause forbids suspending it except in cases of rebellion or invasion when public safety requires.

What the writ does is also narrower than its reputation. It orders whoever holds a prisoner to bring that person before a court and justify the detention; the Latin name refers to producing the body for examination. It tests only whether the detention is lawful, not whether the prisoner is guilty. It is a procedural remedy, not a guarantee of release, since a lawful detention will be upheld. It can also be suspended in extreme circumstances. Abraham Lincoln's suspension during the American Civil War is the famous and controversial case, and the writ's reach over wartime and immigration detention has been repeatedly contested.

The charter story spread because it is tidy. A right with a date and a founding document is easier to teach than one that built up through courts over centuries. Because Magna Carta is so often cited as a precursor, the step from precursor to source is a short one, and retellings take it.

The record's account is less neat and better supported. The writ's roots are older than Magna Carta; it grew through English common law and was codified by the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679.

**Tell someone:** Habeas corpus has roots older than Magna Carta; it grew through English common law and was codified by the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679.

## Did Rousseau coin "the noble savage"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Jean-Jacques Rousseau did not coin "the noble savage." The phrase does not appear in his writings and was pinned on him later.

The popular version makes the phrase Rousseau's own summary of his view: people in a state of nature were noble, and civilization ruined them.

The record shows why the label was easy to attach, and why it is still wrong. Rousseau belongs with the great social contract theorists, who reasoned about government by imagining people without one. They drew opposite portraits. Thomas Hobbes, in *Leviathan* (1651), pictured that condition as a war of every man against every man, in which life is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short. John Locke saw relative peace under natural law, troubled chiefly by the lack of an impartial judge. Rousseau imagined natural humans as simple, healthy and essentially good, corrupted only later by society.

That is close to the label, but it is not the same thing. Rousseau treated the state of nature as a conjecture, a condition that perhaps never existed, not as a report on any real people. The record says that attaching the exact phrase to him distorts his more qualified view. The words themselves are not his.

The label spread because it compresses. Set beside Hobbes's brutish war, Rousseau's picture of good natural humans is easy to reduce to a two-word tag, and a tag can stand in for a long argument. Once it circulated with his name beside it, repetition gave it the look of a quotation. The record does not say who first pinned it on him, or when.

The fact is short. Rousseau wrote about natural goodness and the corrupting effect of society, and he hedged it as a thought experiment. "Noble savage" is a phrase other people used about him, not one he used himself.

**Tell someone:** The phrase "noble savage" appears nowhere in Rousseau's writings; it was pinned on him later, and he hedged his view as a thought experiment.

## Did Clausewitz coin "the fog of war"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Carl von Clausewitz did not coin "the fog of war." In *On War* (1832) he conveyed the uncertainty of war through images of fog, twilight and moonlight. The crisp phrase is a later paraphrase, prominent partly because a twentieth-century translation chose the word "fog."

The popular version presents "the fog of war" as a phrase lifted straight from *On War*, the Prussian theorist's own coinage.

The record shows that the imagery is his and the phrase is not. *On War* was published after his death, in 1832. In Book 1, Chapter 3, Clausewitz wrote of uncertainty, of twilight, and of decisions taken on guesses at truth. He even put a figure on it, saying three-quarters of the factors are wrapped in fog, a number the record calls rhetorical emphasis rather than measurement. What he did not write is the tidy phrase "fog of war," or a German equivalent. Translators and interpreters supplied that later.

The concept he described is distinct from his other famous idea. Fog is the uncertainty of information: incomplete, delayed, contradictory or false reports about one's own forces, the enemy and the battlefield. Friction is the accumulated practical difficulty of carrying anything out. Modern sensors have not lifted the fog either. They reduce some uncertainty while adding new failure modes, from data overload to spoofed information, and forces still misidentify targets and receive contradictory intelligence.

The phrase became Clausewitz's because a label is easier to cite than a paragraph of metaphors. Once a translation set the image in a short, memorable form, it could be quoted without the surrounding text, and his name gave it authority. Each citation pinned it more firmly to him.

The accurate statement keeps both parts. The metaphors of fog, twilight and moonlight are Clausewitz's, from *On War* (1832). The crisp phrase "the fog of war" is a later paraphrase by translators and interpreters.

**Tell someone:** Clausewitz wrote of fog, twilight and moonlight in *On War*, but the tidy phrase "the fog of war" came later from translators and interpreters.

## Was the acronym "MAD" a critic's jibe?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The acronym MAD is attributed to a critic, Donald Brennan of the Hudson Institute, who used it derisively to argue that the nuclear posture it named was irrational. Robert McNamara, under whom the doctrine took shape, promoted "assured destruction," not MAD.

The popular version holds that MAD was the Pentagon's own acronym, a name the planners gave their strategy.

The record separates the policy from the name. The doctrine is rooted in Bernard Brodie's 1946 statement of nuclear deterrence and took shape in the 1960s under McNamara, then US Secretary of Defense. McNamara promoted assured destruction as a policy criterion. In his "Mutual Deterrence" speech in San Francisco on 18 September 1967, he defined the assured second-strike capability at its heart: the power to retaliate devastatingly even after absorbing a first strike. If both sides hold it, neither has a rational incentive to attack. Submarines at sea, hardened silos and dispersible bombers keep that threat alive. The 1972 ABM Treaty limited missile defenses on purpose, to preserve mutual vulnerability.

Brennan's acronym turned the policy's own words against it. The initials spell the charge. The record also notes that MAD was never a single formally adopted doctrine, since actual targeting policy shifted over time, and that critics argue it is immoral, holding whole civilian populations hostage.

The name came to seem official because it fits so well. McNamara's own phrase sits inside it, which makes the borrowed acronym look like his. Once it was in general use, it attached to the doctrine itself and the critic's irony fell away. What survived was a label that sounds like planning jargon and began as an objection.

The fact holds, with the record's hedge. McNamara's term was "assured destruction." The acronym MAD is attributed to Brennan, a critic who used it derisively.

## Did "the prisoner's dilemma" begin without any prisoners?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The game came first and the prisoners second. Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher devised it at the RAND Corporation in 1950 as a table of payoffs. The mathematician Albert Tucker added the story of two prisoners later that year, to explain it to an audience of psychologists at Stanford.

The popular version is that the dilemma began as a story about two suspects held in separate cells, each offered a lighter sentence for betraying the other.

The record starts with the mathematics. RAND had recruited academics after the Second World War to think about strategy scientifically, and game theory was new. Flood and Dresher wanted to know whether players in a game without binding agreements, where one side's gain is not simply the other's loss, would reach what is now called a Nash equilibrium. In early 1950 they had two colleagues play their game a hundred times in a row. The players often cooperated, more than the theory predicted.

The payoffs carry the whole idea. Betraying a cooperator pays best, mutual cooperation next, mutual betrayal worse, and being the one betrayed worst. Betrayal therefore pays more whatever the other player does, so both betray, and each ends up worse off than if both had cooperated.

Tucker's contribution was the story, with prison sentences as the payoffs. The name is usually credited to him too, but the earliest use of "prisoner's dilemma" in print is Luce and Raiffa's 1957 textbook, and the original handout for Tucker's talk did not carry the phrase.

The story spread because it is easier to retell than a table. Two suspects, a bargain and a betrayal turn four numbers into a plot with a moral, and the same plot fits arms races and price wars. A good illustration can outlive its occasion until it seems to be the origin.

So the order is documented. The game came from Flood and Dresher at RAND in 1950, and the prisoners arrived afterward, as Tucker's way of explaining it.

**Tell someone:** The prisoner's dilemma began in 1950 as a RAND payoff game by Flood and Dresher; Albert Tucker added the prisoners later that year to explain it.

*Sources:* "Prisoner's Dilemma," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. "Prisoner's dilemma," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-prisoners-dilemma/

## Did 12 of 16 rising-power rivalries end in war, as the "Thucydides trap" holds?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The tally is reported accurately: a Harvard project led by the political scientist Graham Allison counted sixteen cases in five hundred years in which a rising power threatened a ruling one, and twelve ended in war. What is disputed is the method behind the count, which cases belong in it and how each is scored.

The popular version treats the figure as a law of history. Three in four power transitions end in war, so a rising China and a ruling United States are probably headed for one.

The phrase is older than the count: in a 1980 lecture the novelist Herman Wouk asked how to break out of a "Thucydidean trap" in the American and Soviet rivalry. Allison revived it in a 2012 *Financial Times* article, developed it in *The Atlantic* in 2015 and in the 2017 book *Destined for War*. He anchored it in Thucydides's line that it was the rise of Athens and the fear this instilled in Sparta that made war inevitable. The case file, published by Harvard's Belfer Center, lists the sixteen rivalries.

The claim is narrower than the slogan. Allison does not say war is inevitable. He credits the four peaceful cases to imaginative statecraft, and avoiding war is the point of his argument.

The dispute is about the count. Critics among historians and international-relations scholars argue that the sixteen cases were not chosen by a rigorous, repeatable rule, so the ratio may reflect the selection. The coding of war or no war, and of which side was rising, is often ambiguous. Critics also call the idea single-cause and say it misreads Thucydides, whose account of the war's causes is many-sided. And even granting the tally, correlation is not causation.

The figure spread because a clean ratio under an ancient name sounds measured. A number turns a judgment into what looks like a finding, and the China question makes it urgent.

So the count is accurate as a count. Whether twelve of sixteen is evidence of a trap is still argued over.

**Tell someone:** The "Thucydides trap" rests on a Harvard count of 12 wars in 16 rising-power rivalries; the count is accurate, but which cases belong in it is disputed.

*Sources:* Allison, "The Thucydides Trap: Are the U.S. and China Headed for War?" *The Atlantic* (2015). Thucydides's Trap Case File, Harvard Belfer Center. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-thucydides-trap/

## Did Richard Dawkins coin the word "meme"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Richard Dawkins coined the word "meme" in his 1976 book *The Selfish Gene*, as the name for a unit of cultural transmission.

The popular assumption runs the other way. The word is so common in everyday talk that it is easy to take it for slang that grew up on its own, with no author and no date.

The record gives both. The meme appears in the same 1976 book that set out the selfish gene, the gene-centered view of evolution, for a general readership. Dawkins built on earlier work by W. D. Hamilton, George C. Williams (notably his 1966 *Adaptation and Natural Selection*) and Robert Trivers. In that view natural selection acts at the level of genes, the replicators that copy themselves across generations, and organisms are the survival machines, or vehicles, that carry them. "Selfish" is a metaphor for a gene's tendency to promote its own replication, not a claim that animals or people are innately selfish.

The meme was his analogy for culture. It is a unit of cultural transmission, like the gene, and subject to its own selection.

The gene-centered view itself remains contested. Critics argue that selection acts on whole organisms or groups, and Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin attacked it as reductionist. Many argue that gene-level and individual-level accounts often describe the same process in different terms. The word "meme" is not part of that dispute. Its source is a single, dated book.

The assumption that it has no author follows a common pattern. Words that pass into everyday use tend to shed their origins, and the more ordinary a word becomes, the less often anyone asks who made it.

This one has a maker and a year. Dawkins coined "meme" in *The Selfish Gene* in 1976.

**Tell someone:** The word "meme" has a maker and a year: Richard Dawkins coined it in *The Selfish Gene* in 1976, for a unit of cultural transmission.

# Round 8: Jokes that became laws

Satire, office humor and asides that hardened into rules.

Some laws began as a wink. A satirist needed a rule to hang an essay on, a mathematician left a boast in a margin, colleagues traded remarks over lunch. The rule outlived the occasion, and often the joke was the first thing forgotten. This round asks which famous laws were meant seriously, which were not, and how much of the original humor the record lets survive.

## Did Parkinson's law begin as a joke in *The Economist*?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Parkinson's law began as satire. The naval historian C. Northcote Parkinson opened a satirical essay in *The Economist* on 19 November 1955 with the "commonplace observation" that made his name: work "expands so as to fill the time available for its completion."

The popular version treats it as a management finding, a rule distilled from research into how organizations work.

The record shows a satirist with a target. Parkinson's evidence was the British Admiralty, whose bureaucracy kept growing as the fleet shrank. Officials, he argued, make work for one another regardless of the work to be done. The essay was later expanded into a 1957 book, *Parkinson's Law, or The Pursuit of Progress*, which carried the joke further. He offered a bureaucratic corollary: an organization's staff grows by a roughly fixed percentage each year, which he estimated at five to seven, independent of the workload. A later financial version held that expenditure rises to meet income, so that money, like time, is consumed by whatever is available.

The figures gave the satire the look of a study. Numbers, a named institution and a crisp general rule are the furniture of research, and satire that borrows them is easily read straight. The opening line also describes something most office workers recognize. An hour-long meeting fills the hour; a fifteen-minute slot covers the same ground. A law that matches daily experience does not need its source checked, and the satirical frame fell away in the retelling.

Read as a serious rule, it also gets stretched. The record is careful about what it does not say. It is not proof that every deadline should be brutal or that rushed work is as good. Past a point, compression destroys quality, and creative or research work needs unpredictable slack. The useful reading is narrower: generous time is not free, because it quietly recruits busywork.

The record is clear on the origin. Parkinson's law is a line from a satirical essay in *The Economist*, 19 November 1955, about an Admiralty that grew while its fleet got smaller.

**Tell someone:** Parkinson's law began as a 1955 satirical essay in *The Economist* about an Admiralty whose bureaucracy grew as its fleet shrank.

## Was the Peter Principle written as satire?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The Peter Principle was pitched as satire. The educator Laurence J. Peter and the playwright Raymond Hull set it out in their 1969 book: "In a hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to his level of incompetence." It landed as serious management theory.

The popular version treats the principle as a finding of management research, a measured law of how organizations promote people.

The record shows a different pedigree. The book's subtitle was *Why Things Always Go Wrong*. Its co-author was a playwright. The mechanism it described is simple: promotion rewards being good at the job a person already has, not the job they are moving into, so people keep rising until they reach a role they are bad at, and there they stay. Peter and Hull added a corollary of their own: "In time, every post tends to be occupied by an employee who is incompetent to carry out its duties."

Others extended it in the same spirit. Scott Adams's Dilbert Principle, from the 1990s, held that companies deliberately promote their least competent workers into management, to limit the damage they can do to the product. Archibald Putt's version for technical organizations runs: "Technology is dominated by two types of people. Those who understand what they do not manage, and those who manage what they do not understand." A small literature of corollaries grew from a book meant to amuse.

The satire was taken seriously because the mechanism is real. The record's examples are familiar: the best salesperson made sales manager, the strongest engineer moved into a job about politics and people, the gifted researcher buried in budgets. Where promotion rewards current performance and the next role differs in kind, the effect bites. A joke that describes a real failure mode is easy to read as a finding.

The record also marks its limits. It is a critique of one promotion mechanism, not a claim that everyone ends up incompetent. Plenty of people keep growing into larger roles, and organizations that promote on demonstrated fitness for the next job escape it.

The origin stands. Peter and Hull wrote the principle in 1969 as satire, and readers turned it into management theory.

**Tell someone:** The Peter Principle was pitched as satire, written in 1969 by an educator and a playwright under the subtitle *Why Things Always Go Wrong*.

*Sources:* Peter & Hull, *The Peter Principle: Why Things Always Go Wrong* (1969). "Peter principle," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-peter-principle/

## Was there a real committee that argued over a bike shed?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** No real meeting lies behind the story. The reactor, the bicycle shed and the refreshment budget are C. Northcote Parkinson's invented example, from his 1957 book *Parkinson's Law, or The Pursuit of Progress*.

The popular version reports it as something that happened: a committee approved a nuclear reactor in minutes and then argued for far longer about a bike shed.

The record places the scene in an essay titled "High Finance, or the Point of Vanishing Interest." Parkinson's finance committee approves a £10 million atomic reactor in about two and a half minutes. It argues for three-quarters of an hour over a £350 bicycle shed. It then spends over an hour on a refreshment budget of £21 a year, the smallest sum drawing the longest fight. Everyone understands a shed, and no member wants to admit he cannot follow the reactor. From this Parkinson drew his law of triviality: the time a committee spends on an item is inversely proportional to the money involved.

Parkinson wrote as a satirist. The record calls the committee and its figures a fictional illustration rather than data, and the "inverse proportion" phrasing rhetorical. It is also a separate idea from his better-known law that work expands to fill the time available, though both come from the same book.

The name arrived much later. In 1999 the FreeBSD developer Poul-Henning Kamp, exasperated by an endless mailing-list thread, sent an email headed "A bike shed (any colour will do)…" on 2 October. It popularized "bikeshedding" for teams that agonize over trivia while real design decisions pass unexamined.

The scene passes for a true story because it is built like one. Exact sums and exact timings read like the minutes of a meeting, and satire often borrows the look of a report. Once the scene was retold apart from its book, the figures were what remained, and they sounded like evidence.

The committee never met. The pattern it was invented to show is what the record's everyday examples describe: a large budget nodded through in minutes, then a long debate about a logo's color or the office snacks.

**Tell someone:** The committee that approved a reactor in minutes and argued over a bike shed never met; C. Northcote Parkinson invented it in 1957.

## Did Brooks admit his own law was an oversimplification?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Fred Brooks, who coined Brooks's law in *The Mythical Man-Month* (1975), freely admitted that the law oversimplifies a real tendency: he introduced it with the words "Oversimplifying outrageously."

The popular version treats it as an iron law of software: never add people to a late project, because it will only get later.

The record shows an author more careful than his slogan. The law reads: "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later." Brooks drew it from his experience managing the development of IBM's OS/360, and he gave two mechanisms. New people need time to learn the work, and the existing team must teach them. Communication overhead also grows faster than headcount. A team of n people has n(n−1)/2 pairwise links to keep in sync, so doubling a team from five to ten takes the paths from 10 to 45, and a team of 50 carries 1,225. His analogy for work that cannot be divided: "The bearing of a child takes nine months, no matter how many women are assigned."

The limits are just as clear. Adding people early, before a project is late, can help. A project may be "late" only because its schedule was fantasy. Skilled developers who already know the codebase avoid most of the ramp-up penalty. For divisible tasks, more hands produce more output. The law bites hardest on tightly coupled work under heavy communication load, and strong modularity and clear ownership shrink it.

The iron-law reading spread because the sentence is short and the experience behind it is common. A team that has watched a rescue hire slow everything down needs no further proof. The caveats live in the surrounding chapters, and caveats rarely travel with a slogan.

The author's own verdict is on the record. Brooks stated his law in 1975, explained when it applies, and introduced it with "Oversimplifying outrageously."

**Tell someone:** Fred Brooks introduced his own law, that adding manpower to a late software project makes it later, with the words "Oversimplifying outrageously."

## Did Clarke add "Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic" years later?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Arthur C. Clarke's most famous law came last. He stated his First and Second Laws in 1962 and added the Third, "Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic," to the revised 1973 edition of the same book, having first published it in a 1968 letter to *Science*.

The popular version treats the three laws as a set, written together.

The record shows them arriving over a decade. The first two appeared in Clarke's essay "Hazards of Prophecy: The Failure of Imagination," in *Profiles of the Future* (1962). The First Law: "When a distinguished but elderly scientist states that something is possible, he is almost certainly right. When he states that something is impossible, he is very probably wrong." The Second: "The only way of discovering the limits of the possible is to venture a little way past them into the impossible." His examples were experts who called things impossible. The astronomer Simon Newcomb argued that heavier-than-air flight was impossible; the Wright brothers flew in 1903. Lord Rutherford dismissed the prospect of practical atomic energy.

The Third Law came later, and Clarke was frank about why he stopped there. In a footnote he wrote that "as three laws were good enough for Newton, I have modestly decided to stop there."

The record is clear that none of the three is a law in the scientific sense. They are aphorisms, with no predictive formalism, and the word "law" is playful. The Third is about the onlooker, not the machine: an observer who lacks the mechanism cannot tell engineered cause and effect from the inexplicable. It does not say technology is magic. An inversion, "Any sufficiently advanced magic is indistinguishable from technology," is usually credited to Larry Niven, though the attribution is contested.

The idea that the laws were written together comes from their form. A numbered set of three looks designed as a unit, and the most quoted member is the one people assume came first. Newton's three laws, which Clarke himself invoked, set the template.

The dates settle it. The First and Second Laws are from 1962. The Third appeared in 1968 and joined them in the book in 1973.

**Tell someone:** Clarke's First and Second Laws date from 1962; the magic one came last, in a 1968 letter to *Science*, joining the book in 1973.

## Has toast landing butter-side down been studied seriously?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** In 1995 the physicist Robert Matthews published "Tumbling toast, Murphy's Law and the fundamental constants" in the *European Journal of Physics*. It won a 1996 Ig Nobel Prize, and it argued that toast does tend to land butter-side down, for mundane physical reasons.

The popular version treats falling toast as a joke about bad luck with no science behind it, the emblem of Sod's law.

The law itself is a joke, and an old one. Sod's law is a British colloquialism of obscure, undated origin, used where North Americans say "Murphy's law." Its name comes from the "unlucky sod" to whom misfortune happens: if something can go wrong, it will, and at the worst possible time. The record explains most of it as selective memory. People remember the rain on the day they forgot an umbrella and forget the dry days. The other queue seems faster because a lagging queue is the one people notice. Richard Dawkins dismissed the idea that events "know" what a person wants.

The toast is the exception. Matthews's paper is described in the record as tongue-in-cheek but genuine physics. Dropped from a typical table height, a slice rotates only about half a turn before it lands. A slice that starts butter-up therefore tends to arrive butter-down. Physics, not superstition, tips it over, which makes the law's most famous example the one with a real deterministic basis.

The assumption that nobody had studied it is understandable. The toast is the mascot of a law built on bias, so it is natural to assume it is bias too. A physics paper about breakfast also sounds like a joke, and this one was written tongue-in-cheek.

The record holds both facts. Sod's law is mostly a trick of memory. Toast landing butter-side down is not, and a 1995 paper in the *European Journal of Physics* worked out why.

## Did *Harvard Business Review* reject Conway's law?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** By Melvin Conway's own account, *Harvard Business Review* rejected his paper on the grounds that he had not proved his thesis. "How Do Committees Invent?" appeared instead in *Datamation* in April 1968.

The popular assumption is that a law now quoted so widely in management and software circles must have arrived through a leading management journal.

The record gives it a humbler start. Conway's own wording was that organizations "are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations." After the *Datamation* paper, participants at the 1968 National Symposium on Modular Programming gave the idea the name "Conway's Law." Fred Brooks later cited and popularized it in *The Mythical Man-Month* (1975).

The rejection looks less foolish with time, not more. *HBR*'s objection was that the thesis was unproved, and for decades it was. The empirical support arrived much later. In 2012 MacCormack, Baldwin and Rusnak compared software products of near-identical function, some built by tightly coupled commercial firms and some by loosely coupled open-source communities. The products' modularity mirrored the coupling of the organizations that made them.

The law has since become a method. The "inverse Conway maneuver," coined by Jonny LeRoy and Matt Simons in 2010, runs it backward: to get a target architecture, first reshape the teams to match it. The record still calls the law a strong tendency, not an iron rule. A small, co-located team can build a well-factored system without visible mirroring, and reorganizing teams will not instantly refactor a rigid legacy system.

The prestigious-journal assumption reads a law's later fame back into its birth. Ideas that end up central tend to be imagined as having started at the center. This one started with a rejection.

The record is plain on the facts. *Harvard Business Review* turned the paper down as unproved, *Datamation* published it in April 1968, and the evidence that it was right came decades later.

**Tell someone:** By Melvin Conway's own account, *Harvard Business Review* rejected the paper behind his law as unproved; the evidence that it was right came decades later.

## Did Fermat have a proof too big for the margin?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The margin note is real; the proof almost certainly was not. Around 1637 Pierre de Fermat wrote in his copy of Diophantus's *Arithmetica* that he had a marvelous proof the margin was too narrow to contain, but the record judges he almost certainly had no valid general proof. The accepted proof, by Andrew Wiles with Richard Taylor, was published in 1995 and rests on twentieth-century mathematics.

The popular version takes Fermat at his word. He had a short, elegant proof, and the world lost it to a narrow margin.

The note is documented. Fermat's claim was that no three positive whole numbers satisfy a to the n plus b to the n equals c to the n for any power above two. For the power two there are endless answers, such as 3, 4 and 5; above two, he said, there are none. His son published the note in 1670, after Fermat's death.

The proof is not. Fermat did prove the case of the fourth power, by his method of infinite descent. He posed the cases of three and four as challenges to other mathematicians but never the general one, and he never mentioned the marvelous proof again. Leonhard Euler published a proof for the cube in his *Algebra* of 1770, and others extended the range case by case. No elementary proof has ever been found. Wiles announced his in 1993; a gap was found and repaired with Taylor, ending a search of 358 years.

The margin story spread because it is the most famous aside in mathematics. A boast with a cliffhanger invites everyone to try, and a lost proof makes a better legend than a mistaken one. The record's reading is plainer: the method that finally worked, proving that certain curves are modular, was far beyond any tool Fermat had. The MacTutor history suggests he may well have realized his proof was wrong.

So the note is real, c. 1637, and the proof is not part of the result. The record says Fermat almost certainly did not have one.

**Tell someone:** Fermat's margin note claiming a "marvelous proof" is real, but he almost certainly had none; the accepted proof, published in 1995, needed twentieth-century mathematics.

## Did Kelly Johnson coin "Keep it simple, stupid"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The phrase is attributed to Kelly Johnson, lead engineer at Lockheed's Skunk Works, but the attribution is not airtight. A 1938 *Minneapolis Star* article used the variant "keep it short and simple," before Johnson's association with the phrase, and the earliest dated record of the acronym itself is a U.S. Navy "Project KISS" in 1960.

The popular version credits Johnson alone, the engineer who coined KISS.

The record supports a strong association and no clean first use. The phrase is linked to Johnson's reported instruction that the aircraft his engineers designed be repairable in the field by an average mechanic with a few basic tools. An aircraft only its designers can service is worse than a simpler one anybody can, because a fleet is judged on availability, not peak performance. The Navy documented its Project KISS in 1960, and the term was in general use by 1970. The 1938 newspaper variant sits awkwardly in front of all of this.

The punctuation matters too. By one account Johnson wrote the phrase without a comma, so "stupid" is not an insult aimed at the reader. The instruction is to keep the thing simple, and foolproof. The famous version, comma included, turns a design rule into a scolding.

A problem with essential complexity cannot be made simple, only made to look simple in one place and worse in another, and "we kept it simple" is a common post-mortem finding.

The attribution to Johnson spread because it makes the better story. A famous engineer at Lockheed's Skunk Works, with a field-repair anecdote attached, is easier to retell than a newspaper variant and a Navy project record. Stories about origins favor a person over a paper trail.

The safe statement is the hedged one. KISS is attributed to Kelly Johnson, the Navy recorded it in 1960, and a 1938 variant predates his association with it. Who said it first is not settled.

## Did Fermi pose the Fermi paradox over lunch?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The question began as a lunchtime remark by the physicist Enrico Fermi at Los Alamos in 1950, with his colleagues Teller, Konopinski and York. Firsthand accounts differ on his exact words, and the paradox's name and rigorous form came later, from others.

The popular version has Fermi at lunch asking, "Where is everybody?" and launching the paradox on the spot.

The record confirms the lunch and loosens the rest. The remark was informal. Fermi did not coin the name or write the paradox up. The 1950 exchange is reconstructed from firsthand accounts that disagree about what he said, so "Where is everybody?" is the usual rendering rather than a verified quotation. The question was first put into print by Carl Sagan. Michael Hart gave it rigorous form in 1975, and Frank Tipler followed.

Hart's version made the tension sharp. A civilization, he argued, could spread across the galaxy in far less time than the galaxy's age, so the absence of any civilization reaching Earth suggests none exists. The paradox sets that argument against the numbers: hundreds of billions of stars, an abundance of planets, and a great span of time in which intelligent life should have arisen and spread or signaled.

It is an argument, not a law, and it proves nothing about whether aliens exist. Its force depends on the premise that intelligent life should be common, which rests on the unknown terms of the Drake equation. Proposed resolutions range from Robin Hanson's Great Filter, some improbable step between a lifeless planet and a galactic civilization, to the Rare Earth view that complex life is uncommon, to the possibility that civilizations exist but cannot be detected. There is no consensus.

The popular version is mostly right, and its errors are of a familiar kind. A single quip from a famous physicist is a better story than a lunch remembered differently by those present, followed by decades of work by others. The name then credits Fermi with an argument he never wrote down.

The record supports the lunch. Fermi raised the question at Los Alamos in 1950; the exact words are unknown, and the paradox as an argument belongs to later writers, starting with Hart in 1975.

**Tell someone:** Fermi did raise the question over lunch at Los Alamos in 1950, though firsthand accounts differ on his exact words.

## Was Photo 51 taken by Raymond Gosling?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Raymond Gosling, a doctoral student, took Photo 51 at King's College London in May 1952, while working under Rosalind Franklin's supervision. The credit belongs to both of them.

The popular version gives the photograph to Franklin alone. She is the person the image is most closely associated with, and the short telling of the DNA story has no room for her student.

The record is more exact. Photo 51 is an X-ray diffraction image of the hydrated, B-form of DNA. Gosling made it with Franklin's carefully prepared, humidity-controlled DNA fibers, exposing the sample to X-rays for a total of 62 hours, by Franklin's notebook. The film recorded dark spots arranged in an X. A helix produces exactly that cross when it scatters X-rays, so the X was direct evidence that DNA is helical. The spacing of the spots carried numbers too: the width of the helix and the distance over which it repeats.

Those clues fed into the model of the double helix that James Watson and Francis Crick built in 1953, along with data from Franklin's unpublished reports. The image did not hand over the whole structure on its own. The model also needed chemical knowledge, the base-pairing insight and other data, including Franklin's own measurements. How the photograph reached Watson is the next question in this round.

The shorter version spread because the photograph became a symbol. It stands for an argument about how Franklin's data and her credit were handled, and a symbol of overlooked credit tends to gather all of that credit to the person it represents. A supervisor's name also travels further than a student's, in science as elsewhere. The record makes the point directly: saying simply that Franklin took the photograph omits Gosling's role.

So the attribution has two names in it. Gosling set up the exposure on 2 May 1952. He did it with Franklin's fibers and under her supervision, and the image is most associated with her experimental technique. Both statements are true, and leaving out either one misstates who made Photo 51.

**Tell someone:** Photo 51 was taken by Raymond Gosling, a doctoral student, in May 1952, using Rosalind Franklin's fibers and working under her supervision.

## Did Watson and Crick steal Rosalind Franklin's data?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The record documents that Maurice Wilkins showed Photo 51 to James Watson without Franklin's knowledge, and that her data shaped the 1953 model. Recent scholarship argues that this was not a simple story of theft.

The popular version is a heist. Watson and Crick took Franklin's photograph behind her back and won the Nobel Prize on it.

Part of that is in the record. Wilkins showed the image to Watson without Franklin's knowledge or permission. The X-shaped pattern, and the measurements its spots encoded, fed into the double-helix model that Watson and Crick built in 1953. They also used data from Franklin's unpublished reports. Watson, Crick and Wilkins shared the 1962 Nobel Prize. Franklin had died in 1958, and the prize is not awarded posthumously.

Other parts do not hold as told. The photograph did not reveal the structure by itself. Building the model also needed chemical knowledge, the base-pairing insight and other data, including Franklin's own measurements. And the ethical account is being revisited. In a 2023 article in *Nature*, Cobb and Comfort argue that Franklin was an equal contributor who understood the significance of her data. The heist version spread because it has a clear wrong and a clear victim. Franklin received far less recognition in the popular story than the three men who shared the prize, and her death before the award left her outside it. A story about lost credit is easier to tell as a theft than as a mix of cooperation, rivalry and an image shown without consent. A single photograph also makes a better symbol than a stack of unpublished reports.

What the record supports is narrower than the legend. Wilkins showed Photo 51 to Watson without Franklin's knowledge, and her data went into the 1953 model, which raises lasting questions about how her data and her credit were handled. Whether that amounts to theft is a judgment, not a finding. The latest scholarship the record cites, Cobb and Comfort in 2023, argues that it was a more complex history in which Franklin was an equal contributor.

## Did Philippa Foot invent the trolley problem?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Philippa Foot introduced the original case in 1967, in a paper called "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect." She did not name it. Judith Jarvis Thomson called it the trolley problem and added its best-known variants in 1976 and 1985.

The popular version comes in two forms. One treats the puzzle as an old one, too old to trace. The other treats it as a modern psychology test.

The record gives it a clear birth. In the basic case a runaway trolley will kill five people on the track. In Foot's version its driver can steer onto a side track, where it would kill one; in the version most retold, a bystander pulls a lever to divert it. Most people judge that diverting it is permitted. Foot's case appeared in a paper about the doctrine of double effect, the principle that harm intended as a means is worse than equal harm merely foreseen as a side effect.

Thomson named the problem and sharpened it. In her footbridge variant, the only way to stop the trolley is to push a large stranger off a bridge into its path. Most people judge this forbidden, though it too trades one life for five. In her transplant case, a surgeon could save five patients by taking the organs of one healthy visitor, and almost everyone judges this monstrous.

The psychology came later. In 2001 Joshua Greene's team brought brain imaging to the cases, reporting in *Science* that personal dilemmas such as the footbridge engage emotional brain systems while impersonal ones engage deliberative systems. Those findings are influential but contested, and they have faced replication and interpretation challenges.

Both popular versions have a reason. The puzzle strips a moral choice down to a count of lives, so it reads like folk wisdom rather than an argument from a journal. It also feels like a laboratory test because it has become one: it shaped experimental moral psychology and public discussion of machine ethics, from medical triage to self-driving cars. And the name hides the author. The label that stuck is Thomson's, while the case is Foot's.

The trolley problem has a datable origin. Foot set out the case in 1967, Thomson named and extended it in 1976 and 1985, and Greene's team took it into the brain scanner in 2001.

**Tell someone:** Philippa Foot set out the trolley case in 1967, but the name belongs to Judith Jarvis Thomson, who also added the footbridge variant.

## Was Wegener the first to notice the continents fit together?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Alfred Wegener was not the first. Ortelius and others remarked on the jigsaw fit of the continents centuries before him. Wegener's originality, first put to geologists in a 1912 lecture, lay in combining that fit with fossil, rock and climate evidence.

The popular version has Wegener looking at a map, seeing South America nest into Africa and spotting the jigsaw.

The fit is real. The eastern bulge of South America sits neatly in the western curve of Africa, the match that most strikingly suggests the two were once joined. But the record says plainly that it had been noticed centuries earlier. A related claim that Francis Bacon had the idea of drift is, in the record's words, "a persistent overstatement."

What Wegener added was a case built from several independent lines of evidence. The German meteorologist and geophysicist set it out in lectures in 1912 and in his 1915 book *The Origin of Continents and Oceans*. Fossils of the freshwater reptile Mesosaurus and the seed fern Glossopteris turn up on continents now divided by oceans they could not have crossed. Rock formations and mountain belts line up when the continents are reassembled. Glacial deposits lie in warm regions, and coal, formed in lush climates, lies near the poles. Together they pointed to a single former landmass, which he called Pangaea.

What he lacked was a cause. He pictured continents plowing through the ocean crust, pushed by a pole-fleeing force and by tides. Calculation showed those forces were far too weak, and geophysicists led by figures such as Harold Jeffreys had rejected the idea by around 1930. It was vindicated in the 1950s and 1960s through paleomagnetism and seafloor spreading, and absorbed into plate tectonics.

The myth spread because the fit is the one piece of evidence anyone can see. A story that credits Wegener with noticing it gives him the part that was not his and drops the part that was. His career also has the shape of a legend: a meteorologist challenging geologists, rejected for about half a century and then proved right. A hero in that story tends to be handed every observation in it.

The fit was old news by 1912. Wegener's achievement was the synthesis: coastlines, fossils, rocks and ancient climates read together as one argument that the continents had moved.

**Tell someone:** Ortelius and others remarked on the jigsaw fit of the continents centuries before Wegener; his real feat was adding fossils, rocks and ancient climates.

## Was umami discovered in a seaweed broth?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** In 1908 the Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda isolated glutamate from kombu seaweed broth, coined the word umami and developed MSG as a seasoning. Umami is now widely accepted as the fifth basic taste.

The popular version is skeptical. On this telling, umami is a marketing invention: a word made up to sell a seasoning, not a taste in its own right.

The record says otherwise. Ikeda was the first to identify umami scientifically, and he took its name from a Japanese word meaning, roughly, pleasant savory taste. The taste comes from glutamate, an amino acid, together with certain nucleotides, chiefly inosinate and guanylate. Combined, they show a striking synergy: the savory intensity is far greater than the two alone would give. That is why Japanese dashi, made from kombu, rich in glutamate, and bonito flakes, rich in inosinate, tastes so deep.

Umami is not only MSG. Glutamate occurs naturally in aged cheeses such as Parmesan, in tomatoes, mushrooms and broths, and in soy sauce and miso. The tongue detects it with dedicated taste receptors, including receptors built from taste receptor type 1 proteins and related glutamate receptors. That detection of glutamate as a quality separate from sweet, sour, salty and bitter is what makes umami a basic taste.

The case was not settled quickly. Recognition took most of a century after Ikeda's 1908 work, and the exact identity and workings of the umami receptors are still an area of ongoing research.

The skeptical version spread for understandable reasons. The same chemist named the taste and developed the seasoning that delivers it, which looks like a sales pitch. The long wait for acceptance gave doubt time to settle in. And past fears about MSG, which the record says are unfounded, cast suspicion on anything tied to it.

The order of events runs the other way from the cynical story. Ikeda found glutamate in kombu broth first and named the taste it produced. The seasoning came after, and the receptors found since show that umami is a taste the tongue registers on its own.

**Tell someone:** Umami was found in kombu seaweed broth: Kikunae Ikeda isolated glutamate there in 1908 and named the taste before developing MSG.

## Did Mendeleev invent the periodic table alone?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Dmitri Mendeleev published his periodic table in 1869, but he did not conceive it alone. John Newlands had proposed a related law of octaves in 1865, and Julius Lothar Meyer worked out a similar arrangement independently at about the same time.

The popular version gives the table to one man in one year: Mendeleev, 1869.

The record shares the credit out. Mendeleev ordered the roughly sixty known elements by increasing atomic weight and started new rows so that elements with similar chemistry fell into the same columns. That revealed the periodic law: properties repeat at regular intervals when the elements are listed by weight. But Newlands had already proposed a related law, and Lothar Meyer had reached a similar table on his own.

Mendeleev is credited chiefly for three things, according to the record. He published a full system. He pressed the periodic law harder than anyone. And he made bold predictions that came true. Where an element placed strictly by weight would have landed in the wrong column, he left a gap and predicted what would fill it. His eka-aluminium, eka-boron and eka-silicon were later found as gallium, scandium and germanium. Gallium, found in 1875, matched his forecast closely. He also adjusted some accepted atomic weights so that elements fell into their chemically proper places.

His table was not the last word either. Ordering by weight produced anomalies, such as tellurium and iodine, where the chemically correct order reverses the order of weights. Henry Moseley resolved them in 1913 by showing that elements should be ordered by atomic number. Mendeleev also did not know why the properties repeat; electron shells were understood later.

The single-inventor story spread because the predictions make a better tale than the priority record. A gap in a table, filled years later by a new element that matches the forecast, is a moment people remember. A law of octaves and a parallel table drawn up elsewhere are background. Naming an achievement after one person then makes it sound as though one person did it.

The periodic system had several authors in the 1860s. Mendeleev earned the name by publishing the fullest version and predicting elements before they were found. The modern order, by atomic number, came from Moseley in 1913.

**Tell someone:** Mendeleev had company: Newlands proposed a related law of octaves in 1865, and Lothar Meyer reached a similar table independently at about the same time.

## Were Mendel's pea results too good to be true?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** In 1936 Fisher began a statistical debate over whether Gregor Mendel's reported ratios fit too closely to be chance. The record calls the question unresolved, not a proven charge of fabrication.

The popular version is short: Mendel fudged his data.

The record starts with the experiments. Mendel bred pea plants between 1856 and 1863 and published the results as "Experiments on Plant Hybridization" in 1866. He found that heredity is carried by discrete factors, now called genes, rather than by a blending of parental traits. Crossing plants that differ in one trait gives a uniform first generation and a ratio of three dominant to one recessive in the next. Following two unlinked traits gives nine to three to three to one. The work was largely ignored until about 1900, when Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak rediscovered it.

Fisher's question was about the numbers, not the rules. It asked whether Mendel's reported counts sat closer to the ideal ratios than chance would usually allow. The record says the debate has run since 1936 and remains open. It does not record a finding that Mendel invented results.

The laws themselves have held up as the foundation of classical genetics, within known limits. Independent assortment fails for linked genes. Incomplete dominance, codominance and epistasis change the ratios while the alleles still separate normally. Mitochondrial inheritance and genomic imprinting break one rule or another.

The fraud version spread because a suspicion is easier to repeat than a statistical argument. "Too good to be true" fits in one sentence; an analysis of how far reported ratios should scatter does not. The target makes it memorable too: an accusation against the founder of genetics is a better story than a quarrel about variance. And a debate that stays open for decades is easily rounded off to a verdict, in either direction.

The record supports the open question and nothing stronger. Since Fisher in 1936, statisticians have argued over whether Mendel's ratios fit too well. That argument is unresolved, and it is not a proven charge that he fabricated his data.

**Tell someone:** Since Fisher in 1936, statisticians have argued over whether Mendel's pea ratios fit too well, and no one has settled it either way.

## Did Gauss guess the prime number theorem as a teenager?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The record reports that Gauss, as a teenager in the 1790s, conjectured the prime number theorem from tables of primes. It was proved only in 1896, about a century later.

The popular version remembers the theorem as a nineteenth-century proof, the work of the mathematicians who finally settled it.

The theorem says the number of primes up to a given size is roughly that size divided by its natural logarithm. Primes thin out as numbers grow, but smoothly. Near a million, whose natural logarithm is about fourteen, roughly one number in fourteen is prime. Near a billion, whose logarithm is about twenty-one, roughly one in twenty-one is.

According to the record, Gauss reached this pattern by looking at tables of primes, as a teenager, in the 1790s. Legendre reached it around the same time, in about 1797 or 1798. Neither could prove it. The proof came in 1896, when Jacques Hadamard and Charles-Jean de la Vallée Poussin each settled it, independently, using complex analysis of the Riemann zeta function. Erdős and Selberg found an elementary proof in 1948 and 1949.

Part of the problem is still open. Beyond small numbers, the simple estimate undercounts the primes, and a refined one, the logarithmic integral, tracks the true count far more closely. How small the error can be is tied to the Riemann hypothesis, which is unproven.

The guess is less famous than the proof for a plain reason. A proof is an event with a date and authors. A conjecture read off a table is a private observation, and in this case it had two owners, since Legendre made it too. The teenage detail also sounds like the kind of flourish that gathers around famous mathematicians, so it is easy to dismiss as legend.

This one is in the record. Gauss guessed the law from prime tables in the 1790s, while still a teenager, and it took a century of mathematics and the zeta function to prove that he was right.

## Did Hooke publish his law as an anagram?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Robert Hooke published his law of springs in 1676 as the anagram "ceiiinosssttuu." In 1678 he revealed the answer: "ut tensio, sic vis," as the extension, so the force.

The popular version assumes nothing of the sort. A law taught as a single line of algebra is presumed to have been published plainly, like any other result.

The record gives the sequence. Hooke, an English natural philosopher, said he had known the law since 1660. He published it sixteen years later in scrambled form, and two years after that he gave the solution. The fourteen letters of the anagram are the letters of the Latin phrase, sorted into alphabetical order, with u standing for v as printers then used it.

The record does not say why Hooke chose to hide the law. Any reason offered here would be invented, so none is.

What he hid was simple. Pull a spring twice as hard and it stretches twice as far. Force and deformation rise together in fixed proportion, a straight line whose slope is the spring's stiffness. That is what lets a spring balance read a weight off a length. In its general form, stress is proportional to strain, with an elastic modulus such as Young's modulus as the constant, and it is the starting point for the theory of how solids deform.

The law has firm edges. It holds only within the elastic limit, for small deformations. Beyond that, materials yield, deform permanently and then break. Some materials are not linear even at small stretches: rubber is only roughly linear for small pulls and responds nonlinearly further out. The record calls the law a first-order approximation with a well-defined domain, not a law derived from first principles.

The assumption of plain publication is natural. Textbooks present results stripped of how they first appeared, so a tidy equation looks as though it arrived tidy. An anagram is also the kind of detail that sounds like a later joke rather than a printed fact.

It was printed. Hooke's law first appeared in 1676 as fourteen scrambled letters, and the Latin sentence they spell, "ut tensio, sic vis," followed in 1678.

**Tell someone:** Hooke published his spring law in 1676 as the anagram "ceiiinosssttuu," and only revealed in 1678 that it spelled "ut tensio, sic vis."

*Sources:* Hooke, *Lectures de Potentia Restitutiva, or of Spring* (1678). "Hooke's law," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/hookes-law/

## Was the 1919 eclipse a clean proof of Einstein?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The 1919 eclipse measurements matched Einstein's prediction, and modern reanalyses largely uphold the result. But the proof was not clean: blurred plates from one telescope were discarded, and historians have debated whether that was justified.

The popular version is a single triumphant photograph. Eddington's plates proved relativity beyond doubt.

The record is more careful. On 29 May 1919 two expeditions, organized by Frank Watson Dyson and Arthur Eddington, observed a total solar eclipse: one at Sobral in Brazil and one, with Eddington, on the island of Príncipe off West Africa. They photographed stars of the Hyades cluster near the darkened Sun and compared their positions with photographs of the same stars taken months earlier at night. Newtonian gravity predicted that starlight grazing the Sun would bend by about 0.87 arcseconds. General relativity predicted about twice that, 1.75 arcseconds. The measurements came out near Einstein's value. Announced in November 1919, the result made Einstein world-famous almost overnight.

The trouble lies in what was left out. The main telescope at Sobral produced blurred, defocused images, apparently because the Sun's heat distorted the instrument. That dataset gave a deflection closer to the Newtonian value, and it was dropped from the final conclusion. The shifts that counted came chiefly from a backup telescope at Sobral and from the Príncipe plates. Critics from the 1980s onward argued that Eddington may have selectively excluded data that favored Einstein, and that the error bars were large relative to the effect.

Modern reanalyses of the surviving plates have generally vindicated the original conclusion. They found a real instrumental problem that justified excluding the blurred plates. The conclusion has not been overturned.

The clean version spread because the announcement was a public event built around a hero. A newspaper can report that a famous theory passed a famous test. It cannot easily report error bars or a discarded set of plates. The opposite story, that Eddington simply picked the answer he wanted, spread for a similar reason: a scandal is also a good shape.

The safe statement sits between them. The 1919 eclipse gave the first major observational confirmation of general relativity, and its result has survived later checking. How its data were handled was contested, and the debate is part of the record.

**Tell someone:** The 1919 eclipse results matched Einstein and have largely survived reanalysis, though blurred plates from one Sobral telescope were set aside, a choice historians debated.

*Sources:* "Eddington experiment," Wikipedia. Longair, "Bending space-time: a commentary on Dyson, Eddington and Davidson (1920)," *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A*. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-eddington-eclipse-experiment/

# Round 10: Money, power and policy

Bounties, bombs and bad incentives.

Governments, armies and firms try to steer behavior with rewards, threats and slogans, and the lessons drawn from them are often taught as stories. They include a bounty, a famine, a city that grew safer, a building that ran over budget, a pasture shared by herders. This round checks each story against the record. Some hold up. The ones that do not still teach something, in the way they spread.

## Did a cobra bounty in Delhi breed more cobras?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** No record shows that anyone in Delhi bred cobras for a bounty. The idea the story named rests on firmer evidence, which is the next story in this round.

The popular version is short. British officials in colonial Delhi paid a reward for dead cobras, and local people started breeding cobras to collect it. The German economist Horst Siebert liked the story enough to name an idea after it. His 2001 book *Der Kobra-Effekt* describes how a reward offered to solve a problem can end up paying people to manufacture it.

The trail behind the story is thin. A 2025 review by the Friends of Snakes Society traced it to an 1873 newspaper report on a bounty in the Madras Presidency, not Delhi. That report said only that it "was alleged" that some natives bred cobras for the reward. The review found no contemporary record of a breeding operation or of anyone prosecuted for one.

The story spread because it is the ideal shape for a lesson. It has a plan, a twist and a moral that fits in one sentence. It is also set far enough away, and long enough ago, that few retellers went looking for the source. Each version cited the one before, and repetition did the work that evidence should have done.

The idea itself holds up, and its best evidence is a bounty on rats paid in French colonial Hanoi, which is the subject of the next question in this round. Later cases follow the same pattern. Mexico City's 1989 driving ban took each car off the road one weekday a week by license plate. Households bought a second car, often older and dirtier, with a different plate; the economist Lucas Davis found no gain in air quality.

So the Cobra Effect is real and the cobras are folklore. A reward tied to something people can produce will eventually be met by people producing it.

**Tell someone:** The cobra-breeding story, usually set in Delhi, traces to an 1873 report on a Madras bounty saying only that it "was alleged," yet an economist named an effect after it.

*Sources:* Siebert, *Der Kobra-Effekt* (2001). Friends of Snakes Society review (2025), citing the *Morning Advertiser*, 23 September 1873. Davis, *Journal of Political Economy* 116 (2008). Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/cobra-effect/

## Did a French bounty on rat tails in Hanoi backfire?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** In 1902 the French colonial administration in Hanoi paid a bounty for each rat killed, with a severed tail as proof. Rat-catchers began cutting the tails off live rats and letting them go to breed. The case is documented in a 2003 paper by M. G. Vann in *French Colonial History*.

The popular version is the same story, which is the surprise. Paying for tails produced rats without tails, and rats without tails went on producing rats. It sounds too neat to be true, and it shares a shape with the Delhi cobra story, which the record treats as folklore.

The record backs the rat version. Vann's paper, "Of Rats, Rice, and Race: The Great Hanoi Rat Massacre, an Episode in French Colonial History," appeared in *French Colonial History* in 2003. The Law Tome record calls the Hanoi bounty the documented case behind the cobra effect. Rat-catchers cut the tails off live rats and released them, so the animals could breed and supply more tails to sell.

The mechanism is plain once it is named. A bounty pays for a measurable result on the assumption that the result can only come from doing the wanted thing. Here the result was a tail, and a tail did not require a dead rat. When the thing being counted can be produced instead of removed, the reward funds its production.

The same setup has recurred. Wells Fargo set aggressive cross-selling targets, and employees opened millions of accounts that customers never authorized; the bank paid US$185 million in penalties in 2016 and fired thousands of staff. DigitalOcean's 2020 Hacktoberfest rewarded a set number of GitHub pull requests, and after a viral how-to, maintainers were buried in spam submissions.

The record is careful about limits. The effect is a warning about one design flaw, not a claim that incentives backfire in general; most work as intended. It needs a proxy that the people collecting the reward can manufacture, and a sponsor who does not control its supply. Schemes that pay for verified outcomes and check where the proof came from avoid it.

The rat story is less famous than the cobra because the cobra got the name. Siebert's 2001 book took its title from the snake story, so the better-sourced case became the footnote. Its resemblance to a famous piece of folklore then made it look like folklore too.

It is not. Hanoi's 1902 rat bounty is the documented example of a reward that bred the problem it paid to end.

**Tell someone:** Hanoi's 1902 rat bounty took tails as proof, so rat-catchers cut the tails off live rats and released them to breed.

*Sources:* Vann, "Of Rats, Rice, and Race," *French Colonial History* 4 (2003). Siebert, *Der Kobra-Effekt* (2001). "Perverse incentive," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/cobra-effect/

## Did more efficient steam engines make Britain burn more coal?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** In *The Coal Question* (1865), the economist William Stanley Jevons observed that England's coal use soared after more efficient steam engines expanded coal's industrial uses.

The popular version is an assumption rather than a story. A more efficient engine burns less coal for each job, so a country with better engines should burn less coal.

Jevons said the opposite, and bluntly: "It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth." His case was James Watt's more efficient steam engines. They lowered the cost of coal-powered work, which pushed coal into far more industries and raised total British coal consumption in the nineteenth century.

The mechanism is now called the rebound effect. An efficiency gain lowers the price of each unit of service, so people use more of it. If demand responds strongly enough, the extra use outweighs the saving per unit and total consumption rises. A rebound above 100 percent is called backfire, and that is the full Jevons paradox. At the scale of a whole economy, the Khazzoom–Brookes postulate, named by Harry Saunders in 1992, adds that efficiency raises incomes and growth, which lifts demand further.

The record keeps the claim narrow. In many cases the rebound is only partial, so efficiency still saves something. Fuel-efficient cars lower the cost per mile and encourage more driving, but usually not enough to erase the gain. Lighting may be a stronger case: a Sandia study led by Jeff Tsao projected that cheaper solid-state lighting could raise lighting consumption roughly tenfold by 2030. Economists still debate how large and how common rebound is, and evidence for economy-wide backfire is genuine but contested.

The efficiency assumption persists because it is true where people look. Per unit, a better engine does burn less. The rebound happens across a whole economy, out of sight of any single machine, as new uses appear that the old cost ruled out. Jevons named the mistake in his own phrase: a confusion of ideas.

For coal in nineteenth-century Britain, the record supports Jevons. Better engines made coal cheaper to use, and the country burned more of it.

**Tell someone:** Jevons observed in 1865 that more efficient steam engines made coal cheaper to use, so Britain found new uses and burned more of it.

*Sources:* Jevons, *The Coal Question* (1865). "Jevons paradox," Wikipedia. "Khazzoom–Brookes postulate," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/jevons-paradox/

## Did Irish famine potatoes sell more as they got dearer?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The first real-world evidence of Giffen behavior comes from rice in Hunan, in a 2008 study by Jensen and Miller, not from Ireland. The famine potato, long the textbook example, has been discredited: Rosen's 1999 study of Irish price and quantity data concluded that 1840s potatoes were not Giffen goods.

The popular version is the textbook one: during the famine, potatoes were the classic case of a good bought more as its price rose.

A Giffen good breaks the basic law of demand. Its consumption rises when its price rises, giving it an upward-sloping demand curve over some range. The idea is named after the Scottish economist Sir Robert Giffen, to whom Alfred Marshall attributed it in his *Principles of Economics*. Marshall's own example, as the record gives it, was bread: a rise in its price could drain poor families' budgets so far that they cut back on meat and bought still more bread.

Three conditions must hold at once. The good must be inferior, bought less as people grow richer. It must take a large share of a poor household's budget. And it must lack close substitutes. When its price rises, the household is poorer in real terms, cannot afford the costlier foods it would otherwise buy, and fills the gap with more of the staple.

For a long time no real-world case was firmly documented, and some economists doubted any existed. Jensen and Miller's field study, published in the *American Economic Review* in 2008, found strong evidence of Giffen behavior for rice among poor households in Hunan, and weaker evidence for wheat in Gansu. They present it as the first real-world evidence of such behavior. The same paper says the potato example "has also been discredited (Rosen, 1999)," adds that aggregate potato consumption could hardly have risen when blight destroyed the crop, and traces the potato version to the 1964 edition of Samuelson's textbook.

The potato story spread because it fits the conditions on paper. A famine supplies a staple, poor households and few alternatives, all in a setting most readers already know. Textbooks needed an example, and for decades there was no documented one to give, so a plausible historical case filled the space and was repeated from book to book.

The potato case does not survive the data. Giffen goods are real but rare, and their clearest documented case is rice in Hunan, reported by Jensen and Miller in 2008.

**Tell someone:** The textbook Giffen good, Irish famine potatoes, has been discredited; the first real-world evidence came from rice among poor households in Hunan.

*Sources:* Jensen and Miller, "Giffen Behavior and Subsistence Consumption," *American Economic Review* (2008). Rosen, "Potato Paradoxes," *Journal of Political Economy* 107 (1999). "Giffen good," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/giffen-good/

## Did broken-windows policing cause New York's 1990s crime drop?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The claim that broken-windows policing caused New York's crime decline is disputed and confounded. Crime fell across most American cities in the 1990s, including cities that never adopted the approach, and a 2006 analysis by Harcourt and Ludwig found little causal support for the crime-reduction claim.

The popular version credits the windows. New York fixed the small things, arrested fare-dodgers and cleaned off graffiti, and the city became safe.

The theory came first. James Q. Wilson and George Kelling set it out in "Broken Windows," in *The Atlantic Monthly* in 1982. Visible disorder, they argued, signals that a place is unwatched, which invites more disorder and eventually serious crime. They drew on Philip Zimbardo's abandoned-car experiments. A car left without plates was stripped within a day in the Bronx but sat untouched for a week in Palo Alto, until Zimbardo smashed it himself and passersby joined in.

In 1990s New York, order-maintenance policing under Bratton and Giuliani targeted fare evasion and graffiti. Proponents credited it with the city's crime decline.

The record treats that credit as unproven. Crime fell nationally in the 1990s, amid the end of the crack epidemic, economic growth, more police and other changes, including in cities that never used the policy. Harcourt and Ludwig's analysis of a five-city housing experiment found that families moved to neighborhoods with less disorder did not commit fewer crimes.

One narrow part of the theory has support. In field experiments published in *Science* in 2008, Keizer, Lindenberg and Steg found that visible graffiti and litter increased other rule-breaking, including littering and petty theft. That supports disorder spreading disorder. It does not show escalation to serious crime, or that a policing program prevents it. A rival account from Sampson and Raudenbush holds that disorder and crime may both stem from weak neighborhood cohesion. Kelling's own version also stressed officer discretion, unlike the harder zero-tolerance policing often applied in its name.

The simple version spread because the timing lines up. One city, one visible policy and one falling crime rate sit side by side, and coincidence in time is easily read as cause. A national decline is hard to see from inside a single city, and the image of one broken window is easy to remember.

The record supports a narrower claim. Minor disorder breeds more minor disorder. Whether broken-windows policing caused New York's 1990s crime drop is disputed, because crime fell in places that never tried it.

**Tell someone:** Crime fell across most American cities in the 1990s, including ones that never tried broken-windows policing, so its credit for New York's drop remains disputed.

*Sources:* Wilson and Kelling, "Broken Windows," *The Atlantic Monthly* (1982). Keizer, Lindenberg and Steg, *Science* 322 (2008). Harcourt and Ludwig, *University of Chicago Law Review* 73(1) (2006). Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-broken-windows-theory/

## Did Nixon really have a "madman theory"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Nixon did put the strategy into practice: the National Security Archive documents the secret JCS Readiness Test of October 1969 as his test of the madman approach. The caveat is the label. The name "madman theory" and its founding quote rest on one source, H. R. Haldeman's 1978 memoir *The Ends of Power*.

The popular version has Nixon telling aides he wanted the enemy to think he was crazy enough to use the bomb.

The record traces that to Haldeman, Nixon's chief of staff. By Haldeman's account, Nixon said during a walk on a beach that he wanted the North Vietnamese to believe he might do anything to end the war. They should think he was obsessed and had his hand on the nuclear button, so that they would beg for peace. This is a reported conversation, known from one participant's memoir, and Nixon later told the historian Joan Hoff that he did not remember using the term.

The strategy itself has documentary support beyond the memoir. Burr and Kimball's National Security Archive briefing book (2002) sets out the evidence for the October 1969 readiness test. It cites Haldeman's diary for 17 October 1969 on Nixon's "signal-type activity going around the world to try to jar Soviets + NVN." The same year Nixon and Kissinger drew up an escalation plan called Duck Hook, later aborted. These moves are widely judged to have failed to coerce Hanoi or Moscow.

The idea itself is much older than Nixon. Machiavelli praised Lucius Junius Brutus for feigning folly to survive and overthrow the Tarquins, calling it wise to simulate madness at the right time. Its Cold War theory belongs to Thomas Schelling and Herman Kahn. Schelling showed that a bargainer can gain by worsening his own options, and by a threat that leaves something to chance. The rationality of irrationality, associated with Kahn, holds that a suicidal threat is credible only if the threatener is believed irrational. Schelling did not name or endorse Nixon's version.

The Nixon story spread because it has an insider, a setting and an image. A chief of staff's memoir carries authority, and a president's hand on the nuclear button is hard to forget. It also gave a short name to a strategy that scholars had described in abstract terms. "Madman" never meant mental illness; it meant a performance staged for leverage.

The label "madman theory" rests on Haldeman's 1978 account, and Nixon said he did not remember using it. The strategy does not rest on the label. The logic came from Schelling, Kahn and Machiavelli, and Nixon tested it in October 1969, without success.

**Tell someone:** Nixon did test a madman strategy, with a secret military readiness test in October 1969; only the label "madman theory" rests on Haldeman's memoir, and Nixon said he did not remember using it.

*Sources:* Burr and Kimball, "Nixon's Nuclear Ploy," National Security Archive Briefing Book 81 (2002). Hoff, *Nixon Reconsidered*. "Madman theory," Wikipedia. McManus, "Madman or Mad Genius? The International Benefits and Domestic Costs of the Madman Strategy," Belfer Center. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-madman-theory/

## Did the domino theory get its name at a 1954 press conference?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** President Dwight D. Eisenhower's press conference on Indochina on April 7, 1954, made the falling-dominoes image famous, but it did not clearly give the theory its name. He introduced it as "what you would call the 'falling domino' principle," which suggests the phrase was already current, and the reasoning behind it was older, explicit in the 1947 Truman Doctrine.

The popular version gives the theory a single birthday: Eisenhower coined the name at a press conference in 1954.

The press conference is real and dated. Eisenhower said that if the first domino in a row is knocked over, the last will fall quickly, and used the picture to explain why Indochina mattered to the whole region. His own framing, "what you would call," points to a phrase others were already using, and the columnists Joseph and Stewart Alsop reportedly claimed to have coined "domino theory" themselves. The record says he popularized the image, which is not the same as naming it.

The idea itself came first. The reasoning behind the 1947 Truman Doctrine, that abandoning Greece and Turkey would let communism spread onward, is the same logic without the dominoes. After 1954 the theory became a central justification for deepening American military involvement in Vietnam, and it shaped US strategy across four administrations.

Its prediction was then tested. In 1975 Cambodia and Laos did become communist along with South Vietnam, a limited local toppling. The forecast collapse of Thailand, Indonesia and beyond did not happen, and several of those states grew stronger. Critics fault the theory for treating different nations as interchangeable pieces and for ignoring the nationalism that set communist states against one another, as with China and Vietnam. Singapore's Lee Kuan Yew offered a partial defense: US intervention, however costly, bought time for the non-communist states of the region to stabilize. The record calls the theory largely discredited but still debated.

The single-birthday version spread because a president, a date and a vivid picture make a cleaner origin than a phrase drifting through columns and briefings. A transcript is easy to cite, and the earlier uses it hints at are not. The theory also has two starting points, the 1947 logic and the 1954 image, and the famous one tends to absorb the credit.

The reasoning dates from 1947, and the name was apparently already current when Eisenhower spoke. What he supplied on April 7, 1954, was the image that made the phrase famous.

**Tell someone:** Eisenhower made the falling-dominoes image famous on April 7, 1954, but he called it "what you would call the 'falling domino' principle," a sign the name was already around.

## Did the Sydney Opera House open a decade late and vastly over budget?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The Sydney Opera House was estimated to open in 1963 at a cost of a few million pounds. A scaled-down version opened in 1973 at A$102 million. The exact figures vary by source.

The popular version holds the Opera House up as the classic runaway project. Famous stories of waste tend to grow in the retelling, so it is fair to suspect this one has too.

It has not grown much. The record calls the building the archetypal runaway public project. Its opening came ten years after the estimate, and the finished building was smaller than planned. The figures vary by source, and by whether they are stated in the money of the day or adjusted for inflation, so they are best kept as ranges: a few million at the start, more than a hundred million at the end.

The Opera House is the best-known case of the planning fallacy, a term coined by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1979. It is the tendency to underestimate how long a task will take, even when similar past tasks have overrun. In a 1994 study by Buehler, Griffin and Ross, students predicted they would finish their honors theses in about 34 days on average, and in about 49 if everything went as badly as possible. They took about 55, and fewer than a third finished within their own estimate.

Large projects show the same pattern in money. In 2002 Flyvbjerg, Holm and Buhl studied 258 transport projects worth some $90 billion. Actual costs averaged about 28 percent above estimate, and roughly nine in ten were underestimated. Flyvbjerg argues that for megaprojects the cause is often not honest optimism but strategic misrepresentation: promoters low-ball costs to win approval. The record treats that as a rival explanation, not a settled one.

The story spread because the building is famous and the gap is easy to state: a decade and a large multiple of the budget. The variation between sources is real, and it invites doubt. But it concerns the precise numbers, not the shape of the story.

The record supports the popular version. Planned for 1963 at a few million pounds, the Sydney Opera House opened in scaled-down form in 1973 at A$102 million.

**Tell someone:** The Sydney Opera House opened a decade late, in 1973, scaled down and costing more than a hundred million, though exact figures vary by source.

## Did Hardin later admit the tragedy of the commons was missing a word?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** Garrett Hardin later conceded that his error had been to omit one word: "unmanaged." His tragedy describes an unmanaged, open-access resource, not every shared one.

The popular version treats the tragedy as an iron law: any resource held in common will be ruined unless it is privatized or controlled by the state.

The argument first appeared as "The Tragedy of the Commons" in *Science* in 1968, given as a presidential address to the Pacific Division of the AAAS. Its logic rests on herders sharing a pasture. Adding one more cow gives a herder its full benefit, while the cost of overgrazing is divided among everyone, so each private calculation favors adding more. That works where no one can be kept out and where one person's use leaves less for others.

The record states the correction. Hardin himself later conceded that his error was omitting the word "unmanaged." Historical commons were in fact regulated by custom and law to prevent overgrazing. The model fits an open-access resource with no rules, which is a different thing.

Elinor Ostrom's fieldwork then showed what managed commons can do. In *Governing the Commons* (1990), she documented communities that govern shared resources successfully through their own institutions, without privatizing or nationalizing them. She won the 2009 Nobel Prize. The depletion mechanism is real where a resource is truly open: the record points to overfished cod on the Grand Banks and to the atmosphere as a sink for greenhouse gases. What the record rejects is the claim that ruin is inevitable.

The iron-law reading spread because the essay was short, prominent and memorable, and the qualifier that limits it is a single word, easy to drop. The concession came thirty years later, in a 1998 essay, so it never traveled with the original. The name itself also invites the misreading. It borrows the word "commons" from exactly the kind of regulated pasture to which the model does not apply.

Hardin's own correction is the safe statement. The tragedy belongs to the unmanaged commons, and Ostrom showed that managed ones need not end in ruin.

**Tell someone:** Garrett Hardin later conceded his famous essay left out one word, "unmanaged," and Elinor Ostrom documented communities that manage shared resources successfully.

*Sources:* Hardin, "The Tragedy of the Commons," *Science* 162 (1968). Hardin, "Extensions of 'The Tragedy of the Commons,'" *Science* 280 (1998). "Tragedy of the commons," Wikipedia. "Elinor Ostrom," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-tragedy-of-the-commons/

## Did Garrett Hardin invent the overgrazed-pasture parable?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The overgrazed-pasture parable came from William Forster Lloyd in 1833. Garrett Hardin named the idea and popularized it in 1968, 135 years later.

The popular version gives the herders to Hardin. His 1968 essay in *Science*, on this telling, introduced the picture of herders overgrazing a common pasture.

The record splits the credit. Hardin's paper, "The Tragedy of the Commons," appeared in *Science* 162 in 1968 and was given as a presidential address to the Pacific Division of the AAAS. It gave the idea its name and its wide audience. But the parable itself came earlier, from Lloyd in 1833. Lloyd's version appeared in *Two Lectures on the Checks to Population*, and Hardin's essay credits it by name.

The parable is simple, which helps explain why it outlived its author. A pasture is open to every herder. Each extra cow brings its owner the full benefit, while the cost of the grass it eats is shared among all. So every herder keeps adding cows, and the pasture is ruined. It needs two conditions: no one can be kept out, and one animal's grazing leaves less for the rest.

The idea has since been tested against real commons. Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) challenged the claim that such ruin is inevitable, by documenting communities that manage shared resources well. The record presents Hardin's 1968 essay as the version that named and spread the parable, and Lloyd's 1833 version as its source.

The misattribution spread because names stick to whoever makes an idea famous. Hardin supplied the phrase people repeat, "the tragedy of the commons," in a prominent journal, and the frame went on to shape debates over pollution, conservation and property rights. A memorable title tends to absorb the history behind it. It is the same drift met in the round on names that mislead: the person remembered is the one who popularized the idea, not the one who first told it.

The parable is older than its name. Lloyd described the overgrazed pasture in 1833, and Hardin gave it the title it carries in 1968.

**Tell someone:** The overgrazed-pasture parable came from William Forster Lloyd in 1833; Garrett Hardin gave it its famous name 135 years later.

## Using this book in class

{{ornament}}

Each story is a short exercise in checking a claim. A lesson can take one story in about fifteen minutes:

1. Read the question aloud. Students vote and write one reason for their vote.
2. Ask what evidence would settle it: who would have to have written what, and when?
3. Read the verdict and the story. Compare it with the students' reasons.
4. Discuss why the myth spread. Each answer names the mistake or motive behind it.

Rounds 3 (Famous experiments, retold) and 4 (Charts and numbers) suit psychology and statistics classes; rounds 1, 6 and 9 suit science; rounds 2 and 7 suit history and English.
