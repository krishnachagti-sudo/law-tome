## Did Henry Kissinger say academic politics are vicious because the stakes are so small?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** The line has been credited to Henry Kissinger, but he is one of several claimants, and none has a clean title. The record calls the attribution famously tangled. Its usual namesake is the Columbia political scientist Wallace Sayre, and Quote Investigator traces the idea back to Samuel Johnson in 1765.

The popular version is a Kissinger line: academic politics are so vicious because the stakes are so small.

The record lists the claimants in order. Quote Investigator's 2013 history begins with Samuel Johnson in 1765, who remarked that commentators quarreling over matters of small importance wrote with more venom than political writers. Its earliest full statement of the academic quip comes in a 1969 speech by Charles Frankel, who credited no one. Wallace Stanley Sayre (1905 to 1972), a political scientist at Columbia University, is said to have used the quip from around the 1950s. The best-known version of the corollary, that academic politics are so vicious "because the stakes are so low," was recorded in the *Wall Street Journal* in 1973. The same year the economist Charles Philip Issawi published the maxim as "Sayre's Law." Versions have also been credited to Kissinger, Woodrow Wilson and Laurence Peter.

The general form of the law is broader than the academic quip. It holds that "in any dispute the intensity of feeling is inversely proportional to the value of the issues at stake." The record treats that as a humorous aphorism, not a measured finding. It plainly fails as a universal rule, since wars are high-stakes and fiercely emotional.

The attribution stays tangled for three reasons the record supports. Sayre's own use is reported ("is said to have used") rather than documented. The line circulated in more than one wording, "so low" and "so small," which makes a single first source harder to fix. And a sharp remark about academics sounds more authoritative under a famous name, which is the pull Kissinger's name exerts. The record notes the irony itself: a small dispute over a small saying.

The safe statement is limited. The earliest dated source in the record for the idea is Samuel Johnson in 1765. The academic version was in circulation by 1969 and is usually called Sayre's law. Kissinger is a later claimant whose authorship the record does not establish.

**Tell someone:** Kissinger is only one claimant to the academic-politics quip; Quote Investigator traces the idea back to Samuel Johnson in 1765.

*Sources:* Quote Investigator, "Academic Politics Are So Vicious Because the Stakes Are So Small" (2013). "Sayre's law," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/sayres-law/

## Did Stigler credit Stigler's law to someone else?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** In the 1980 paper that named the law after himself, the statistician Stephen Stigler credited its discovery to the sociologist Robert K. Merton. The law says no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer, so it was built to obey itself.

The popular assumption is the natural one. A law with a person's name on it was found by that person, and Stigler's law of eponymy belongs to Stigler.

The record shows a joke with a citation. Stigler set out the law in a paper titled "Stigler's Law of Eponymy," published in the *Transactions of the New York Academy of Sciences* and in a festschrift for Merton. He credited the true discovery to Merton, an intentional self-application. He grounded the law in Merton's earlier work on priority in science and on the Matthew effect, the pattern in which recognition flows to scientists who are already eminent. On that account an established name becomes the lasting label, while an originator who published obscurely, or without follow-through, is forgotten.

The record supplies cases. The normal distribution was first derived by Abraham de Moivre in a 1733 pamphlet, some 76 years before Gauss's 1809 work, yet it carries Gauss's name. The Pythagorean relation was known to Babylonian mathematicians centuries before Pythagoras, who may never have proved it himself. A version for mathematics alone, Boyer's law, was named by Hubert Kennedy after Carl Boyer, and so confirms its own claim as well.

The self-reference works because the assumption it mocks is so strong. A name repeated often enough reads as a credit line, and the record's account of eponymy is that a community attaches the name it hears most often. Stigler made that habit the subject of the law, then let the law's own title demonstrate it. The "no discovery" wording is deliberately hyperbolic. Plenty of results, such as Newton's laws and Planck's constant, are correctly named for their discoverers. The law is a wry observation about how communities remember, not a measured rate of misattribution or a charge that scientists steal credit.

So the joke is by design and on the record. Stigler's 1980 paper puts his own name on the law and hands its discovery to Merton.

**Tell someone:** Stigler named Stigler's law after himself and then credited its discovery to Robert K. Merton, so the law obeys itself.

*Sources:* Stigler, "Stigler's Law of Eponymy," *Transactions of the New York Academy of Sciences* 39 (1980), also in Gieryn, ed., *Science and Social Structure: A Festschrift for Robert K. Merton* (1980). "Stigler's law of eponymy," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/stiglers-law/

## Did Say write "supply creates its own demand"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** The phrase "supply creates its own demand" does not appear in Jean-Baptiste Say's own writing. John Maynard Keynes popularized it in 1936, and even Keynes seems to have been quoting a phrase already current.

The popular version treats the slogan as Say's law in Say's words: a French economist's claim that whatever is produced will find a buyer.

Say's own statement came in his *Treatise on Political Economy* (1803), as the *loi des débouchés*, the law of markets. James Mill and John Stuart Mill made related statements. The claim is subtler than the slogan. Producing generates the income to buy. A baker who bakes a hundred loaves pays workers, suppliers and himself enough to buy those loaves and other goods too. On that reasoning a general glut, an oversupply of everything at once, cannot last. Say himself accepted that particular markets can glut. The disputed part is only the impossibility of a general one.

Keynes's central attack, in *The General Theory* (1936), was that demand can fall short of full-employment output because money can be hoarded rather than spent or invested. General gluts and involuntary unemployment are then possible, and markets need not correct themselves promptly. The 1930s became the great real-world argument. Keynesians read mass unemployment and unsold output as proof that the law fails, while its defenders held that markets self-correct in the long run. Both the law and its refutation remain live in macroeconomic debate.

The slogan stuck because of how it entered that debate. It reached its widest audience through the law's most famous critic, in the same year as his attack on it. A five-word paraphrase is easier to remember than Say's argument, and easier to refute. Where Keynes found it is not settled: the record says the phrase's exact origin is unclear.

The fact stands apart from the argument over who was right. Say set out the law of markets in 1803. The words that now stand for it are a later paraphrase, popularized by Keynes in 1936.

**Tell someone:** "Supply creates its own demand" is not in Say's writing; Keynes popularized it in 1936, and even he seems to have been quoting it.

*Sources:* Say, *A Treatise on Political Economy* (1803). Keynes, *The General Theory* (1936), Chapter 2. "Say's law," Wikipedia. "Supply creates its own demand," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/says-law/

## Was "moving the goalposts" used in Parliament a century ago?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**TRUE.** The earliest documented figurative use of the phrase is in a House of Commons debate on 2 May 1924. The idiom is British, and it was in political speech some sixty years before it came into widespread use in the 1980s.

The popular assumption dates the phrase to the 1980s and files it with that decade's business jargon.

The record moves the date back. In the 1924 debate the Labour MP George Hardie said the Liberals, having lost, "wanted to shift the goal posts because they could not play any more." The verb was "shift," not "move," but the figure is the one still in use: changing the rules after the other side has met them. The metaphor comes from goal-based sports, where relocating a goal after play has begun would be plainly unfair.

The 1980s did matter. That is when the phrase entered widespread use. In September 1987 the UK Chancellor, Nigel Lawson, was quoted saying, "I see no reason to move the goalposts at all," which shows the idiom in mainstream political use by then.

The phrase has a precise meaning that is often stretched. It refers to changing the success criterion after that criterion has been met, so the goal stays out of reach. Updating a view in light of new evidence is something else, and so is raising a standard that was too weak to settle the question in the first place.

The 1980s date spread for an ordinary reason. People tend to date a phrase from when they first noticed it, and most English speakers noticed this one when it became common. A Commons debate from 1924 is not where anyone goes looking.

Sport has since supplied a literal case. In a 2014 American Hockey League game, according to the published account, the goaltender David Leggio deliberately dislodged his own goalpost during a breakaway to stop a goal. The incident prompted rule changes, and by 2019 the NHL awarded automatic goals in such cases.

The figurative phrase, however, is older than any of that. Its first documented use is in the House of Commons on 2 May 1924.

**Tell someone:** In the House of Commons on 2 May 1924, the Labour MP George Hardie said the Liberals "wanted to shift the goal posts."

*Sources:* wordhistories.net, "the phrase 'to move the goalposts', as used in Britain" (citing *The Scotsman*, 3 May 1924). phrases.org.uk, "Move the goalposts" (citing *The Gleaner*, September 1987). "Moving the goalposts," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/moving-the-goalposts/

## Did Weber write "iron cage"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** Max Weber did not write "iron cage." The English phrase is Talcott Parsons's 1930 translation of Weber's German *stahlhartes Gehäuse*, which more literally means a shell or casing as hard as steel.

The popular version quotes "the iron cage" as Weber's own words, his image of modern people locked inside bureaucracy.

The idea is Weber's; the words are the translator's. The metaphor appears near the end of *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*, published as essays in 1904 and 1905. Weber feared that rationalization and bureaucracy would trap people inside a system of efficiency, calculation and impersonal control. He argued that capitalism, once established, no longer needed the religious roots of its disciplined ethic and became a self-sustaining order. He worried it would produce, in the record's English rendering of his words, "specialists without spirit and sensualists without heart."

The translation changes the picture. The scholar Peter Baehr, writing in *History and Theory* in 2001, argued that "steel-hard casing" is more faithful. Steel is a human fabrication, both hard and flexible. A casing suggests a person reshaped by rationalized capitalism. A cage merely confines someone who is otherwise intact. On Baehr's reading, Weber's image is of people altered by the system, not just shut in by it.

The record adds that the phrase is a metaphor and a warning, not a precise empirical law, and that how inescapable modern life really is remains open to debate. Nor is it a flat condemnation of bureaucracy: Weber saw rationalization as powerful and potentially dehumanizing at once.

"Iron cage" spread because it is vivid and because, in English, it was the version on the page. A strong two-word image is easy to quote, and once quoted it carries the author's name rather than the translator's. Attributing the exact words to Weber is, in the record's word, imprecise.

So the concept belongs to Weber, from essays of 1904 and 1905. The phrase "iron cage" belongs to Talcott Parsons, in 1930.

**Tell someone:** Weber did not write "iron cage"; it is Talcott Parsons's 1930 translation of a German phrase more literally meaning a steel-hard casing.

*Sources:* Weber, *The Protestant Ethic*, trans. Parsons (1930), Chapter 5. Baehr, "The 'Iron Cage' and the 'Shell as Hard as Steel'," *History and Theory* 40(2) (2001). "Iron cage," Wikipedia. Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/the-iron-cage/

## Did Philip Greenspun write rules one to nine?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** There are no rules one to nine. The "tenth" in Greenspun's Tenth Rule is itself the joke; Philip Greenspun has said he just wanted a memorable name.

The popular version takes the number at face value: the rule is the tenth item on a list of programming laws, and the other nine are out there somewhere.

The record shows a single rule with a borrowed rank. Greenspun coined it in the early-to-mid 1990s. It is commonly dated to around 1993, but he says he no longer recalls where he first wrote it, so the date is approximate. The rule holds, in paraphrase, that any big enough C or Fortran program contains a bad reimplementation of half of Lisp. The exact punctuation of the line varies between sources.

The claim is not that such programs literally contain Lisp. It is that large programs written in low-level languages tend to reinvent, informally and badly, the features Lisp provides natively: garbage collection, dynamic dispatch, first-class functions, an interactive loop and runtime metaprogramming. The record's illustrations include configuration languages that creep toward full programming languages and applications that bolt on scripting engines once compiled code proves too rigid.

A corollary, attributed to Robert Morris, adds "...including Common Lisp." The point is that real Lisp implementations often rest on a compiled low-level core, so they too contain the ad hoc half of Lisp. The record calls the corollary well-attested folklore rather than a dated source. It also calls the rule itself a Lisp-advocacy in-joke, unfalsifiable as stated, since "sufficiently complicated" and "half of" are undefined.

The missing nine are a natural inference. An ordinal implies a sequence, and readers reasonably went looking for the rest. A rule with a number also sounds as though it belongs to a codified body of knowledge, which lends a joke the weight of a statute.

The record is plain. Greenspun wrote one rule, around the early 1990s, and numbered it tenth for the sake of a memorable name.

**Tell someone:** Greenspun's Tenth Rule is the only one; there are no rules one to nine, and Greenspun has said he just wanted a memorable name.

*Sources:* "Greenspun's tenth rule," Wikipedia. philip.greenspun.com, author's page. Greenspun, "10th rule of programming," philip.greenspun.com forum (27 September 2003). Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/greenspuns-tenth-rule/
