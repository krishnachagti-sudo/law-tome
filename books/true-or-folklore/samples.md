## Did a cobra bounty in Delhi breed more cobras?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**FOLKLORE.** No record shows that anyone in Delhi bred cobras for a bounty. The documented case involves rats, in Hanoi, in 1902.

The popular version is short. British officials in colonial Delhi paid a reward for dead cobras, and local people started breeding cobras to collect it. The German economist Horst Siebert liked the story enough to name an idea after it. His 2001 book *Der Kobra-Effekt* describes how a reward offered to solve a problem can end up paying people to manufacture it.

The trail behind the story is thin. A 2025 review by the Friends of Snakes Society traced it to an 1873 newspaper report. That report said only that it "was alleged" that natives bred cobras for the reward. The review found no contemporary record of a breeding operation or of anyone prosecuted for one.

The story spread because it is the ideal shape for a lesson. It has a plan, a twist and a moral that fits in one sentence. It is also set far enough away, and long enough ago, that few retellers went looking for the source. Each version cited the one before, and repetition did the work that evidence should have done.

The idea itself holds up, and its best evidence is the Hanoi case. In 1902 the French colonial administration paid a bounty for every rat killed, with a severed tail as proof. Rat-catchers began cutting the tails off live rats and letting them go to breed, so there would be more tails to sell. Later cases follow the same pattern. Mexico City's 1989 driving ban took each car off the road one weekday a week by licence plate. Households bought a second car, often older and dirtier, with a different plate; the economist Lucas Davis found no gain in air quality.

So the Cobra Effect is real and the cobras are folklore. A reward tied to something people can produce will eventually be met by people producing it. The documented example is a rat without a tail.

*Sources:* Siebert, *Der Kobra-Effekt* (2001). Friends of Snakes Society review (2025), citing an 1873 newspaper report. Vann, "Of Rats, Rice, and Race," *French Colonial History* 4 (2003). Davis, *Journal of Political Economy* 116 (2008). Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/cobra-effect/

## Did Edward Murphy say "Anything that can go wrong will go wrong"?

TRUE · FOLKLORE · DISPUTED?

*(turn the page)*

**DISPUTED.** A maxim like it came out of an Air Force rocket-sled project around 1949, and Murphy's name stuck to it. Who first said it, and in what words, is not settled. The earliest firm printed version, from 1952, credits an unnamed physicist.

The popular version has an engineer called Murphy, annoyed by a botched job, coining the law on the spot.

The record is more tangled. Around 1949 Edward A. Murphy Jr., an aerospace engineer, worked on project MX981, a series of rocket-sled deceleration tests at what became Edwards Air Force Base. In one run, strain-gauge transducers wired to a harness returned zero readings because they had been installed the wrong way round. A part that could be fitted two ways had been fitted the wrong way. The project's lead, Colonel John Stapp, then popularized the maxim at a press conference. He framed it as a strength: the team's habit of designing against every failure it could imagine.

The wording is where the trail breaks. Murphy's son and his colleagues later recalled materially different phrasings, and the accounts of the 1949 events are anecdotal. The earliest firm appearance in print of "if anything can go wrong, it will" is in Anne Roe's 1952 book *The Making of a Scientist*. Roe credits it to a physicist she does not name.

The gloomy reading won because the law is so easy to confirm. Across enough events some failures are statistically certain, which the statistician David Hand calls the law of truly large numbers. People remember the toast that landed butter-side down and forget the far more numerous times nothing went wrong. That turned a working engineer's rule into a cosmic joke.

The safe statement is narrower than the legend. A rule about failure came out of the MX981 tests around 1949 and carries Murphy's name. Its first wording is contested, and its first firm appearance in print is 1952. What it meant to the people who used it was practical: if a part can be fitted the wrong way, design it so it can't be.

*Sources:* Spark, *A History of Murphy's Law* (2006). "The Real-Life Murphy and How 'Murphy's Law' Came to Be," Military.com. Roe, *The Making of a Scientist* (1952). Full list: conyso.com/lawtome/laws/murphys-law/
