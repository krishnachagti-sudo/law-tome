# Five strangers, twenty minutes each

This is the one pre-launch check nothing in the build can do for you. Every
judgement made about this site's usability so far — including all of mine — is
inference from looking at it, and the people who built a thing are the worst
available judges of whether it explains itself.

You need five people. Not five *users* — five people who have never seen it,
ideally who don't work in software, and who owe you nothing. Five is the number
where new problems stop appearing; three will find most of it.

## Rules for whoever runs it

1. **Say nothing.** No "so this is an index of…". Hand them the URL. If they ask
   what it is, say "that's what I'm trying to find out — what do you think?"
2. **Do not help.** When they get stuck, that is the result. Watching someone
   fail for ninety seconds is worth more than the whole rest of the exercise.
3. **Ask them to think aloud.** "Tell me what you're looking at, and what you
   expect to happen before you click."
4. **Write down the exact words they use.** If four people say "definitions" and
   the site says "entries", the site is wrong, not the four people.
5. **Time the tasks.** Not to score them — to notice which ones take three times
   longer than you assumed.

## Task 0 — the five-second test

Show the home page for five seconds. Take it away. Ask:

- What is this site for?
- Who is it for?
- What would you do next?

**This is the most important thing on the page.** Everything else can be fixed
later. If four of five can't say what it's for, the positioning is wrong no
matter how good the rest is.

Write down whether anyone mentions the *rating* — that the site says how well
established each idea is. That is the claim the whole front page is now built
on. If nobody notices it, it isn't landing.

## Task 1 — find one thing

"Find out what the Dunning–Kruger effect actually says."

Watch: do they use search, the nav, or leave and use Google? Do they trust the
answer when they get there? Do they scroll past the answer looking for it?

## Task 2 — the promise

"You've got a team that keeps adding people to a late project and it keeps
getting later. Is there a name for that?"

This is the site's distinctive claim — that you can arrive with a problem
instead of a term. It should lead to Brooks's Law via search or `/situations/`
or `/diagnose/`. If they can't get there, the hero promise is unkept.

## Task 3 — the differentiator

"Is the Dunning–Kruger effect actually real? How much would you trust it?"

Watch whether they find the reliability badge on their own, whether they
understand what "Contested" means without clicking through, and whether they
believe it. Ask directly afterwards: **"who decided that?"** Their answer tells
you whether the trust argument works. It is the single most load-bearing and
least tested thing on the site.

## Task 4 — depth

"Find something on this site you'd send to a friend."

Open-ended on purpose. Watch where they wander. Anything they find delightful
should be one click from the home page and probably isn't.

## Task 5 — the loop

"Play today's quiz." Then: "would you come back tomorrow?"

Watch whether they understand that the round is the same for everyone, whether
they share the result, and whether the grid means anything to them. If they
don't get the Wordle reference, the whole mechanic is decoration.

## What to write down for each person

- Where they hesitated for more than three seconds
- Every word they used that the site doesn't use
- Every moment they said "oh" — good or bad
- What they thought the site was, at the end, in one sentence

## How to read the results

**Fix anything two or more people hit.** One person struggling is noise; two is
a pattern. Resist the urge to explain why they were wrong — you won't be
standing behind the next thousand visitors.

Rank the findings by where they occur: a problem on the home page costs you
every visitor, a problem four clicks deep costs you the few who got that far.

## What I could not test

Everything above. I have never watched a person use this site, and neither has
anyone else. The build is green, the pages render, the links resolve — none of
which is evidence that a stranger understands what they're looking at.
