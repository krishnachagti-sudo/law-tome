Goodhart's Law

also known as — Goodhart–Strathern
"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."
In plain English

The moment you reward people for hitting a number, they start optimising the number itself — not the thing the number was supposed to stand for. The measure and the goal quietly drift apart.

An example
In practice A call centre starts paying agents by calls handled per hour. Handle-time drops beautifully — but agents now rush people off the line, split one problem into three calls, and hang up on hard cases. The metric soars while the thing it was meant to track — customer problems actually solved — gets worse. The number stopped measuring the goal the instant it became the target.
Why it matters

Any time you set a KPI, an OKR, a bonus, or a ranking, you are turning a proxy into a target — and inviting Goodhart's Law. It's the reason test scores, hospital wait-times, and engagement metrics so often improve on paper while the underlying reality stalls or declines. The defence isn't to abandon measurement, but to watch measures for gaming, use several imperfect ones rather than a single sacred one, and revisit them before they harden into goals.

Origin

The economist Charles Goodhart, then an adviser to the Bank of England, stated it in a 1975 paper on monetary policy: any statistical regularity "will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." He was describing why targeting a particular measure of the money supply stopped working once the Bank tried to steer by it. The now-famous pithy phrasing — "when a measure becomes a target…" — was actually a later generalisation by the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern in 1997, which is why the sharpened form is sometimes called Goodhart–Strathern.

Reliability

Heuristic. A widely-observed regularity in social and economic systems rather than a formally proven law — its force comes from how reliably it recurs wherever incentives meet metrics, not from a controlled result.

Commonly confused with
Campbell's Law The Lucas Critique

Campbell's Law says almost the same thing about social indicators and is the one most often mistaken for this — they were formulated independently, four years apart.
