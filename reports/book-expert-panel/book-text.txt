TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 1 · DID ARCHIMEDES SHOUT “EUREKA!” IN THE BATH?
 FOLKLORE
Archimedes stated the law of buoyancy around 250 BC, in his treatise On 
Floating Bodies . The bath, the crown and the cry of “Eureka!” are almost 
certainly a later embellishment.
The popular version is a single moment. Archimedes stepped into his bath, 
saw the water rise, and realized how to settle a problem about a crown. He ran 
out shouting “Eureka!”
The record separates the principle from the anecdote. The principle is in 
Archimedes’ own work. On Floating Bodies  states that a body in a fluid is 
buoyed up by the weight of the fluid it displaces, and its Proposition 5 gives the 
corollary for floating bodies: a floating object settles until it displaces exactly 
its own weight. That is why a steel hull floats and why a balloon rises when it 
displaces more than its own weight of air. Within its assumptions the principle 
is exact.
The anecdote has a different author. It was first told by Vitruvius, writing 
some two centuries after Archimedes. The method Vitruvius describes, judg-
ing the crown by the water it pushes aside, is crude, and too imprecise to have 
worked. Archimedes more likely used hydrostatic weighing.
The story spread because it gives an abstract law a face and a date. A rule 
about fluid pressure is hard to picture; a man running from his bath is not. It 
also fits a popular idea of discovery as one flash of insight rather than patient 
measurement. Once Vitruvius had written it down, each retelling had a source 
to point to, and nobody needed to ask how a writer two centuries later could 
know what was said in a bathroom.
The physics stands on its own. Archimedes’ principle is one of the oldest 
quantitative results in physics, and the earliest source for it is his own treatise 
of around 250 BC. The anecdote is not evidence for it. The earliest source for 
the bath is Vitruvius.
TELL SOMEONE
Archimedes did state the law of buoyancy, but the bath and the “Eureka!” al-
most certainly come from Vitruvius, writing some two centuries later.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 2 · DID AN APPLE FALL ON NEWTON’S HEAD?
 FOLKLORE
There is an apple in Newton’s own account of his thinking about gravity. It 
does not hit him on the head. That detail is a later embellishment.
The popular version is a piece of slapstick. An apple dropped onto Newton’s 
head, and the idea of gravity arrived with the bump.
The record is plainer. Newton published his law of universal gravitation in 
Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica in 1687, a book whose printing 
Edmond Halley paid for. The law says that any two particles of matter attract 
each other with a force that grows with the product of their masses and falls 
with the square of the distance between them. Robert Hooke had suggested 
an inverse-square attraction in letters, but he could not derive the orbits of 
the planets from it. The mathematical derivation was Newton’s own.
The apple story itself, according to Encyclopaedia Britannica, traces back to 
Newton’s own later recollection. That recollection includes an apple. It does 
not include an apple striking him.
The bump spread because it improves the story as a story. A falling apple 
seen from a distance is a thought. An apple landing on a scientist’s head is 
an event, with a victim and a punchline, and it makes discovery look like 
something that happens to a person by accident. Each retelling had a reason 
to keep the joke and none to check it.
The apple is also a fair symbol of what the law did. Newton’s rule covers an 
apple falling to the ground and the Moon orbiting the Earth with the same 
mathematics, which is why the law is often called the first great unification in 
physics. The symbol worked without any help from a bruise.
So the fruit belongs to Newton and the bump belongs to his retellers. The 
law is in the Principia of 1687. The apple comes from Newton’s own later 
account, and in that account it stays off his head.
TELL SOMEONE
According to Britannica, Newton’s own recollection includes an apple but no 
bump; the hit on the head was a later embellishment.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 3 · DID NEWTON HIMSELF TELL THE APPLE STORY?
 TRUE
The apple story is not a fable invented by Newton’s admirers. According to 
Encyclopaedia Britannica , it traces back to Newton’s own later recollection. 
Only the apple hitting his head was added afterward.
The popular version here is a skeptic’s one. The apple, it says, is pure myth: 
a tidy tale invented by later admirers to dress his work in a moment of genius.
The record does not support the skeptic. The account of an apple comes 
from Newton himself, in his own later recollection. What the record marks as 
embellishment is narrower: the detail of the apple striking him. That detail is 
not in Newton’s account.
The law the apple is attached to is fully documented. Newton published it 
in 1687, in Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica. It states that every 
mass attracts every other mass, with a force that scales with the product of 
the masses and falls with the square of the distance between them. Newton 
showed that this rule, combined with his laws of motion, produces Kepler’s 
three laws of planetary motion. Later work kept testing it. Galle found 
Neptune in 1846 within about a degree of where Le Verrier’s Newtonian 
prediction placed it.
The myth of a wholly invented apple spread for a reason that runs opposite 
to most myths in this book. The head-bump is so plainly comic that it invites 
suspicion, and people who rightly doubted the bump threw out the apple 
with it. A debunking can overshoot. Once “the apple story is a myth” became 
a known correction, it traveled as easily as the original, and with as little 
checking.
The safe statement keeps both halves. Newton told of an apple; nobody 
needed to invent it for him. The bump on the head is the later addition. The 
law itself rests on the earliest dated source in the record, the Principia of 1687, 
and the apple rests on Newton’s own word.
TELL SOMEONE
Newton himself told of the apple, according to Britannica; only the bump on 
the head was added later.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 4 · DID GALILEO DROP BALLS FROM THE LEANING TOWER OF PISA?
 FOLKLORE
The tower drop is likely a legend. It is not in Galileo’s own writings. His docu -
mented work on falling bodies used balls rolling down ramps, with notes from 
around 1603 to 1604 and results published in 1638.
The popular version is a public demonstration. Galileo climbed the Leaning 
Tower of Pisa, let go of a heavy weight and a light one together, and proved that 
both fall at the same rate.
The record traces the tower story to a biography by Galileo’s student Viviani, 
written decades later. Because it is absent from Galileo’s own writings and comes 
from that late source, it is widely regarded as legendary.
What Galileo did document was cleverer. The clocks of his day could not time 
a free fall, so he slowed the fall down. He rolled a polished ball down a gently 
sloping grooved ramp and timed each descent with a water clock, marking equal 
intervals by equal weights of water. In successive equal times the ball covered 
distances in the ratio one, three, five, seven. The total distance grew as the square 
of the time, which is the mark of constant acceleration. The acceleration was 
the same whatever the ball’s weight. Without air resistance, all bodies fall at the 
same rate. These results overturned the old Aristotelian teaching that heavier 
objects fall proportionally faster.
The tower story spread because it does in one afternoon what the real work 
did over years. A drop from a famous building is visible, dramatic and easy to 
retell. Ramps, water clocks and ratios of odd numbers are not. The legend also 
has the right moral, an old authority refuted in a single visible test, which made 
it useful to anyone telling the story of modern science.
The documented record is the ramp. Galileo’s working notes date to around 
1603 to 1604, and he published the results in Two New Sciences in 1638. That 
book, not the tower, is the earliest dated source for his law of falling bodies.
TELL SOMEONE
The tower drop is likely a legend; Galileo’s documented work on falling bodies 
rolled balls down ramps and timed them with a water clock.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 5 · DID PYTHAGORAS DISCOVER THE PYTHAGOREAN THEOREM?
 FOLKLORE
The relation that carries Pythagoras’s name was known and used long before 
him. A Babylonian tablet from around 1800 BC lists its number triples, and 
Pythagoras lived from about 570 to 495 BC.
The popular version is simple. Pythagoras of Samos discovered that in a right 
triangle the square on the longest side equals the sum of the squares on the other 
two, and the theorem is named after him for that reason.
The record puts the relation much earlier and in several places. Babylonian 
tablets such as Plimpton 322, from around 1800 BC, record Pythagorean triples. 
The relation also appears in the Indian Sulba Sutras and the Chinese Zhoubi 
Suanjing. Their dates are less certain: the Sulba Sutras are placed between the 
8th and 5th centuries BC, and the surviving Zhoubi Suanjing dates from around 
the 1st century BC, though its contents are thought to be older. The simplest 
triple, three, four and five, has been used since antiquity to lay out right angles, 
and builders still check a corner that way.
As for Pythagoras himself, the record is cautious. Encyclopaedia Britannica 
calls the attribution to him uncertain, and the record adds that it is doubtful he 
personally proved the theorem.
The myth spread because names on theorems work like labels, and labels get 
read as credits. Once a result is called “Pythagorean, ” the natural assumption is 
that Pythagoras found it. A name is repeated every time the theorem is taught; 
the older tablets and texts are not. Repetition turned a label into a history.
The theorem itself is not in doubt. Within flat, Euclidean geometry it holds 
without exception. Its limits are of scope: it covers right triangles only, and for 
any other triangle the law of cosines applies, with Pythagoras’s relation as its 
special case. What is in doubt is the credit. The earliest dated source in the record 
for the relation is a Babylonian tablet of around 1800 BC, more than a thousand 
years before Pythagoras was born.
TELL SOMEONE
A Babylonian tablet from around 1800 BC already lists Pythagorean triples, 
more than a thousand years before Pythagoras was born.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 6 · DID EINSTEIN WRITE E = MC² IN HIS 1905 PAPER?
 FOLKLORE
Einstein’s 1905 paper contains the idea but not the famous formula. It states in 
a single sentence that a body which gives off energy as radiation loses mass in 
proportion.
The popular version has Einstein setting down E = mc² in one short line in 
1905, the equation appearing fully formed in the paper that announced it.
The paper exists and can be read in translation. It is a short follow-up to 
his special relativity paper of the same year, titled “Does the Inertia of a Body 
Depend Upon Its Energy Content?” Einstein’s derivation ends in a sentence 
about a body radiating energy and losing mass: if it gives off energy L, its mass 
diminishes by L/V², with V for the speed of light. That is not the compact 
formula now known everywhere.
The record is clear that the idea was his in a way that mattered. Others before 
him, Poincaré and Hasenöhrl, had linked mass and energy only for electromag-
netic cases. Einstein was the first to state the relation as a general principle 
that applies to all energy. A compressed spring, a heated object and a chemical 
reaction all change mass by their energy divided by the speed of light squared. 
The change is far too small to weigh. Nuclear reactions are where it becomes 
large enough to notice.
The myth spread because the formula became an icon, and icons get read back 
into their origins. Readers who meet E = mc² everywhere assume it must appear 
in the founding document, much as people assume a famous quotation has a 
famous speaker. The popular tie to atomic bombs is a second misreading the 
record corrects: the equivalence is not specifically about nuclear physics at all.
The statement survives the check; the typography does not. The earliest 
dated source is Einstein’s 1905 paper, and in it the mass-energy relation is set 
out in a sentence, not as E = mc². The record does not say when the compact 
form first appeared in print.
TELL SOMEONE
Look for E = mc² in Einstein’s 1905 paper and it isn’t there: he set out the mass-
energy relation in a sentence.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 7 · DID EINSTEIN WIN HIS NOBEL PRIZE FOR SOMETHING OTHER THAN 
RELATIVITY?
 TRUE
Einstein’s Nobel Prize, the physics prize designated for 1921, was for his work on 
the photoelectric effect. NobelPrize.org gives the citation as “for his services to 
Theoretical Physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric 
effect. ” Relativity is not what the prize named.
The popular version assumes the obvious. Einstein is famous for relativity, so 
the Nobel committee must have given him the prize for relativity.
The record tells a longer story about light. Heinrich Hertz first noticed in 1887 
that light striking a material can knock electrons out of its surface. Philipp Lenard 
studied the effect quantitatively. Its features defeated classical wave theory. Below 
a threshold frequency no electrons come out, however bright the light. Above 
it, the energy of each electron depends on the light’s frequency, and brightness 
changes only how many electrons emerge.
In 1905 Einstein explained this in Annalen der Physik with the light-quantum 
hypothesis and an equation relating electron energy to frequency. Robert Millikan 
confirmed the frequency relation around 1916. By the American Physical Society’s 
account, Millikan spent about a decade testing Einstein’s equation, convinced it 
had to be wrong, and his own measurements confirmed it and pinned down 
Planck’s constant, though he kept doubting the photon behind it.
The myth spread because fame and prizes are assumed to line up. Relativity is 
the work most people connect with Einstein, and E = mc² is the formula they know. 
The photoelectric effect is a laboratory result about electrons and metal surfaces. 
It is easy to assume the most famous work won the most famous prize.
The record adds one caution in the other direction. The effect is popularly 
overcredited as proof that light comes in quanta. It motivated the idea powerfully, 
but on its own it is not airtight; the decisive evidence for photons came later, from 
other experiments.
The fact holds. The earliest source is Einstein’s 1905 paper on light quanta, 
and the prize designated for 1921 named his law of the photoelectric effect, not 
relativity.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 8 · DID GORDON MOORE SAY CHIPS DOUBLE EVERY 18 MONTHS?
 FOLKLORE
Gordon Moore never gave 18 months. His 1965 article predicted that the 
number of components on a chip would double roughly every year, and in 
1975 he revised that to every two years.
The popular version is that Moore’s law says computing power doubles 
every 18 months.
The record goes back to the article. On 19 April 1965 Moore, then head of 
research and development at Fairchild Semiconductor and later a co-founder 
of Intel, published “Cramming more components onto integrated circuits” in 
Electronics magazine. His rate was a doubling roughly every year. In 1975 he 
slowed it to every two years. The name “Moore’s law” is usually credited to the 
Caltech professor Carver Mead.
The 18 months came from someone else. Intel’s David House combined the 
two-year doubling in transistors with rising performance and projected that 
chip performance would double every 18 months. Moore said he never used 
the figure.
The myth spread because House’s figure answered the question most people 
were asking. Moore’s law is about transistor count and cost, an economic 
trend with no physical principle behind it. A claim about speed was easier to 
use and repeat than a claim about component density. The record notes that 
the popular reading of ever-doubling “speed” mixes Moore’s trend up with a 
separate one, Dennard scaling, under which shrinking transistors kept power 
density roughly constant. Dennard scaling broke down around 2005 to 2006, 
ending the free gains in clock speed.
The two-year version held for more than forty years, from a few thousand 
transistors per chip in the early 1970s to billions. Its pace has slowed since 
about 2010.
So the law is Moore’s and the 18 months is House’s. The earliest dated 
source is Moore’s own article of 19 April 1965, which said about once a year. 
His 1975 revision said two years.

№ 9 · DID EDWARD MURPHY SAY “ANYTHING THAT CAN GO WRONG WILL GO WRONG”?
 DISPUTED
A maxim like it came out of an Air Force rocket-sled project around 1949, and 
Murphy’s name stuck to it. Who first said it, and in what words, is not settled. 
The earliest firm printing under Murphy’s name, from 1952, credits an unnamed 
physicist.
The popular version has an engineer called Murphy, annoyed by a botched job, 
coining the law on the spot.
The record is more tangled. Around 1949 Edward A. Murphy Jr., an aerospace 
engineer, worked on project MX981, a series of rocket-sled deceleration tests at what 
became Edwards Air Force Base. In one run, strain-gauge transducers wired to a 
harness returned zero readings because they had been installed the wrong way round. 
A part that could be fitted two ways had been fitted the wrong way. The project’s lead, 
Colonel John Stapp, then popularized the maxim at a press conference. He framed it 
as a strength: the team’s habit of designing against every failure it could imagine.
The wording is where the trail breaks. Murphy’s son and his colleagues later 
recalled materially different phrasings, and the accounts of the 1949 events are 
anecdotal. The earliest firm appearance in print of Murphy’s law by name, as “If 
anything can go wrong, it will, ” is in Anne Roe’s 1952 book The Making of a Scientist. 
Roe credits it to a physicist she does not name. Similar sayings without Murphy’s 
name were in print as early as 1877.
The gloomy reading won because the law is so easy to confirm. Across enough 
events some failures are statistically certain, which the statistician David Hand calls 
the law of truly large numbers. People remember the toast that landed butter-side 
down and forget the far more numerous times nothing went wrong. That turned a 
working engineer’s rule into a cosmic joke.
The safe statement is narrower than the legend. A rule about failure came out of the 
MX981 tests around 1949 and carries Murphy’s name. Its first wording is contested, 
and its first firm appearance in print under his name is 1952. What it meant to the 
people who used it was practical: if a part can be fitted the wrong way, design it so it 
can’t be.

№ 10 · DID CHURCHILL PROMISE “BLOOD, SWEAT AND TEARS”?
 FOLKLORE
In May 1940 Churchill offered “blood, toil, tears and sweat. ” That is four items, 
not three, and “toil” is the word that dropped out.
The popular version is the three-word phrase almost everyone quotes: 
Churchill, in May 1940, offering the nation blood, sweat and tears.
The record states his actual words and their date, May 1940, and files the 
shorter version as a misquotation. It lists the misquote alongside another piece 
of shaky lore attached to the same idea: the Latin tag omne trium perfectum , 
“everything that comes in threes is perfect. ” That motto is popularly cited as an 
ancient proverb, but it is not attested in the classical Latin corpus in reliable 
references, and is better treated as a modern slogan.
Both belong to the rule of three, the principle that a trio of words, phrases 
or examples is more satisfying and memorable than other numbers. The usual 
explanation is that three is the smallest number of items that can set up a 
recognizable pattern. A triad is short, has rhythm, and feels complete. The device 
runs from classical rhetoric, where three parallel clauses are called a tricolon, to 
Caesar’s veni, vidi, vici, the three rights of the US Declaration of Independence 
and the closing phrase of Lincoln’s Gettysburg Address.
That is the most likely reason the misquote spread. Memory and retelling 
favor the shape that feels finished. Four items are one too many for the pattern 
the rule describes, so the phrase was trimmed to the form that sounds right. The 
record warns that a well-made group of three can lend an idea an unearned air 
of completeness. Here it did more: it rewrote a famous line to fit the pattern.
The words on the record are Churchill’s own. In May 1940 he promised 
“blood, toil, tears and sweat. ” The three-word version is what the rule of three 
made of them.

№ 11 · DID WILLIAM OF OCKHAM WRITE “ENTITIES SHOULD NOT BE MULTIPLIED 
BEYOND NECESSITY”?
 FOLKLORE
The famous Latin sentence is nowhere in Ockham’s surviving works. Its earliest 
close version was written by John Punch in 1639, nearly three centuries after 
Ockham died. Ockham argued for the same principle, in other words.
The popular version is that the medieval friar William of Ockham wrote Entia 
non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem , “entities should not be multiplied 
beyond necessity, ” and so gave his name to the razor.
The record starts with Ockham himself. William of Ockham (c. 1287 to 1347) 
was an English Franciscan who argued repeatedly for parsimony. One of his own 
statements is in his Summa Logicae, part I, chapter 12. The phrasing the record 
quotes from him is Pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate: “plurality should 
not be posited without necessity. ”
The famous sentence came later. The Irish Franciscan John Punch wrote a 
close version, non sunt multiplicanda entia sine necessitate , in a 1639 commen -
tary on Duns Scotus, and called it a common axiom of the Scholastics. The name 
came later still. Libert Froidmont coined novacula occami , Occam’s razor, in 
1649. And the idea is older than all of them: Aristotle and Ptolemy both stated 
versions.
The misattribution spread because the sentence and the name fit together so 
neatly. Punch’s line is crisper than Ockham’s own, and Punch presented it as a 
shared saying rather than his own invention, so it traveled without an author. 
Ten years later Froidmont’s coinage supplied one. A sentence without a writer 
attached itself to the nearest famous name, and the razor has been quoted in 
words close to Punch’s under Ockham’s name ever since.
None of this weakens the principle, which is a tie-breaker between explana -
tions that both fit the facts: prefer the one that assumes less. It changes only the 
credit. Ockham’s own wording is in the Summa Logicae. A close version of the 
famous Latin sentence first appears in Punch’s commentary of 1639, and the 
name in Froidmont in 1649.

 FOLKLORE
Donald Hebb set out the rule in 1949. The slogan is not his. It came in 1992, 
usually credited to Carla Shatz as a mnemonic, and crediting it to Hebb is, in the

how learning works in the brain.

stated his rule in The Organization of Behavior in 1949. His version is careful and 
causal: when one neuron repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing another, 
the connection between them is strengthened. Hebb also proposed that this 
strengthening welds groups of neurons into working units, which he called cell 
assemblies.
The slogan flattens that. “Fire together” suggests that firing at the same mo -
ment is enough. Hebb’s wording is about one cell helping to fire the next. Later 
research backed the causal reading. Under spike-timing-dependent plasticity, a 
signal arriving shortly before the receiving cell fires strengthens the connection, 
and the reverse order weakens it.

long-term potentiation, the physiological effect consistent with his rule; Bliss 
and Lømo found it in 1973. And the idea was not wholly his: earlier associa -
tionist proposals came first. His contribution was the crisp synthesis and the 
cell-assembly framework.
The slogan spread because it was built to. A mnemonic is designed to be 
remembered, and a rhyme is easy to remember. Once it was in circulation, it was 
natural to attach it to the scientist whose rule it summarized rather than to the 
scientist who wrote the summary. The shorter and catchier a line is, the faster it 
loses its author.
The rule is Hebb’s, set out in The Organization of Behavior in 1949. The rhyme 
is usually credited to Carla Shatz, who printed it in 1992; Siegrid Löwel and Wolf 
Singer printed a similar line the same year.

There is no evidence that Mark Twain said it. The idea was named by the

man. The Twain attribution is the most common misattribution of all, and there

A line with no obvious author tends to settle on a famous wit. The paraphrase 
helped: once the wording had moved away from Kaplan’s and Maslow’s own, 
nothing in the line itself pointed back to them. A quip that sounds like Twain 
gets credited to Twain.

method where it does not fit, not against reuse or expertise. The earliest dated 
source in the record is Kaplan’s 1962 talk, set in print in 1964. Maslow’s version 
followed in 1966. Twain has no place in the record.

№ 17 · DID GODWIN SAY WHOEVER MENTIONS HITLER FIRST LOSES?
 FOLKLORE
Mike Godwin’s 1990 law says only that a long online argument is likely to produce 
a comparison to Hitler or the Nazis. The rule that whoever makes the comparison 
loses was added later by the Usenet community, not by Godwin.
The popular version treats the “loser” rule as part of Godwin’s law: whoever 
invokes Hitler ends the argument and loses it.
The record goes back to 1990. Godwin, an American attorney, coined the law 
that year on early internet forums, chiefly Usenet. In its usual form it says that 
as an online discussion grows longer, the probability of a comparison to Hitler or 
the Nazis approaches one. Godwin has described it as a deliberate experiment in 
memetics: a counter-meme, seeded to discourage the reflexive Hitler comparisons 
he felt trivialized the Holocaust. He documented the method in a 1994 Wired 
article, “Meme, Counter-meme. ”
The loser rule came from the community that adopted the law. A Usenet 
tradition, recorded in the hacker Jargon File, added that once someone invokes 
the Nazis the thread is over and the person who made the comparison has lost. 
Godwin resists it. He has said the law should start a conversation, not end one, and 
that it was never meant to be predictive. After the 2017 rally in Charlottesville he 
publicly set the law aside for marchers carrying swastikas, saying that comparing 
them to Nazis was fair.
The corollary spread because it turned an observation into a weapon. Godwin’s 
version describes a pattern. The loser rule gives anyone in an argument a way to 
win it, by declaring the other side out of bounds. A rule that ends arguments is 
more useful in an argument than a remark about how arguments tend to go, so it

coinage, with his own account in Wired in 1994. The loser rule is folk tradition

Theodore Sturgeon’s printed word was “crud, ” not “crap. ” His Venture Science

percent of everything is crap. ”

aired the idea in a lecture around 1951 and at the 1953 World Science Fiction 
Convention in Philadelphia. He first put it in print in his Venture Science Fic-

because most of every genre is, and that the best of it stands with the best 
writing anywhere. The saying was a case for judging a field by its finest work 
rather than its most abundant.

his Revelation. His actual law was a different sentence: “Nothing is always 
absolutely so, ” a warning against absolutes. Popular usage, and the Oxford

standard sense.

familiar: a quotation that travels by word of mouth tends to get sharper, and a 
blunter word makes a stronger line. The same drift turned the Revelation into 
the law, because a named law is easier to cite than a revelation.

never “crap. ” The earliest printing is Sturgeon’s column of September 1957, 
and like the March 1958 version it says crud.

 FOLKLORE
The two psychologists who named it, Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes, called it 
“the impostor phenomenon” in a 1978 paper. “Syndrome” is popular usage, not 
their term, and the experience appears in neither of the two main diagnostic 
manuals.
The popular version is that impostor syndrome is a recognized condition, 
described under that name by the researchers who first identified it.
The record starts with the paper itself: “The Impostor Phenomenon in High 
Achieving Women: Dynamics and Therapeutic Intervention, ” published in Psy-

with high-achieving professional women who believed their success came from 
luck, timing or a fooled assessor, whatever their record showed. They described a 
loop. Over-prepare, or put the work off and then rush it; succeed either way; then 
credit the effort or the charm rather than the ability.
The difference in wording matters. A phenomenon names an experience a 
person has; a syndrome suggests a disorder. The record keeps to the first. The ex-
perience is in neither DSM-5 nor ICD-11, so its prevalence and its best treatment 
are still unsettled, and a 2019 systematic review of 62 studies found that none 
had tested whether any treatment works. Later research also found the experience 
across genders and professions, not only among the women of the first study.

private feeling seem official and shared, and sharing was much of what people 
wanted from the name: those who thought they were uniquely fraudulent learned 
the feeling was common. The term became one of the most searched psycholog -

ordinary, proportionate nerves about new work, which is not what the 1978 paper 
described.

term in 1978, and the “syndrome” most people now say came from popular usage, 
not from the researchers.

№ 21 · DID 38 WITNESSES WATCH KITTY GENOVESE DIE AND DO NOTHING?
 FOLKLORE
The record finds no basis for the story that 38 witnesses watched Kitty Genovese die 
and did nothing. At least one witness shouted at her attacker.
The popular version is short and bleak. Kitty Genovese was murdered in 1964 while 
38 people watched, and not one of them acted. It is often told as proof that a crowd will 
leave a person to die.
The record does not support it. In a 2007 paper in American Psychologist, Manning, 
Levine and Collins documented the exaggerations in the account of her murder. The 
number 38 has no basis, and at least one witness shouted at the attacker. Kitty 
Genovese’s death was real. The image of a silent crowd watching it is not supported by 
the evidence.
The case, as misreported, did prompt real research. In two experiments in 1968, John 
Darley and Bibb Latané formalized what became known as the bystander effect. People 
who believed they alone could hear someone having a seizure over an intercom almost 
all responded quickly. Those who thought several others were listening responded less 
often and more slowly. The effect holds in the laboratory under narrow conditions: 
ambiguous, non-dangerous emergencies among strangers.
Outside the laboratory the picture is kinder. A 2020 analysis by Philpot and 
colleagues of 219 real public conflicts recorded on CCTV found that in nine cases in 
ten at least one bystander intervened, and that more bystanders meant more help. In 
clearly dangerous emergencies the effect weakens or reverses.
The false version spread because it was vivid and because it became attached to a 
real finding. A single shocking case with a precise number is easy to remember and easy 
to repeat, and each retelling of the science carried the anecdote along with it. The effect 
even acquired a colloquial name, the Genovese syndrome, which tied her name to a 
partly mythologized event. Once a story becomes the standard illustration of a result, 
few people go back to check it.
The bystander effect is real in narrow laboratory conditions, and in real public 
conflicts intervention is the norm. The earliest source in the record to set out the 
corrections to the Genovese story is Manning, Levine and Collins, 2007. The record, 
drawing on it, finds no basis for the 38, and at least one witness acted.

 FOLKLORE
The guards did not become cruel on their own. Archival recordings and later schol -

given guard uniforms and authority over fellow students playing prisoners, and 
within days they turned abusive without being told to. The lesson drawn is that a bad 
situation can make anyone cruel.
The basic events are on record. The study was led by the psychologist Philip

assigned to be guards or prisoners in a mock prison in the basement of the psychol -

abusive and prisoners showed extreme distress. Zimbardo argued that the situation 
and the roles, not the participants’ dispositions, drove the cruelty.

notably by Thibault Le Texier, show that the experimenters actively instructed and 
pressured the guards to be tough; Le Texier concluded that the abuse was substan -

for a study of prison life, which the sources say attracted people higher in aggression 
and authoritarianism. A key prisoner later said his breakdown was acting, and many 
guards said they were consciously playing a role. The study had no control condition, 
and critics have said it never went through normal peer review, which Zimbardo 
disputed. The sources also record serious ethical problems, including participants 
who effectively could not leave.

question. The study was widely taught, and it was invoked in discussions of prisons, 
abuse and atrocities such as Abu Ghraib. A story that seems to explain real cruelty is 
easy to repeat and hard to give up. The sources now treat the study as an example of 
a compelling narrative outrunning weak evidence.

amid abuse and distress. The claim that the guards’ cruelty arose from the situation 
alone is Zimbardo’s interpretation, and the record pairs it with archival evidence that 
the guards were coached.

№ 23 · DID HAWTHORNE FACTORY OUTPUT RISE WHATEVER THE LIGHTING?
 FOLKLORE
The original Hawthorne lighting data do not show output rising whatever the 
lighting. When Steven Levitt and John List reanalyzed them, they called the 
famous patterns “entirely fictional” .
The popular version is a neat paradox. At a factory in the 1920s, researchers 
brightened the lights and output rose. They dimmed the lights and output 
rose again. The conclusion drawn was that the workers were responding to the 
attention of being studied, not to the lighting.
The studies were real. They ran from 1924 to 1932 at the Hawthorne Works, 
a Western Electric factory in Cicero, Illinois, and included the illumination 
and relay-assembly experiments associated with Elton Mayo’s team. The term 
“Hawthorne effect” came decades later, during reanalyses in the 1950s. It is 
usually credited to Henry Landsberger in 1958, with an earlier informal use 
sometimes attributed to John R. P. French.
The data tell a weaker story. The illumination records were long thought lost. 
Levitt and List located them and published their reanalysis as an NBER working 
paper in 2009 and in American Economic Journal: Applied Economics  in 2011. 
They reported that the claim that every lighting change boosted output is not 
supported. The apparent effect largely disappears once day-of-week and pay-
period cycles are controlled for, leaving only, in their words, “hints of more 
subtle manifestations” . The original studies also rested on tiny samples and 
tangled confounds, among them pay incentives, changes in supervision and the 
replacement of workers.
The paradox spread because it was memorable and, for decades, unchecked. 
With the original records thought lost, the story could be retold but not tested 
against the numbers. It also came with a name, and a name makes an idea easy to 
cite. The sources describe the Hawthorne effect as a case of a memorable name 
outrunning its evidence.
The general idea survives. People who know they are being watched can 
change their behavior, which is why researchers use blinding and control groups. 
The founding factory data, reopened by Levitt and List in 2009, do not show 
the famous pattern.

fixed script, and that results may have been presented selectively. These are Perry’s 
arguments. The record treats them as part of a live dispute, not as a settled finding.

arrived with moral weight. Coming after the Holocaust and the Eichmann trial, a 
finding that a majority would obey to the end shocked the public and the profession.

conditions.

participants went to 450 volts. That figure is not a fixed human rate, and how far 
participants believed what they were doing is disputed.

№ 25 · DID MILGRAM PROVE WE ARE ALL SIX HANDSHAKES APART?
 FOLKLORE
Milgram did not prove that everyone is six handshakes apart. Most of the chains in his 
1967 experiment never reached their target, and the “six” is a median over the minority 
that did.
The popular version credits “six degrees of separation” to a single experiment. Stanley 
Milgram, it says, showed that any two people are linked by six acquaintances.
The experiment was real. Around 1967 Milgram had people forward a folder toward 
a target in Boston, from acquaintance to acquaintance. The chains that were completed 
passed through a median of roughly five to six intermediaries. Most never arrived. When 
Judith Kleinfeld re-examined Milgram’s data and archives in 2002, she found that few 
chains were completed, that the samples were small, and that selection bias was present. 
If people drop out at a roughly constant rate at each step, longer chains are the ones most 
likely to fail, so the true distance could even be larger than the completed chains suggest.
The idea, and the phrase, came from elsewhere. The Hungarian writer Frigyes Karinthy 
proposed it in a 1929 short story, “Chains” , which posited no more than five interme-
diaries. John Guare’s 1990 play popularized the phrase “six degrees of separation” . 
Counting adds confusion: five intermediaries make about six links, and degrees and hops 
are often mixed up.
Later work put the idea on firmer ground in a different form. In 1998 Duncan Watts 
and Steven Strogatz showed that a network can be tightly clustered and still crossed in a 
few steps, provided a few links reach far away. A 2012 study of the whole Facebook graph, 
some 721 million users, found an average distance of about 4.7 hops. That figure counts 
every link in a complete graph and is not directly comparable to Milgram’s lossy chains.
The legend spread because a round number is easier to remember than a completion 
rate. “Six degrees” had a catchy phrase, a play behind it and a famous experiment to point 
to. The failed chains were invisible, because a chain that never arrives leaves nothing to 
count. The sources call it a case of a vivid number outrunning the evidence behind it.
What Milgram showed is narrower. Short paths exist between some pairs of people, 
and people can sometimes find them using only local knowledge of who seems closer to 
the target. The six is a median of the chains that finished, not a law.

№ 27 · DOES WAITING FOR A SECOND MARSHMALLOW PREDICT SUCCESS IN LIFE?
 DISPUTED
Early follow-ups linked a preschooler’s ability to wait with later achievement. A 
larger 2018 replication found the link much weaker and largely explained by family 
background.
The popular version is a parable about willpower. Children who could wait for a 
second marshmallow, it says, grew into more successful adults, so self-control at four 
predicts success in life.
The study came from the psychologist Walter Mischel and colleagues at Stanford 
University around 1970. A child, typically aged between about three and a half 
and five and a half, was left alone with one treat and told that waiting until the 
experimenter returned, usually up to fifteen minutes, would earn two. Children who 
distracted themselves, or pictured the marshmallow as a cloud rather than food, 
tended to wait longer. In follow-ups, notably a 1990 study by Shoda, Mischel and 
Peake, preschool wait times were correlated with later measures such as SAT scores 
and parental ratings.
The limits are on record. The original follow-up samples were small and 
drawn from a relatively privileged Stanford-community population. In 2018 Watts, 
Duncan and Quan re-ran the design with about 900 children and controls for back-
ground, and the association shrank substantially. A child’s willingness to wait can 
also reflect trust that the promised reward will arrive, and the reliability of the child’s 
surroundings, not only self-control. The test records one moment in one artificial 
situation.
The verdict is DISPUTED rather than FOLKLORE because the record does not 
say the link is zero. It says the effect is much weaker and largely explained by 
background, so the strong claim is heavily qualified rather than refuted.
The strong version spread because it confirmed something many people already 
believed. It gave scientific weight to the intuition that resisting temptation pays, and 
it became a staple of popular writing on willpower, parenting and character. A small, 
privileged sample produced a clean story, and clean stories travel further than their

№ 30 · WAS THE FORGETTING CURVE BASED ON JUST ONE PERSON?
 TRUE
The forgetting curve rests on one subject: Hermann Ebbinghaus, who tested him-
self. Its best-known modern replication, in 2015, also used essentially one subject.
The popular version treats the curve as a general law of memory, measured 
across many people and describing how everyone forgets.
The record shows a single, methodical experimenter. In the early 1880s Ebbing-
haus memorized lists of consonant-vowel-consonant nonsense syllables, such as 
“WID” and “ZOF” , chosen to strip away prior meaning. He measured retention by 
the savings method: how much time or effort he saved when relearning a list after a 
delay. He published the results in 1885 as Über das Gedächtnis, translated in 1913 
as Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology . The curve he found falls 
steeply in the first hours to a day after learning, then slows and levels toward a 
floor rather than dropping to zero.
In 2015 Murre and Dros replicated the curve in PLOS ONE. They largely con-
firmed its shape, but again with essentially one subject. “Replicated” here means 
the shape reproduces, not that it was validated across a large sample. They also 
found a probable upward jump around the 24-hour mark, so the curve is not 
perfectly smooth, and its usual exponential formula is an approximation.
The material matters as much as the sample. Nonsense syllables were chosen 
to mean nothing. Meaningful material is encoded and forgotten differently, and 
emotionally vivid memories do not follow the same decay.
The large-sample version spread because the curve looks like a law. It has a 
clean shape, a formula and a long history, and it underwrites practical tools, from 
flashcards to spaced-repetition software. A replication sounds like independent 
confirmation by many people, and few users of the curve ask how many people 
were in either study.
The record is plain on the point. The forgetting curve’s shape has held up 
twice, and both times it was measured in essentially one person learning syllables 
designed to mean nothing. Its robust lessons are qualitative: forgetting is fast at 
first and then slows, and spaced review counters it.

syllables, and its 2015 replication again used essentially one subject.

№ 32 · DO THE LEAST SKILLED PEOPLE REALLY OVERRATE THEMSELVES THE MOST?
 DISPUTED
In 1999 Kruger and Dunning found that the lowest scorers on several tests overestimated 
their rank. Since 2016 several researchers have argued that much of that pattern is a 
statistical artifact, and a 2026 reanalysis reports that it inverts.
The popular version is blunter than the paper. The incompetent, it says, are too incom-
petent to know it, while experts underrate themselves. In its shortest form: stupid people 
think they are geniuses.
The original finding is narrower. Justin Kruger and David Dunning’s 1999 paper, 
“Unskilled and Unaware of It” , tested people on grammar, logic and humor. Students who 
scored in the bottom quarter, around the 12th percentile, estimated they were around the 
62nd. The explanation offered was that the knowledge needed to do a task well is largely 
the knowledge needed to judge how well it was done, so a beginner lacks both. Even taken 
at face value, the record describes the effect as a modest miscalibration, not a claim that 
the least able think they are the best.
The critics’ case is statistical. In 2016 Nuhfer and colleagues showed that random 
numbers can produce the same picture, low performers overestimating and high per -
formers underestimating. In 2020 Gignac and Zajenkowski argued that the usual method 
is confounded by regression to the mean and the better-than-average effect, and in their 
own sample of 929 adults found little support for it. People with extreme scores on one 
measure tend to drift toward the average on any second measure, so the pattern can 
appear whether or not a cognitive effect exists. In 2026 Dawson and de Meza went further 
in Psychological Review. Reanalyzing large replication datasets with methods that account 
for randomness in both the test score and the confidence prediction, they report that the 
pattern disappears and then inverts. In their analysis overconfidence appeared at every 
ability level and was most pronounced among the highest performers.
The pop version spread because it flatters whoever repeats it. It explains other people’s 
confident mistakes and places the speaker among the self-aware. It also has the look of 
science: a named effect, a journal, a chart. Regression to the mean, the critics’ explanation, 
is invisible in a chart and hard to explain in a sentence.
What is settled is narrow. The 1999 paper reported that low scorers overestimated 
their rank. Whether that reflects a real cognitive effect, a statistical artifact or the reverse 
of the popular story is disputed. The record’s advice is to read it as a caution about one’s 
own self-assessment, not a verdict on other people.

The smooth inverted-U curve of stress against performance is not a plot of Yerkes

studies find simply that more stress means worse performance.

exam can sharpen focus, while overwhelming anxiety impairs recall and reasoning. A 
smooth curve with two old names attached looks like settled science. Popularity also 
stripped out the awkward detail. The task-difficulty point, the part of the original 
most worth keeping, is the part the popular curve usually drops. That omission 
wrongly implies that high arousal harms even simple tasks.

learned under shocks of different strengths, with a peak that depended on task diffi-

№ 34 · DID MASLOW DRAW THE PYRAMID OF NEEDS?
 FOLKLORE
Maslow did not draw the pyramid. The famous diagram was created by later authors, 
not by Maslow himself.
The popular version is a five-tier pyramid credited to Abraham Maslow, with physi-
ological needs at the base and self-actualization at the tip, each layer to be completed 
before the next.
The theory is Maslow’s. He set it out in a 1943 paper, “A Theory of Human Moti -
vation” , in Psychological Review , and developed it in his 1954 book Motivation and 
Personality. He arranged needs in ascending levels: physiological needs such as food, 
water, warmth and rest; safety; love and belonging; esteem; and self-actualization, the 
drive to become all one is capable of becoming. He argued that more basic needs must 
be substantially satisfied before higher ones become strong motivators. He also allowed 
that the ordering is not rigid, and that people may pursue several needs at once or in 
a different order. In later versions he added cognitive and aesthetic needs and, above 
self-actualization, self-transcendence.
A pyramid hides much of that. It has five fixed layers, each resting fully on the one 
below, and a summit that looks like a finish line, though Maslow held that self-actual -
ization is never fully satisfied. His own account was looser than the shape, and later had 
more levels.
The theory’s evidence is also weaker than its fame. A 1976 review by Wahba and 
Bridwell found little evidence for the strict hierarchy, and later research has not 
consistently confirmed that lower needs must be met first. Critics also see a Western 
individualist bias in placing self-actualization at the top.
The pyramid spread because it is easy to remember and easy to redraw. The theory 
moved out of psychology into management, education, marketing and healthcare, and 
the pyramid became one of the most recognizable images in the social sciences, cred -
ited to Maslow wherever it went. A picture that simple comes to stand for the theory, 
and the qualifications in the text are left behind.
The hierarchy is Maslow’s and the pyramid is not. The earliest pyramid that Bridg -
man, Cummings and Ballard could trace is Charles McDermid’s, in Business Horizons 
in 1960. It is clear that the diagram came from later authors, and that Maslow’s own 
ordering was less rigid than the shape suggests.

quality control: rank the causes of a problem, then fix the few that matter most.
Juran later corrected the attribution himself. In “The Non-Pareto Principle; Mea 
Culpa” (1974), he wrote that Pareto “had made extensive studies of the unequal distrib-
ution of wealth” and that he himself had “mistakenly applied the wrong name” to the 
rule. The principle as a universal, Juran wrote, “was not original with Pareto, ” and later 
authors “fabricated some embellishments” about him.
Suspicion of the round numbers is half right. Eighty and twenty measure different 
things, a share of causes and a share of effects, so their adding to 100 is a coincidence of 
the famous example. A real split might be 90/10, 70/30 or 99/1. The principle is about 
skew, formalized as a power-law, or Pareto, distribution. It is a rule of thumb, not a law of 
nature, and forcing 80/20 onto a roughly even phenomenon is a category error.
The skew does turn up in practice. In 2002 Microsoft reported that fixing the roughly 
20 percent of most-reported bugs removed about 80 percent of the errors and crashes 
users actually hit. These are the company’s own figures, not a controlled study.
The land story spread because it gives an abstract rule a concrete origin. A named 
economist, a country and a tidy split make a better story than a general finding about 
income, and a citation to the Cours as a whole, with no page, is hard to check. Juran’s 
borrowed name then made the link look settled.
The 80 percent of Italy’s land has not been found in Pareto’s writing, and Juran later 
called the name his own mistake. What Pareto did document was the heavy concentration 
of income and wealth, the kind of skew the rule now describes.

№ 38 · DID ROULETTE LAND ON BLACK 26 TIMES IN A ROW AT MONTE CARLO?
 DISPUTED
The gambler’s fallacy is real, but its most famous example is unverified. No 1913 record 
of a run of 26 blacks at Monte Carlo has been found; the earliest known account is a 1959 
popular book on probability by Darrell Huff and Irving Geis.
The popular version is precise. On 18 August 1913, at the Monte Carlo Casino, the ball 
landed on black 26 times in a row, and with each black the gamblers grew surer that red 
was due, raised their bets on red, and lost heavily.
That version has no contemporary witness. Huff and Geis tell it in How to Take a 
Chance (1959), pp. 28–29, with players who “doubled and tripled their stakes” and a run 
that “enriched the Casino by some millions of francs, ” as they put it. The writer Robin 
Quinn searched the Times Digital Archive, the British Newspaper Archive and Google 
Books, found no mention before Huff, and suspects Huff invented it as an illustration. 
Wikipedia repeats the date and count without a 1913 source. The error itself is also called 
the Monte Carlo fallacy.
Whatever happened in 1913, the reasoning in the story is wrong because a roulette 
wheel has no memory. Each spin keeps its original odds, whatever came before. The 
mistake comes from misapplying the law of large numbers to short runs. That law says 
the proportions of outcomes settle down over a large number of trials. It does not say 
that short sequences must balance out. Early deviations are diluted by volume, not 
canceled. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman studied the error as a consequence of the 
representativeness heuristic: expecting a handful of trials to look like the long run.
The fallacy has a strict scope. It applies to independent trials. Where events are not 
independent, as when sampling without replacement, adjusting expectations from past 
outcomes is rational. A long run can also reasonably make someone suspect a biased 
wheel. The error is only the belief that a fair, memoryless wheel must correct itself.
The story spread because it is the ideal illustration. A run of 26 feels as though it must 
mean something, and a room of gamblers ruined by that feeling turns an abstract error 
into a scene. A date and a famous casino make an example sound like a record, and each 
retelling borrowed the precision of the last.
The 1913 run rests on nothing earlier than a 1959 book. The error it illustrates is real: 
each spin of a fair wheel, including one after 26 blacks, keeps the same odds as the first.
TELL SOMEONE
The famous 1913 run of 26 blacks at Monte Carlo has no known record before a 1959

№ 39 · DID MARILYN VOS SAVANT’S CORRECT MONTY HALL ANSWER DRAW A STORM 
OF OBJECTIONS?
 TRUE
When Marilyn vos Savant gave the correct answer to the Monty Hall problem in 1990, 
some ten thousand readers wrote in, close to a thousand of them with doctorates, 
insisting she was wrong.
The story is told as a parable of expert overconfidence. A columnist got a probability 
puzzle right, and thousands of readers, many with advanced degrees, told her she had 
got it wrong. It sounds too neat to be true.
The puzzle is named for Monty Hall, host of the game show Let’s Make a Deal . A 
contestant picks one of three doors, one of which hides a car. The host, who knows 
where the car is, opens another door to show a goat and offers a switch. Switching wins 
two times in three. The first pick is right one time in three, and that does not change 
when a door is opened. Because the host can never open the contestant’s door or the 
car’s, whenever the first pick was wrong he is forced to leave the car behind the other 
closed door.
The problem was first posed by the biostatistician Steve Selvin in a 1975 letter to 
The American Statistician. It became famous through vos Savant’s 1990 “Ask Marilyn” 
column and the storm of objections that followed. She offered an intuition pump: with 
a hundred doors, a contestant picks one and the host opens ninety-eight goats, leaving 
two doors, one of which holds almost all the probability. The puzzle fooled professional 
mathematicians too. Paul Erdős reportedly stayed unconvinced until he was shown a 
simulation.
Two things fed the objections. Intuition says that two closed doors must be fifty-fifty, 
and intuition misjudges conditional probability badly. The original wording also did 
not pin down the host’s rules. The two-thirds answer holds only if the host always opens 
a goat door, never the contestant’s, always offers the switch, and chooses at random 
between goats when the first pick is the car. If he opens a door at random and it happens 
to show a goat, switching wins only half the time. The record says this ambiguity is 
much of why the dispute raged.
The storm of letters is on record, and so is the answer. Under the standard host rules, 
vos Savant was right: switching wins two-thirds of the time.
TELL SOMEONE
By her own estimate, some ten thousand readers, close to a thousand with doctorates, 
wrote in to tell Marilyn vos Savant she was wrong, and she was right.

№ 40 · WAS BENFORD’S LAW FIRST SPOTTED IN WORN LOGARITHM TABLES?
 TRUE
The astronomer Simon Newcomb first noticed the leading-digit law in 1881, from 
the observation that the early pages of logarithm tables, those for numbers starting 
with 1, were the most worn. Frank Benford rediscovered it in 1938.
The popular version credits Frank Benford with the discovery, as the name sug -
gests.
The law itself is odd enough to deserve the attention. In large sets of real-world 
numbers that span many orders of magnitude, 1 is the leading digit about 30 percent 
of the time and 2 about 18 percent, and the frequencies fall away to under 5 percent 
for 9. The distribution follows P(d) = log₁₀(1 + 1/d).
Newcomb set out the observation in “Note on the Frequency of Use of the Different 
Digits in Natural Numbers” , in the American Journal of Mathematics in 1881. The clue 
was physical. The early pages of the tables, covering numbers that begin with 1, had 
worn fastest.
The physicist Frank Benford rediscovered the pattern in his 1938 paper “The 
Law of Anomalous Numbers” . He tested it across more than twenty thousand values, 
among them river surface areas, populations, physical constants and molecular 
weights, and their leading digits followed the curve. The law was named after him.
Benford’s name stuck because his paper did the work that makes a law famous. 
It gathered thousands of numbers from unrelated sources, all obeying one curve. A 
short note about worn pages in a book of tables is easier to overlook. The record calls 
the case a classic instance of Stigler’s law of eponymy: discoveries are seldom named 
after the people who made them.
The law now has a working use and a notorious misuse. Auditors, following Mark 
Nigrini, screen ledgers and tax figures for sharp deviations that can flag fabricated 
entries for a closer look. It is also wrongly applied to election vote counts, where 
precinct totals sit in a narrow range and the law is not expected to hold. Deviation is 
a prompt to look closer, never proof of manipulation.
The law carries Benford’s name and his 1938 evidence. The first observation was 
Newcomb’s, in 1881, made from the wear on books of logarithms.

№ 41 · DID HOWARD GARDNER PROPOSE “LEARNING STYLES”?
 FOLKLORE
Howard Gardner’s 1983 book Frames of Mind proposed multiple intelligences, 
not learning styles. The record calls learning styles a distinct and largely 
debunked idea that Gardner himself rejects, and in 2013 he wrote that the two 
should not be confused.
The popular version runs the two together: Gardner showed that some chil -
dren are visual learners and others auditory, and good teaching matches each 
lesson to each child’s style.
The record describes a different claim. Gardner, an American developmental 
psychologist, argued that intelligence is not one general ability captured by an 
IQ score but several relatively independent ones. He first named seven: linguis-
tic, logical-mathematical, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, spatial, interpersonal and 
intrapersonal. He later added an eighth, naturalistic, and has discussed a possi-
ble ninth, existential, without adopting it. He reached them not by analyzing 
test scores but by pulling together evidence from prodigies and savants, from 
brain injuries that spare some abilities and damage others, and from the skills 
different cultures value.
An intelligence, in this account, is an ability a person has in some measure. A 
learning style is a claim about how a learner prefers to take material in. In a 2013 
Washington Post piece, “‘Multiple intelligences’ are not ‘learning styles’ , ” Gardner 
said he was troubled that people credited him with the notion, and noted that 
the term was already in use before his theory.
The confusion spread through classrooms. The theory became widely popular 
in education, where it encouraged teachers to present material in several ways, 
which sounds much like teaching to styles. “Musical intelligence” and “auditory 
learner” also sound like neighbors. A famous name then lends a classroom habit 
the look of research. The record adds a caution about Gardner’s own theory: 
intelligence researchers criticize it for lacking validating evidence, since scores 
on different mental tests consistently correlate, pointing to the general factor 
the theory downplays.
So Gardner’s name belongs to multiple intelligences, a contested theory from 
1983. Learning styles is a separate idea, and he is on record rejecting the match.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 42 · DID MAXWELL WRITE THE FOUR MAXWELL’S EQUATIONS?
 FOLKLORE
James Clerk Maxwell built the theory, but the four compact equations that carry 
his name are Oliver Heaviside’s reformulation of 1884. Maxwell’s own 1865 
paper contained twenty equations.
The popular version holds that Maxwell wrote down the four equations 
taught today, in the vector form printed in textbooks.
The record separates the physics from the notation. Maxwell published his 
field theory in 1861–1862 and consolidated it in his 1865 paper, “A Dynamical 
Theory of the Electromagnetic Field. ” He drew on Coulomb’s, Ampère’s and 
Faraday’s laws and added a term of his own, the “displacement current, ” to make 
the system self-consistent. Combining the equations yields a wave equation 
whose calculated speed matched the known speed of light. That led Maxwell to 
conclude that light is an electromagnetic wave. The result is his.
The form is not. The 1865 paper wrote every directed quantity as three 
Cartesian components and, by Maxwell’s own count, set out twenty equations 
in twenty unknowns; quaternion forms came later, in his 1873 Treatise. In 1884 
Heaviside condensed them into the four vector equations taught today, publish-
ing them in The Electrician from 1885, with related work by Josiah Willard Gibbs 
and Heinrich Hertz. Two of the four, the Gauss’s laws, relate electric field lines 
to charge and record the absence of magnetic monopoles. Faraday’s law says a 
changing magnetic field creates an electric field. The Ampère–Maxwell law says 
a changing electric field, together with current, creates a magnetic field.
The misattribution follows the name. The equations are called Maxwell’s, so 
a reader assumes the page in the textbook is the page he wrote. The compact 
version is also the one that gets reproduced, and it hides the twenty-equation 
original behind it. A label that credits the physicist for the physics has come to 
be read as crediting him for the notation too.
Maxwell’s claim to the theory is secure, and the earliest version of it is his own, 
from 1861–1865. The four equations in their familiar form date from Heaviside 
in 1884, nineteen years after Maxwell’s twenty.

The novelist Michael Crichton coined and named the effect in a 2002 talk, after once

the habit the effect names. A reader spots a newspaper’s errors on a subject they

on subjects they do not. Crichton named this after Gell-Mann tongue in cheek. He 
said he used the name because he had once discussed the effect with Gell-Mann, and 
that dropping a famous name lent himself and the effect more importance than they 
would otherwise have.

like a finding, and the record is clear that this one is not a finding. It is an anecdote 
from a persuasive talk, not a measured effect, and it does not appear in the peer-

a deliberate joke by its coiner. A close cousin, Knoll’s law of media accuracy, attributed

everything in the papers is true except the rare story the reader knows firsthand.

different reporters and standards, so errors in one section do not necessarily predict 
errors in another. Pushed too far, the idea turns into blanket cynicism about all media, 
which is its own unwarranted leap. What it asks for is calibration: an error spotted in 
a known field should count as evidence about the source.

he admitted to. Gell-Mann’s part, on Crichton’s own account, was one conversation.

№ 44 · DID THOMAS GRESHAM FORMULATE GRESHAM’S LAW?
 FOLKLORE
Thomas Gresham did not formulate Gresham’s law. Henry Dunning Macleod named 
it after him in 1858, and the principle had been stated long before: by Oresme, by 
Copernicus around 1519, and in Aristophanes’ play The Frogs around 405 BC.
The popular version credits Gresham, a Tudor financier, with the observation that 
bad money drives out good.
The record gives Gresham a smaller part. Gresham (1519–1579) was a financier 
to the English Crown. After Henry VIII and Edward VI debased England’s coinage, 
full-weight silver coins were hoarded, melted or shipped abroad while the debased 
coins circulated. Writing to Elizabeth I at her accession in 1558, Gresham blamed the 
debasement for carrying the realm’s fine gold abroad. He reported a case; he never 
set it out as a general law. The name came three centuries later. Macleod attached it 
in The Elements of Political Economy (1858). The idea itself was far older. Copernicus 
set it out around 1519, which is why it is also called the Gresham–Copernicus law, 
and Oresme, around 1357, and Aristophanes had stated it earlier still.
The law is narrower than its slogan. It holds when a legal-tender rule or a fixed rate 
forces two monies to trade at the same face value. A rational holder then pays debts 
with the coin whose metal is worth less and keeps the better one. The economist 
Robert Mundell made the condition explicit: bad money drives out good only if 
they exchange for the same price. When the United States took silver out of dimes 
and quarters in 1965, the older 90%-silver coins were hoarded and vanished from 
circulation.
The misattribution spread the way eponyms do. Macleod gave the principle a 
name, and a name is easier to repeat than a list of predecessors. Gresham was also 
a fitting example: he lived through a debasement and reported what it did, which 
made the credit plausible on its face. The record calls the law a textbook case of 
Stigler’s law of eponymy, the rule that discoveries are seldom named after their 
original discoverers.
So the earliest statement in the record is Aristophanes, around 405 BC. Gresham 
observed the effect, and Macleod put his name on it in 1858.

№ 45 · WAS HUBBLE THE FIRST TO FIND HUBBLE’S LAW?
 FOLKLORE
Georges Lemaître derived the relation between a galaxy’s distance and its 
speed of recession, and estimated its constant, in 1927. Edwin Hubble’s paper 
came two years later, in 1929. In 2018 the International Astronomical Union 
recommended renaming the law the Hubble–Lemaître law.
The popular version credits Hubble alone with discovering, in 1929, that 
the universe is expanding.
The record gives the two men different parts. Hubble published the relation 
observationally in 1929. The law states that the farther away a galaxy is, the 
faster it recedes, so a galaxy twice as far away recedes twice as fast. Its direct 
observational form is redshift: a galaxy’s spectral lines shift toward the red by 
an amount proportional to its distance. Lemaître reached the same relation 
from general relativity, and estimated its constant, two years before Hubble 
published. The IAU’s 2018 recommendation acknowledges that priority.
The record also corrects the picture of what is moving. The recession is 
not galaxies flying through space but the metric expansion of space itself, 
which stretches the distance between galaxies and stretches their light to 
longer, redder wavelengths. The Hubble constant is the current rate of that 
expansion. Nearby, local gravity wins: the Andromeda galaxy is approaching 
the Milky Way.
The single-name version spread because a name, once attached, repeats 
itself. Until the 2018 recommendation the law carried Hubble’s name alone, 
and each retelling passed the credit on. The record files the case under a 
familiar pattern: the law is not named for its first discoverer. A theoretical 
derivation published first and an observational paper published second can 
both lay claim to a result, and here the name went to the second.
So the relation was first derived, with an estimate of its constant, by 
Lemaître in 1927. Hubble’s observational paper followed in 1929, and since 
2018 the IAU’s recommended name carries both men.

№ 46 · DID ARTHUR LAFFER SKETCH THE LAFFER CURVE ON A NAPKIN?
 DISPUTED
First-hand accounts of the napkin evening exist, but they disagree, Arthur Laffer 
says he does not remember the details, and the napkin now at the Smithsonian has 
disputed provenance. The firm dated anchor is a 1978 article by the journalist Jude 
Wanniski, who named and popularized the idea.
The popular version is set in a restaurant in 1974. Laffer, arguing against a tax 
increase, sketches a curve on a napkin for Dick Cheney, Donald Rumsfeld and 
Wanniski.
The accounts that do exist disagree. Wanniski, who arranged the meeting, told the 
story. Grace-Marie Turner, then a young aide who says she sat in, recalls a paper 
cocktail napkin and doubts Rumsfeld was at the table. Laffer has written that he 
does not remember the details. A cloth napkin inscribed to Rumsfeld, found among 
Wanniski’s papers, is at the Smithsonian, but Turner judges it a later replica. What 
the record does date is the name: the curve was popularized under Laffer’s name by 
Wanniski in a 1978 article. Laffer, for his part, disclaimed originality for the idea 
itself.
The story spread because the curve really is simple enough to draw on a napkin. 
Tax revenue is zero at a rate of zero, since nothing is taxed. It is zero again at a rate of 
100%, since no one bothers to earn taxable income. Somewhere between the two lies 
a rate that raises the most. Two points on the baseline and a hump between them can 
be drawn in seconds. A story in which a policy argument fits on a napkin, sketched for 
powerful listeners, has the shape that travels: a small object, a famous room and a big 
consequence. The curve later became the intellectual centerpiece of 1980s supply-
side policy, which gave its origin story a large audience.
The record is careful about what even the drawing shows. The curve says a revenue-
maximizing peak exists, not where it is, nor which side of it any economy sits on. A 
napkin can carry the geometry but not the evidence about where the peak lies.
So the napkin evening rests on accounts that disagree, and the man at the center of 
it does not remember the details. The earliest dated source in the record is Wanniski’s 
1978 article, which put Laffer’s name on the curve.

№ 47 · DOES LAFFER HIMSELF SAY THE LAFFER CURVE WASN’T HIS IDEA?
 TRUE
Arthur Laffer has disclaimed originality for the curve that bears his name. He 
credits earlier sources, including Ibn Khaldun in the fourteenth century and 
John Maynard Keynes.
The popular version makes Laffer the inventor of the idea that cutting taxes 
can raise revenue.
The record says otherwise on both counts. The curve is named after Laffer 
and was popularized under that name by the journalist Jude Wanniski in a 1978 
article. Laffer himself points to precedents, among them Ibn Khaldun six cen -
turies earlier and Keynes. The idea is an inverted U. Revenue is zero at a tax rate 
of zero and zero again at 100%, so some rate between them raises the most. The 
engine is how people respond. As the marginal rate rises, they shift away from 
taxed work, investment and reported income toward leisure, avoidance, evasion 
or relocation, and the tax base shrinks. How strongly reported income reacts to 
the after-tax rate, the elasticity of taxable income, sets the curve’s shape.
The popular version also runs two claims together. That a peak exists is, 
in the record’s words, nearly uncontroversial. Where it lies, and which side of 
it an economy is on, is fiercely contested. Most economists place developed 
economies on the upward side, where cuts typically lose revenue. In a 2012 
survey of leading economists by the IGM panel, none agreed that a US income-
tax cut would raise revenue within five years. George H. W. Bush called the claim 
that cuts pay for themselves “voodoo economics” in 1980.
The single-inventor story spread with the policy. The curve was the intellec-
tual centerpiece of 1980s supply-side policy, used to justify cutting the top US 
income-tax rate from 70% to 28%. A name attached to a famous policy argument 
travels with it, while the disclaimers and precedents do not.
So the curve carries Laffer’s name through Wanniski, and Laffer himself 
credits Ibn Khaldun and Keynes with the idea. The geometry is sound. The 
claim that cuts raise revenue in today’s rich economies is the part the evidence 
disputes.

 TRUE
Joseph P. Overton developed the idea in the mid-1990s, but it was his 
colleague Joseph Lehman who named and elaborated it, after Overton died in 
a 2003 ultralight-aircraft crash.
The popular version assumes that Overton coined “the Overton window” 
himself.

Lehman named the concept and elaborated it. The term then reached a wider 
public through Glenn Beck’s 2010 novel The Overton Window.
The idea is a range. On any issue, only some positions can be advocated by 
mainstream politicians without being dismissed as too extreme. Positions can 
be ranked from unthinkable through radical, acceptable, sensible and popular 
to settled policy. The window is the band from acceptable to popular, and it 
shifts over time. It is a qualitative model, not a measured law, and there is 
no agreed metric for where the window sits. The Mackinac Center illustrates 
it with education policy, from no government involvement at one end to 
government providing all education at the other.
The assumption that Overton named it comes from the name itself. An 
eponym looks like a signature, and a concept that reached many readers 
through a novel bearing the name, years after the naming, carried no note 
about who chose it. The popular telling changed the idea as well as the credit. A 
dramatic version, in which activists deliberately propose extremes to drag the 
window, is contested and arguably overstated. Lehman, the concept’s steward, 
emphasizes that lawmakers mainly detect and follow the window. The Mack-
inac Center attributes most shifts to gradual change in society’s values rather 
than to manipulation.

tribute, given after Overton’s death in 2003.

№ 49 · IS THE BAADER–MEINHOF PHENOMENON NAMED AFTER A MILITANT GROUP?
 TRUE
The nickname comes from the West German militant group. It traces to a 1994 
reader submission to the St. Paul Pioneer Press from a writer who had heard the 
group’s name twice in quick succession.
The popular assumption is understandable. Two surnames joined by a dash 
have the look of a scientific eponym, and it is easy to suppose that two 
researchers stand behind the effect.
The record shows a newspaper reader instead. The phenomenon is the expe-
rience of noticing something new, a word, a car model, an unusual name, and 
then seeming to meet it everywhere. The 1994 submission to the Pioneer Press 
gave that experience its nickname, after the writer’s own run-in with the name of 
the militant group. The scholarly term came later. The Stanford linguist Arnold 
Zwicky coined “frequency illusion” in an August 2005 post on the blog Language 
Log, and the Oxford English Dictionary added the term in its March 2019 update. 
Zwicky also coined a sibling, the “recency illusion”: the belief that something 
only recently noticed is itself new.
The mechanism, as the record gives it, has two parts. Selective attention 
means that once something is flagged as relevant, the brain filters for it and reg-
isters instances it used to ignore. Confirmation bias means each new sighting is 
taken as evidence that the thing has become more common, while the absences 
go uncounted. The base rate never moves.
The odd name stuck for a reason the record makes plain: its origin story is 
itself an example. A reader heard an unusual name twice in quick succession and 
took it for a pattern. That is the effect in miniature, and it makes a better story 
than a dry technical label. The record adds a caution. The construct comes from 
linguistics blogging and lexicography rather than a controlled experiment, and 
much of the evidence is anecdotal.
So the nickname does come from the militant group, by way of a 1994 
newspaper reader. The scholarly name, “frequency illusion, ” dates from Zwicky 
in 2005.

№ 50 · IS “MUPHRY’S LAW” MISSPELLED ON PURPOSE?
 TRUE
The misspelling is deliberate. John Bangsund, of the Society of Editors (Victoria) 
in Australia, coined Muphry’s law in the society’s March 1992 newsletter as “the 
editorial application of the better-known Murphy’s law. ”
The popular version takes the name for a slip: someone misspelled Murphy’s 
law and the error stuck.
The record shows a joke built into the title. The law states that any criticism 
of someone’s writing will itself contain an error, and the name “Muphry” 
demonstrates the point in its own spelling. Bangsund’s fuller statement adds 
corollaries. A book that thanks someone for proofreading will contain mistakes. 
The stronger the criticism, the greater the fault. Any book on editing will be 
internally inconsistent. An internet-native twin, Skitt’s law, says that any post 
correcting an error in another post will itself contain at least one error.
The record gives two reported cases. In 2008 the author Stephen Dubner sug-
gested that The Economist had printed “pasties” in error for “pastries. ” “Pasties” 
was correct, readers invoked Muphry’s law, and the magazine reportedly sent 
him a Cornish pasty. In 2009 The Sun attacked Prime Minister Gordon Brown 
for misspelling a soldier’s surname in a handwritten condolence letter; Brown 
blamed his handwriting. Days later the paper misspelled the same surname on 
its own website and had to apologize.
The belief that the name is a typo spreads for the same reason the joke works. 
The name only lands if it looks like a mistake, so a reader who sees “Muphry” 
and corrects it has acted out the law. The law’s own durability has a plainer 
cause, which the record names: memorable confirming cases get shared, while 
the many error-free corrections go unremarked. It is a humorous folk adage, not 
a measured tendency, and its charm is that it proves itself.
So the spelling is no accident. Bangsund chose it in 1992 so that the law’s 
name would carry its own error.

№ 51 · DO ESKIMO LANGUAGES HAVE FAR MORE WORDS FOR SNOW THAN ENGLISH?
 DISPUTED
The answer depends on what counts as a word. By roots, Eskimo languages have roughly 
as many snow words as English; by whole words, specialist surveys find far more. The 
inflated figures of fifty and a hundred are myth.
The popular version holds that Eskimo languages have a great many words for snow, 
and offers this as proof that the language people speak shapes what they see.
Franz Boas listed four snow words in 1911 with no count attached. Benjamin Lee 
Whorf ’s 1940 popular article made the contrast explicit, setting English’s one all-inclu-
sive snow word against several Eskimo ones. Later writers inflated the number, to fifty by 
1978 and a hundred in a 1984 New York Times editorial. The linguist Geoffrey Pullum’s 
1989 essay “The Great Eskimo Vocabulary Hoax, ” building on Laura Martin’s 1986 study, 
attacked those numbers and found the root count roughly comparable to English.
The question stays open because the answer turns on what is counted. Pullum’s root 
count stands, but surveys led by Igor Krupnik count about 40 terms in Central Siberian 
Yupik and at least 53 in Nunavik Inuktitut, and a 2010 study supports more frozen-water 
roots than English.
The hypothesis the story was meant to support is real, and more modest. Linguistic 
relativity is associated with Edward Sapir and Whorf in the early twentieth century, 
though they never co-authored it or called it a hypothesis; the label came later, commonly 
credited to Harry Hoijer. It comes in two versions. The strong one, that language deter -
mines and limits thought, is rejected by mainstream cognitive science. The weak one, 
that language influences habitual thought, has some support. Russian has separate basic 
words for lighter and darker blue, and Russian speakers tell blues apart across that 
boundary faster than English speakers. The advantage vanishes under verbal, but not 
spatial, interference. Speakers of Guugu Yimithirr, which uses compass directions rather 
than left and right, keep constant track of orientation. Effect sizes are modest, and some 
findings, such as those on grammatical gender, have failed to replicate.
The snow story spread because it does in one image what the real evidence does slowly. 
A people surrounded by snow, with words to match, is easy to picture and easy to retell. 
Pinning it on Whorf, whose name was already on the idea, made an anecdote look like 
scholarship. Each retelling then borrowed authority from the last.
So the counts of fifty and a hundred are myth, and the story began with Boas, not 
Whorf. Whether the real total is far above English depends on counting roots or words, 
and specialists still argue it. The firmer evidence for the weak hypothesis concerns blues 
and compass points.

№ 52 · DOES THE CORIOLIS EFFECT DECIDE WHICH WAY A SINK DRAINS?
 FOLKLORE
At the scale of a sink, bath or toilet, the Coriolis effect is thousands of times too 
weak to set the direction of draining. Water drains both ways within the same 
hemisphere.
The popular version says the Earth’s rotation makes water swirl down drains 
one way in the Northern Hemisphere and the other way in the Southern.
The record shows a real effect at the wrong scale. The Coriolis effect is the 
apparent deflection of a moving object when its motion is described from a 
rotating frame such as the turning Earth. Freely moving air or water appears to 
curve to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern. It 
is not a real force pushing the object but an inertial effect of viewing motion from 
a spinning frame. Its sideways push is proportional to the object’s speed, and its 
horizontal strength scales with the sine of the latitude: zero at the equator, greatest 
at the poles. Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis gave the mathematical expression in an 
1835 paper on rotating machines, and the effect entered meteorology in the early 
twentieth century.
Over long distances and long times, that weak nudge adds up. It makes large 
low-pressure systems rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and 
clockwise in the Southern. It helps drive the great ocean gyres, and long-range 
shells travel far enough that gunners must correct for it. A basin of water does none 
of this. Its draining is set by the basin’s shape, the angle of the jet that filled it and 
whatever motion is left in the water. Only under extreme laboratory control, with 
still, symmetric tanks left for hours, has a Coriolis signature in draining water been 
measured.
The myth spread because it borrows real physics. Storms do turn opposite ways 
in the two hemispheres, and a drain vortex looks like a small storm. The step from 
one to the other is natural and wrong, because the effect only builds up over large 
scales and long times.
So the effect is real and shapes the weather. In a sink it is swamped, and water 
drains both ways in either hemisphere.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 53 · IS THE UNCERTAINTY PRINCIPLE JUST ABOUT MEASURING DISTURBING 
THINGS?
 FOLKLORE
The uncertainty principle is not merely the idea that measuring a particle disturbs 
it. It says a quantum state cannot have sharply defined values of both position and 
momentum at once, and it holds even for perfect instruments.
The popular version runs like this: a particle’s position and speed cannot both 
be known, because looking at it knocks it off course.
The record describes something deeper than clumsy measurement. Certain 
pairs of quantities, called conjugate variables, cannot both be defined to arbitrary 
precision at the same time. For position and momentum, the spread in position 
times the spread in momentum is at least the reduced Planck constant divided by 
two. This is a feature of quantum states themselves, which do not possess sharp 
values of both quantities at once. No state that breaks the bound can even be 
prepared.
The reason is the wave nature of matter. A quantum state’s description in 
position and its description in momentum are Fourier transforms of each other, 
and a function sharply peaked in one is necessarily spread out in the other. Pin 
a particle down in space and its momentum fans out. A wave passing through a 
narrow slit shows the trade: narrow the slit and the position sharpens while the 
spread beyond it widens. The bound applies only to such pairs. It does not say 
everything is uncertain.
The disturbance reading spread partly through the principle’s own history. 
Heisenberg introduced it in a 1927 paper in Zeitschrift für Physik with a thought 
experiment about a gamma-ray microscope, a picture of looking at a particle, and 
gave only an order-of-magnitude relation. Earle Kennard supplied the rigorous in-
equality the same year, and Hermann Weyl also contributed to it. The record notes 
a separate relation describing how a measurement disturbs a system; conflating 
the two has caused lasting confusion. A picture of looking is easier to hold than a 
Fourier transform.
So the principle is about the states, not the instruments. The precise bound, half 
the reduced Planck constant, comes from Kennard’s 1927 inequality, not from 
Heisenberg’s microscope.
TELL SOMEONE

state cannot have sharply defined values of both position and momentum at once.

№ 54 · WAS SCHRÖDINGER’S CAT MEANT TO MAKE A QUANTUM IDEA LOOK ABSURD?
 TRUE
Erwin Schrödinger devised the cat in 1935 as an objection to the Copenhagen inter-
pretation of quantum mechanics. His aim was to show the absurdity of extending 
quantum superposition to a large object, not to claim that a cat can be alive and dead 
at once.
The popular version turns the argument around: Schrödinger proposed that a cat 
in a sealed box really is both alive and dead until someone looks.
The record describes a reductio ad absurdum. A cat is sealed in a box with a 
radioactive atom, a Geiger counter, a hammer and a flask of poison. If the atom 
decays, the counter trips the hammer, the flask shatters and the cat dies. Radioactive 
decay is a quantum event, and until it is measured the atom is in a superposition of 
decayed and not decayed. A naive reading of the formalism therefore puts the cat in 
a superposition of alive and dead until the box is opened. Schrödinger found that 
conclusion absurd for a cat, and used it to press the measurement problem: when 
and how the wavefunction collapses, and what counts as an observer.
The puzzle is still open, and interpretations answer it in incompatible ways. 
The Copenhagen view holds that measurement yields a single outcome. In the 
many-worlds interpretation the cat is alive in one branch and dead in another. Deco-
herence, a physical process rather than an interpretation, adds that contact with the 
environment suppresses interference between alive and dead almost instantly, so 
no such superposition can be observed. On its own it does not say why one outcome 
occurs. On none of these does collapse require a conscious observer.
The reversed reading spread because the image outlived the argument. A cat both 
alive and dead is a picture that sticks after one hearing. The objection attached to it 
takes a paragraph, and it gets dropped in the retelling. Popular versions then added a 
mystical observer whose mind settles the cat’s fate, which the record calls a popular 
distortion of the physics.
So the cat was a critique. Schrödinger built it in 1935 to make an idea look absurd, 
not to claim that cats live double lives.

№ 55 · DID VORTEX SHEDDING BRING DOWN THE TACOMA NARROWS BRIDGE?
 FOLKLORE
The 1940 collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge is more accurately explained by 
aeroelastic flutter, a distinct, self-excited mechanism. The record says the case should 
not be presented as simple vortex-street resonance.
The popular version attributes the collapse purely to vortex shedding: wind peel-
ing eddies off the bridge in a steady rhythm until the structure shook itself apart.
The record keeps the mechanism and moves the example. Vortex shedding is real. 
When a fluid flows past a blunt body and the Reynolds number that characterizes the 
flow passes a critical value, about 47 for a circular cylinder, the wake turns unstable. 
Vortices roll up and break away first from one side and then from the other, never 
both at once, and trail downstream in a staggered double row called a Kármán vortex 
street. The alternating shedding pushes the body one way and then the other. The 
rhythm is set by the Strouhal number, which links the shedding frequency, the size 
of the body and the speed of the flow. If that frequency matches a natural frequency 
of the structure, the push can drive resonance and damaging vibration. That is why 
some chimneys, masts and antennas are fitted with helical strakes or dampers, and 
why wires hum in the wind. Theodore von Kármán analyzed the pattern, building on 
earlier observations by Mallock and Bénard.
Tacoma Narrows, on the record’s account, belongs to a different instability. It 
names that mechanism as aeroelastic flutter and calls it distinct and self-excited. In 
flutter the wind feeds energy into the deck’s own twisting faster than the structure 
can damp it, so the motion grows with no need for a matching rhythm in the wind. 
The numbers fit flutter, not vortices: the destructive twisting ran at about 0.2 hertz, 
while vortex shedding at that wind speed would have been near 1 hertz.
The vortex version spread because it is a true mechanism attached to the wrong 
case. Vortex shedding does set structures shaking, and a famous collapse in the wind 
makes a tempting illustration. Both stories feature wind and oscillation, which makes 
the substitution easy to miss and easy to repeat.
So vortex shedding is real and can wreck a chimney. The famous bridge failure of 
1940 is better explained by flutter.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 56 · DOES AIR OVER A WING SPEED UP TO MEET THE AIR UNDERNEATH?
 FOLKLORE
Air does move faster over a wing’s upper surface, and the pressure there is lower. 
But the popular reason, that the air must hurry to rejoin the air beneath, is a 
misconception that NASA debunks.
The popular version is the “equal transit time” story. The upper surface of 
a wing is longer, so air passing over it must speed up to meet the air passing 
underneath at the back of the wing. Faster air means lower pressure, and the 
wing is lifted.
The record keeps the conclusion and rejects the reason. NASA’s Glenn 
Research Center explicitly debunks the equal-transit-time account. Symmetric 
wings and inverted flight both contradict it, and it predicts far too little lift. The 
record does not say why the upper air speeds up; it says only that this popular 
justification is wrong and that Bernoulli’s principle does contribute to lift.
The principle itself is sound. Daniel Bernoulli set it out in Hydrodynamica 
in 1738, from experiments with liquids, and Leonhard Euler later derived the 
equation in its usual form. Along a streamline, faster flow means lower pressure. 
It is an energy budget: the kinetic energy, pressure energy and gravitational 
potential energy per unit volume add to a constant, so more speed leaves less 
pressure. Seen through Newton’s second law, it is a pressure difference that ac-
celerates a parcel of fluid, so the faster region sits at lower pressure. The standard 
form holds for steady, incompressible flow without viscosity. A narrowing pipe 
shows it plainly: the fluid speeds up through the constriction and its pressure 
falls, the Venturi effect.
The wrong story spread because it ends at the right answer. Faster air and 
lower pressure are both true, and equal transit time supplies a tidy reason for 
them. An explanation that reaches the correct result by a wrong route is hard to 
catch, because every check of the result seems to confirm it.
So Bernoulli’s principle stands, and the air over a wing is faster. The idea that 
it races to meet the air below is the misconception.
TELL SOMEONE

Whether hot water can freeze faster than cold is unresolved. Erasto Mpemba and 
Denis Osborne published the claim in 1969, a careful 2016 study by Burridge and 
Linden found no evidence for a meaningful effect, and the record says the dispute 
remains open.
The popular version is simple: hot water freezes first, and the effect carries 
Mpemba’s name.
The record tells a longer and less settled story. In 1963 Mpemba, a Tanzanian 
student, noticed that a hot sugared-milk mix for ice cream froze faster than a cooler 
one. He later published with the physicist Denis Osborne, in a 1969 paper titled 
“Cool?” in Physics Education. The idea is far older. Aristotle, Francis Bacon and 
Descartes each remarked that previously heated water can freeze quickly, though 
their observations are anecdotal. So Mpemba did not discover the idea first.
The modern evidence cuts against a robust effect. Burridge and Linden’s 2016 
study in Scientific Reports timed how long samples took to cool to 0 °C, not to freeze 
solid, and found no evidence for a meaningful effect. Misplacing a thermometer by 
as little as a centimeter could produce an apparent one. There is no agreed defin -
ition of what freezing faster means, and no accepted mechanism. The candidates 
include evaporation, which leaves less hot water to freeze; differences in convec -
tion; dissolved gases driven out by heating; differences in supercooling; and a hot 
container melting frost and improving thermal contact. Each is contested, and 
some may be artifacts. Some narrow studies report reproducing something under 
specific conditions, which is why the record stops short of calling it debunked.
The claim persists because it is counterintuitive, and a surprise is easy to 
remember and fun to repeat. It also has a memorable origin, a schoolboy and an ice-
cream mix. And it is easy to get a result either way at home, because the outcome 
depends on the container, the volume and exactly what counts as frozen.
So it is not an established fact that hot water freezes faster, and it is not fully 
debunked. The careful test of 2016 found nothing meaningful, and the question 
remains open.
TELL SOMEONE
A careful 2016 study found no evidence for a meaningful Mpemba effect, but 
whether hot water can freeze faster remains unresolved.

№ 58 · DOES A BROKEN BONE HEAL BACK STRONGER?
 FOLKLORE
There is no evidence, contrary to popular belief, that a healed fracture becomes 
stronger than the original unbroken bone. The claim is an overstatement of Wolff ’s 
law, which describes how healthy bone adapts to the loads placed on it.
The popular version holds that a bone knits back stronger at the break than it 
was before.
The record finds no support for that and traces where the idea comes from. 
Wolff ’s law is frequently overstated as implying that broken bones heal back 
stronger. What the law actually says is narrower. The German anatomist and 
surgeon Julius Wolff set it out in his 1892 book Das Gesetz der Transformation der 
Knochen (The Law of Bone Transformation). Healthy bone adapts its structure to 
the loads it carries, growing stronger under more stress and weaker under less.
The mechanism is living tissue at work. Cells embedded in bone, called osteo -
cytes, sense strain, largely through fluid flowing in tiny channels. They signal the 
cells that build bone and the cells that remove it. The internal lattice of the bone 
is reshaped first, and the outer wall can then thicken or thin. The record gives 
three cases. Racquet-sport players develop measurably denser bone in the arm 
that holds the racquet. After a hip replacement, the metal implant carries load the 
femur used to bear, and studies have recorded falls of roughly 10 to 40% in bone 
density around the upper femur in the first two years. Astronauts on long missions 
lose bone density because microgravity removes the loading signal.
Wolff ’s own strict mathematical formulation has been discredited in its precise 
form, and researchers now prefer the looser term “bone functional adaptation. ”
The myth spreads because the true part makes the false part sound like a corol-
lary. Bone does get stronger under stress, and a break looks like the greatest stress 
of all, so stronger healing feels like the same rule applied once more. The step is 
an inference the evidence does not support.
So bone does adapt to load. A healed fracture becoming stronger than the 
original bone is not something the evidence shows.

healthy bone instead adapts to the loads it carries.

№ 59 · DID THOMAS YOUNG SHINE LIGHT THROUGH TWO SLITS?
 DISPUTED
Thomas Young showed that light interferes, in his Bakerian Lectures around 
1801 and 1803. Whether he performed a literal two-slit experiment is disputed: 
his demonstrations used sunlight, pinholes and narrow cards.
The popular version has Young shining light through two slits around 1801, 
seeing bands on a screen and proving that light is a wave.
The record keeps the result and questions the apparatus. Young argued for 
the wave nature of light by showing that light passing two closely spaced 
openings produces interference fringes. His interference work is best dated to 
the Bakerian Lectures of around 1801 and 1803. But the tidy two-slit setup of 
the textbooks is partly a later idealization, since his actual demonstrations used 
sunlight, pinholes and narrow cards.
The physics is the same in either form. Light passing two narrow, closely 
spaced openings spreads out from each, and the two waves overlap. Where crest 
meets crest the light is bright; where crest meets trough it cancels. The result is 
a row of evenly spaced fringes whose spacing depends on the wavelength and 
the geometry. Those fringes are the hallmark of waves.
The experiment’s later fame came from its quantum version. When electrons 
or photons are sent through one at a time, each lands as a single dot, yet the dots 
build up the same fringes, as Tonomura’s 1989 experiment filmed with electrons. 
If a detector records which slit each particle takes, the fringes disappear. That 
is a physical effect of the detector, not of a conscious mind watching. Richard 
Feynman called the phenomenon the only mystery at the heart of quantum 
mechanics.
The neat two-slit picture of Young spread because the quantum version made 
the apparatus famous, and the history was drawn to match. A clean diagram with 
two slits is easier to teach than pinholes and cards, so the textbook setup was 
projected back onto its founder.
So Young’s interference work around 1801 and 1803 is secure. Whether it 
used two slits as pictured is not, and the record says he worked with pinholes 
and cards.

№ 60 · WAS THE DOPPLER EFFECT TESTED WITH TRUMPETERS ON A TRAIN?
 TRUE
C. H. D. Buys Ballot tested the Doppler effect for sound in 1845, using trumpeters 
on a moving train. Christian Doppler had predicted the effect three years earlier, 
in an 1842 paper in Prague.
The popular assumption is that the trumpeters belong in a textbook, not a rail-
way timetable: the scene sounds like an illustration invented to explain a passing 
siren, not an experiment anyone ran.
The record says it was run. Doppler, an Austrian physicist, predicted the effect 
in 1842. Buys Ballot confirmed it for sound in 1845 with his railway experiment, 
and Hippolyte Fizeau described the effect independently for light in 1848.
The effect is a change in the frequency an observer detects when source and 
observer move relative to each other. Approaching, the observed frequency is 
higher than the emitted one; receding, it is lower. The source’s own output never 
changes. As a source approaches, each wave cycle starts from a point a little closer 
to the observer than the last, so the crests bunch up ahead and spread out behind. 
For sound, which travels in a medium, the effect depends separately on how the 
source, the observer and the air are moving, so a moving source is not equivalent 
to a moving observer.
The doubt comes from how well the story fits a classroom. A brass note rising as 
the train approaches and falling as it passes is exactly what a teacher would invent 
to explain an ambulance siren, so it is easy to file as an invention. The record also 
shows that Doppler’s own grand application went wrong. He thought the effect 
explained the visible colors of stars, but stellar speeds are far too small for that, and 
the real signature is a shift of spectral lines, as Fizeau clarified. The prediction’s 
founder got its most ambitious use wrong; the modest test with trumpeters got 
it right.
So the trumpeters were real. Doppler predicted the effect in 1842, and Buys 
Ballot confirmed it for sound on a moving train in 1845.

№ 61 · DOES “RED HERRING” COME FROM TRAINING HOUNDS WITH SMOKED FISH?
 FOLKLORE
The hound-training origin is likely a myth. The figurative sense of “red herring” 
is real, and it is traceable to the journalist William Cobbett in 1807.
The popular version explains the phrase with a scene from the hunting field. 
Hunters, it says, dragged a strong-smelling smoked fish across a trail, either to 
train their hounds or to throw them off a scent. A red herring, on this account, 
is whatever pulls the dogs away from the trail they should be following.
The record does not support the practice. Michael Quinion’s article “The Lure 
of the Red Herring” traces the figurative usage to Cobbett in 1807 and treats 
the hound-training practice as dubious. The phrase’s documented history starts 
with a writer using it as a figure of speech, not with any recorded kennel routine. 
The record’s hedge matters here: it calls the fish story likely a myth, not a proven 
fabrication.
The meaning the phrase settled into is precise. A red herring is something 
brought in to distract from the question at hand. Its fault is irrelevance, not 
falsehood: a true point that leads a discussion away from the issue is still a 
red herring. Logicians file it among the fallacies of relevance, close to the Latin 
ignoratio elenchi, the irrelevant conclusion. Writers of mysteries use the same 
term for a deliberate false clue that steers readers toward the wrong suspect, a 
legitimate device rather than a fault.
The hunting story spread because it fits the meaning so neatly. An odd phrase 
invites an explanation, and one that matches the sense of being led off a scent 
feels right on first hearing. Folk etymologies often work this way: the story is 
built backward from the meaning, and the fit is taken as proof. Repetition does 
the rest.
What can be stated with confidence is narrower than the legend. The figura -
tive “red herring” is traceable to Cobbett in 1807. The hounds and the smoked 
fish are, on the record’s evidence, likely a myth.

№ 62 · IS THE “INVISIBLE HAND” THE HEART OF ADAM SMITH’S ECONOMICS?
 FOLKLORE
Adam Smith used the exact phrase “invisible hand” only three times in his 
works, and just once in The Wealth of Nations . It was not the center of his 
economics, and he supported many roles for government.
The popular version makes the invisible hand Smith’s master idea. The free 
market, left alone, turns private gain into public good, and Smith built his 
economics on that principle.
The record shows a narrower use. In The Wealth of Nations (1776), Smith wrote 
that an individual who intends only his own gain is led “by an invisible hand to 
promote an end which was no part of his intention, ” namely the interest of so-
ciety. His example was a merchant who prefers domestic to foreign industry for 
his own security and so, without meaning to, supports the wider economy. That 
is its only appearance in The Wealth of Nations. A study in Econ Journal Watch, 
“Adam Smith’s Invisible Hands, ” documents how sparse and varied Smith’s uses 
of the phrase were across his works.
Smith did not advocate unregulated laissez-faire. He supported government 
roles in defense, justice, public works and education. He was sharply critical 
of merchants, and warned that people of the same trade seldom meet without 
conspiring against the public. The invisible-hand result also breaks down under 
market failures such as externalities, monopoly, public goods and imperfect 
information, where self-interest does not serve society.
The phrase grew in other hands. The record says later economists inflated 
a limited observation into a sweeping principle of market efficiency, and 
the phrase became the central image of free-market economics. A two-word 
metaphor travels more easily than a long book’s qualifications. Once it stood for 
the whole case for markets, readers expected to find it at the center of Smith’s 
work, and its fame came to far outstrip its frequency in his writing.
The invisible hand is Smith’s, and the economic passage is real. It appears 
once in that book, whose author also backed public works, courts and education, 
and distrusted merchants.

№ 63 · DID BARBRA STREISAND’S LAWSUIT MAKE THE PHOTO OF HER HOUSE

 TRUE
In 2003 Barbra Streisand sued over an aerial photograph of her Malibu home, and 
news of the suit drew far more viewers than the picture had ever had. Before the

California coastline, taken by the California Coastal Records Project to document 
erosion. Streisand sued the photographer for violation of privacy. The sum is 
usually given as $50 million, though contemporary reports varied. Once the suit 
was news, more than 420,000 people visited the site within a month. The case 
was dismissed, and she was ordered to pay the photographer’s legal costs. The 
download and visitor figures are the widely cited ones, and they come from the 
project itself.

Techdirt about a Florida resort’s demand that the site urinal.net remove a photo 
of one of its urinals. “Let’s call it the Streisand Effect, ” he proposed. In her 2023 
memoir Streisand called the suit a mistake in retrospect and said her concern had 
been her name attached to the photo.

had seen, a famous plaintiff, and a lawsuit that became the advertisement. The 
record adds a caution the fable leaves out. The effect names the case in which 
suppression itself becomes the story, not a rule that suppression always fails. Most 
takedowns succeed quietly and are never noticed, so the remembered cases suffer 
from survivorship bias.
So the core is documented. A 2003 suit over one frame in a coastal survey, six 
downloads before it, more than 420,000 visitors in the month after, and a name 
coined by Masnick in 2005.

№ 64 · DID HABEAS CORPUS COME FROM MAGNA CARTA?
 FOLKLORE
Habeas corpus did not come from Magna Carta. Its roots predate the charter of 
1215. It grew out of English common law over centuries, and the Habeas Corpus 
Act of 1679 codified it.
The popular version gives the writ a single birth certificate. Magna Carta, in 
1215, created the right to make a jailer justify an imprisonment before a court.
The record treats that as a simplification. Magna Carta is often cited as a 
precursor, and often called the origin, but the writ traces to earlier English prac-
tice. Its beginnings are older and more diffuse than the charter story suggests. 
The protection developed through common law over centuries, until the Habeas 
Corpus Act of 1679 codified it. Long known as the Great Writ of liberty, it is 
also protected in the United States, where the Constitution’s Suspension Clause 
forbids suspending it except in cases of rebellion or invasion when public safety 
requires.
What the writ does is also narrower than its reputation. It orders whoever 
holds a prisoner to bring that person before a court and justify the detention; the 
Latin name refers to producing the body for examination. It tests only whether 
the detention is lawful, not whether the prisoner is guilty. It is a procedural 
remedy, not a guarantee of release, since a lawful detention will be upheld. 
It can also be suspended in extreme circumstances. Abraham Lincoln’s suspen-
sion during the American Civil War is the famous and controversial case, and 
the writ’s reach over wartime and immigration detention has been repeatedly 
contested.
The charter story spread because it is tidy. A right with a date and a founding 
document is easier to teach than one that built up through courts over centuries. 
Because Magna Carta is so often cited as a precursor, the step from precursor to 
source is a short one, and retellings take it.
The record’s account is less neat and better supported. The writ’s roots are 
older than Magna Carta; it grew through English common law and was codified 
by the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679.

a report on any real people. The record says that attaching the exact phrase

tag, and a tag can stand in for a long argument. Once it circulated with his

the uncertainty of information: incomplete, delayed, contradictory or false

have not lifted the fog either. They reduce some uncertainty while adding 
new failure modes, from data overload to spoofed information, and forces still 
misidentify targets and receive contradictory intelligence.

graph of metaphors. Once a translation set the image in a short, memorable 
form, it could be quoted without the surrounding text, and his name gave it 
authority. Each citation pinned it more firmly to him.

and moonlight are Clausewitz’s, from On War (1832). The crisp phrase “the

№ 68 · DID 12 OF 16 RISING-POWER RIVALRIES END IN WAR, AS THE “THUCYDIDES 
TRAP” HOLDS?
 DISPUTED
The tally is reported accurately: a Harvard project led by the political scientist 
Graham Allison counted sixteen cases in five hundred years in which a rising 
power threatened a ruling one, and twelve ended in war. What is disputed is the 
method behind the count, which cases belong in it and how each is scored.
The popular version treats the figure as a law of history. Three in four power 
transitions end in war, so a rising China and a ruling United States are probably 
headed for one.
The phrase is older than the count: in a 1980 lecture the novelist Herman Wouk 
asked how to break out of a “Thucydidean trap” in the American and Soviet rivalry. 
Allison revived it in a 2012 Financial Times article, developed it in The Atlantic 
in 2015 and in the 2017 book Destined for War. He anchored it in Thucydides’s 
line that it was the rise of Athens and the fear this instilled in Sparta that made 
war inevitable. The case file, published by Harvard’s Belfer Center, lists the sixteen 
rivalries.
The claim is narrower than the slogan. Allison does not say war is inevitable. He 
credits the four peaceful cases to imaginative statecraft, and avoiding war is the 
point of his argument.
The dispute is about the count. Critics among historians and international-
relations scholars argue that the sixteen cases were not chosen by a rigorous, 
repeatable rule, so the ratio may reflect the selection. The coding of war or no 
war, and of which side was rising, is often ambiguous. Critics also call the idea 
single-cause and say it misreads Thucydides, whose account of the war’s causes is 
many-sided. And even granting the tally, correlation is not causation.
The figure spread because a clean ratio under an ancient name sounds mea -
sured. A number turns a judgment into what looks like a finding, and the China 
question makes it urgent.
So the count is accurate as a count. Whether twelve of sixteen is evidence of a 
trap is still argued over.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 69 · DID RICHARD DAWKINS COIN THE WORD “MEME”?
 TRUE
Richard Dawkins coined the word “meme” in his 1976 book The Selfish Gene, 
as the name for a unit of cultural transmission.
The popular assumption runs the other way. The word is so common in 
everyday talk that it is easy to take it for slang that grew up on its own, with 
no author and no date.
The record gives both. The meme appears in the same 1976 book that set 
out the selfish gene, the gene-centered view of evolution, for a general read-
ership. Dawkins built on earlier work by W. D. Hamilton, George C. Williams 
(notably his 1966 Adaptation and Natural Selection) and Robert Trivers. In 
that view natural selection acts at the level of genes, the replicators that copy 
themselves across generations, and organisms are the survival machines, or 
vehicles, that carry them. “Selfish” is a metaphor for a gene’s tendency to 
promote its own replication, not a claim that animals or people are innately 
selfish.
The meme was his analogy for culture. It is a unit of cultural transmission, 
like the gene, and subject to its own selection.
The gene-centered view itself remains contested. Critics argue that selec-
tion acts on whole organisms or groups, and Stephen Jay Gould and Richard 
Lewontin attacked it as reductionist. Many argue that gene-level and indi -
vidual-level accounts often describe the same process in different terms. The 
word “meme” is not part of that dispute. Its source is a single, dated book.
The assumption that it has no author follows a common pattern. Words 
that pass into everyday use tend to shed their origins, and the more ordinary 
a word becomes, the less often anyone asks who made it.
This one has a maker and a year. Dawkins coined “meme” in The Selfish 
Gene in 1976.
TELL SOMEONE
The word “meme” has a maker and a year: Richard Dawkins coined it in 
The Selfish Gene in 1976, for a unit of cultural transmission.

№ 70 · DID “THE PRISONER’S DILEMMA” BEGIN WITHOUT ANY PRISONERS?
 TRUE
The game came first and the prisoners second. Merrill Flood and Melvin 
Dresher devised it at the RAND Corporation in 1950 as a table of payoffs. The 
mathematician Albert Tucker added the story of two prisoners later that year, 
to explain it to an audience of psychologists at Stanford.
The popular version is that the dilemma began as a story about two suspects 
held in separate cells, each offered a lighter sentence for betraying the other.
The record starts with the mathematics. RAND had recruited academics after 
the Second World War to think about strategy scientifically, and game theory 
was new. Flood and Dresher wanted to know whether players in a game without 
binding agreements, where one side’s gain is not simply the other’s loss, would 
reach what is now called a Nash equilibrium. In early 1950 they had two col -
leagues play their game a hundred times in a row. The players often cooperated, 
more than the theory predicted.
The payoffs carry the whole idea. Betraying a cooperator pays best, mutual 
cooperation next, mutual betrayal worse, and being the one betrayed worst. 
Betrayal therefore pays more whatever the other player does, so both betray, and 
each ends up worse off than if both had cooperated.
Tucker’s contribution was the story, with prison sentences as the payoffs. The 
name is usually credited to him too, but the earliest use of “prisoner’s dilemma” 
in print is Luce and Raiffa’s 1957 textbook, and the original handout for Tucker’s 
talk did not carry the phrase.
The story spread because it is easier to retell than a table. Two suspects, a 
bargain and a betrayal turn four numbers into a plot with a moral, and the same 
plot fits arms races and price wars. A good illustration can outlive its occasion 
until it seems to be the origin.
So the order is documented. The game came from Flood and Dresher at RAND 
in 1950, and the prisoners arrived afterward, as Tucker’s way of explaining it.

№ 71 · DID PARKINSON’S LAW BEGIN AS A JOKE IN THE ECONOMIST ?
 TRUE
Parkinson’s law began as satire. The naval historian C. Northcote Parkinson 
opened a satirical essay in The Economist on 19 November 1955 with the “com-
monplace observation” that made his name: work “expands so as to fill the time 
available for its completion. ”
The popular version treats it as a management finding, a rule distilled from 
research into how organizations work.
The record shows a satirist with a target. Parkinson’s evidence was the British 
Admiralty, whose bureaucracy kept growing as the fleet shrank. Officials, he 
argued, make work for one another regardless of the work to be done. The 
essay was later expanded into a 1957 book, Parkinson’s Law, or The Pursuit of 
Progress, which carried the joke further. He offered a bureaucratic corollary: 
an organization’s staff grows by a roughly fixed percentage each year, which 
he estimated at five to seven, independent of the workload. A later financial 
version held that expenditure rises to meet income, so that money, like time, is 
consumed by whatever is available.
The figures gave the satire the look of a study. Numbers, a named institution 
and a crisp general rule are the furniture of research, and satire that borrows 
them is easily read straight. The opening line also describes something most 
office workers recognize. An hour-long meeting fills the hour; a fifteen-minute 
slot covers the same ground. A law that matches daily experience does not need 
its source checked, and the satirical frame fell away in the retelling.
Read as a serious rule, it also gets stretched. The record is careful about what 
it does not say. It is not proof that every deadline should be brutal or that 
rushed work is as good. Past a point, compression destroys quality, and creative 
or research work needs unpredictable slack. The useful reading is narrower: 
generous time is not free, because it quietly recruits busywork.
The record is clear on the origin. Parkinson’s law is a line from a satirical essay 
in The Economist, 19 November 1955, about an Admiralty that grew while its 
fleet got smaller.

№ 72 · WAS THE PETER PRINCIPLE WRITTEN AS SATIRE?
 TRUE
The Peter Principle was pitched as satire. The educator Laurence J. Peter and the 
playwright Raymond Hull set it out in their 1969 book: “In a hierarchy, every 
employee tends to rise to his level of incompetence. ” It landed as serious manage-
ment theory.
The popular version treats the principle as a finding of management research, 
a measured law of how organizations promote people.
The record shows a different pedigree. The book’s subtitle was Why Things 
Always Go Wrong. Its co-author was a playwright. The mechanism it described is 
simple: promotion rewards being good at the job a person already has, not the job 
they are moving into, so people keep rising until they reach a role they are bad 
at, and there they stay. Peter and Hull added a corollary of their own: “In time, 
every post tends to be occupied by an employee who is incompetent to carry out 
its duties. ”
Others extended it in the same spirit. Scott Adams’s Dilbert Principle, from the 
1990s, held that companies deliberately promote their least competent workers 
into management, to limit the damage they can do to the product. Archibald Putt’s 
version for technical organizations runs: “Technology is dominated by two types 
of people. Those who understand what they do not manage, and those who manage 
what they do not understand. ” A small literature of corollaries grew from a book 
meant to amuse.
The satire was taken seriously because the mechanism is real. The record’s 
examples are familiar: the best salesperson made sales manager, the strongest 
engineer moved into a job about politics and people, the gifted researcher buried in 
budgets. Where promotion rewards current performance and the next role differs 
in kind, the effect bites. A joke that describes a real failure mode is easy to read as 
a finding.
The record also marks its limits. It is a critique of one promotion mechanism, 
not a claim that everyone ends up incompetent. Plenty of people keep growing 
into larger roles, and organizations that promote on demonstrated fitness for the 
next job escape it.
The origin stands. Peter and Hull wrote the principle in 1969 as satire, and 
readers turned it into management theory.
TELL SOMEONE
The Peter Principle was pitched as satire, written in 1969 by an educator and a 
playwright under the subtitle Why Things Always Go Wrong.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 73 · WAS THERE A REAL COMMITTEE THAT ARGUED OVER A BIKE SHED?
 FOLKLORE
No real meeting lies behind the story. The reactor, the bicycle shed and the 
refreshment budget are C. Northcote Parkinson’s invented example, from his 1957 
book Parkinson’s Law, or The Pursuit of Progress.
The popular version reports it as something that happened: a committee 
approved a nuclear reactor in minutes and then argued for far longer about a bike 
shed.
The record places the scene in an essay titled “High Finance, or the Point of 
Vanishing Interest. ” Parkinson’s finance committee approves a £10 million atomic 
reactor in about two and a half minutes. It argues for three-quarters of an hour 
over a £350 bicycle shed. It then spends over an hour on a refreshment budget 
of £21 a year, the smallest sum drawing the longest fight. Everyone understands 
a shed, and no member wants to admit he cannot follow the reactor. From this 
Parkinson drew his law of triviality: the time a committee spends on an item is 
inversely proportional to the money involved.
Parkinson wrote as a satirist. The record calls the committee and its figures 
a fictional illustration rather than data, and the “inverse proportion” phrasing 
rhetorical. It is also a separate idea from his better-known law that work expands 
to fill the time available, though both come from the same book.
The name arrived much later. In 1999 the FreeBSD developer Poul-Henning 
Kamp, exasperated by an endless mailing-list thread, sent an email headed “A bike 
shed (any colour will do)… ” on 2 October. It popularized “bikeshedding” for teams 
that agonize over trivia while real design decisions pass unexamined.
The scene passes for a true story because it is built like one. Exact sums and 
exact timings read like the minutes of a meeting, and satire often borrows the look 
of a report. Once the scene was retold apart from its book, the figures were what 
remained, and they sounded like evidence.
The committee never met. The pattern it was invented to show is what the 
record’s everyday examples describe: a large budget nodded through in minutes, 
then a long debate about a logo’s color or the office snacks.
TELL SOMEONE
The committee that approved a reactor in minutes and argued over a bike shed 
never met; C. Northcote Parkinson invented it in 1957.

№ 74 · DID BROOKS ADMIT HIS OWN LAW WAS AN OVERSIMPLIFICATION?
 TRUE
Fred Brooks, who coined Brooks’s law in The Mythical Man-Month  (1975), 
freely admitted that the law oversimplifies a real tendency: he introduced it 
with the words “Oversimplifying outrageously. ”
The popular version treats it as an iron law of software: never add people 
to a late project, because it will only get later.
The record shows an author more careful than his slogan. The law reads: 
“Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later. ” Brooks drew it 
from his experience managing the development of IBM’s OS/360, and he gave 
two mechanisms. New people need time to learn the work, and the existing 
team must teach them. Communication overhead also grows faster than 
headcount. A team of n people has n(n−1)/2 pairwise links to keep in sync, so 
doubling a team from five to ten takes the paths from 10 to 45, and a team of 
50 carries 1,225. His analogy for work that cannot be divided: “The bearing 
of a child takes nine months, no matter how many women are assigned. ”
The limits are just as clear. Adding people early, before a project is late, 
can help. A project may be “late” only because its schedule was fantasy. 
Skilled developers who already know the codebase avoid most of the ramp-up 
penalty. For divisible tasks, more hands produce more output. The law bites 
hardest on tightly coupled work under heavy communication load, and strong 
modularity and clear ownership shrink it.
The iron-law reading spread because the sentence is short and the experi -
ence behind it is common. A team that has watched a rescue hire slow 
everything down needs no further proof. The caveats live in the surrounding 
chapters, and caveats rarely travel with a slogan.
The author’s own verdict is on the record. Brooks stated his law in 1975, 
explained when it applies, and introduced it with “Oversimplifying outra -
geously. ”

№ 75 · DID CLARKE ADD “ANY SUFFICIENTLY ADVANCED TECHNOLOGY IS INDIS -
TINGUISHABLE FROM MAGIC” YEARS LATER?
 TRUE
Arthur C. Clarke’s most famous law came last. He stated his First and Second Laws in 
1962 and added the Third, “Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguish -
able from magic, ” to the revised 1973 edition of the same book, having first published 
it in a 1968 letter to Science.
The popular version treats the three laws as a set, written together.
The record shows them arriving over a decade. The first two appeared in Clarke’s 
essay “Hazards of Prophecy: The Failure of Imagination, ” in Profiles of the Future  
(1962). The First Law: “When a distinguished but elderly scientist states that some -
thing is possible, he is almost certainly right. When he states that something is 
impossible, he is very probably wrong. ” The Second: “The only way of discovering 
the limits of the possible is to venture a little way past them into the impossible. ” 
His examples were experts who called things impossible. The astronomer Simon 
Newcomb argued that heavier-than-air flight was impossible; the Wright brothers 
flew in 1903. Lord Rutherford dismissed the prospect of practical atomic energy.
The Third Law came later, and Clarke was frank about why he stopped there. In a 
footnote he wrote that “as three laws were good enough for Newton, I have modestly 
decided to stop there. ”
The record is clear that none of the three is a law in the scientific sense. They are 
aphorisms, with no predictive formalism, and the word “law” is playful. The Third is 
about the onlooker, not the machine: an observer who lacks the mechanism cannot 
tell engineered cause and effect from the inexplicable. It does not say technology 
is magic. An inversion, “Any sufficiently advanced magic is indistinguishable from 
technology, ” is usually credited to Larry Niven, though the attribution is contested.
The idea that the laws were written together comes from their form. A numbered 
set of three looks designed as a unit, and the most quoted member is the one 
people assume came first. Newton’s three laws, which Clarke himself invoked, set the 
template.
The dates settle it. The First and Second Laws are from 1962. The Third appeared 
in 1968 and joined them in the book in 1973.

Clarke’s First and Second Laws date from 1962; the magic one came last, in a 1968 
letter to Science, joining the book in 1973.

№ 76 · DID HARVARD BUSINESS REVIEW  REJECT CONWAY’S LAW?
 TRUE
By Melvin Conway’s own account, Harvard Business Review rejected his paper 
on the grounds that he had not proved his thesis. “How Do Committees 
Invent?” appeared instead in Datamation in April 1968.
The popular assumption is that a law now quoted so widely in management 
and software circles must have arrived through a leading management journal.
The record gives it a humbler start. Conway’s own wording was that 
organizations “are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the 
communication structures of these organizations. ” After the Datamation pa-
per, participants at the 1968 National Symposium on Modular Programming 
gave the idea the name “Conway’s Law. ” Fred Brooks later cited and popular-
ized it in The Mythical Man-Month (1975).
The rejection looks less foolish with time, not more. HBR’s objection was 
that the thesis was unproved, and for decades it was. The empirical support 
arrived much later. In 2012 MacCormack, Baldwin and Rusnak compared 
software products of near-identical function, some built by tightly coupled 
commercial firms and some by loosely coupled open-source communities. The 
products’ modularity mirrored the coupling of the organizations that made 
them.
The law has since become a method. The “inverse Conway maneuver, ” 
coined by Jonny LeRoy and Matt Simons in 2010, runs it backward: to get a 
target architecture, first reshape the teams to match it. The record still calls 
the law a strong tendency, not an iron rule. A small, co-located team can build 
a well-factored system without visible mirroring, and reorganizing teams will 
not instantly refactor a rigid legacy system.
The prestigious-journal assumption reads a law’s later fame back into its 
birth. Ideas that end up central tend to be imagined as having started at the 
center. This one started with a rejection.
The record is plain on the facts. Harvard Business Review turned the paper 
down as unproved, Datamation published it in April 1968, and the evidence 
that it was right came decades later.

later.

№ 77 · DID FERMAT HAVE A PROOF TOO BIG FOR THE MARGIN?
 FOLKLORE
The margin note is real; the proof almost certainly was not. Around 1637 Pierre 
de Fermat wrote in his copy of Diophantus’s Arithmetica that he had a marvelous 
proof the margin was too narrow to contain, but the record judges he almost 
certainly had no valid general proof. The accepted proof, by Andrew Wiles with 
Richard Taylor, was published in 1995 and rests on twentieth-century mathe -
matics.
The popular version takes Fermat at his word. He had a short, elegant proof, 
and the world lost it to a narrow margin.
The note is documented. Fermat’s claim was that no three positive whole 
numbers satisfy a to the n plus b to the n equals c to the n for any power above 
two. For the power two there are endless answers, such as 3, 4 and 5; above two, 
he said, there are none. His son published the note in 1670, after Fermat’s death.
The proof is not. Fermat did prove the case of the fourth power, by his 
method of infinite descent. He posed the cases of three and four as challenges 
to other mathematicians but never the general one, and he never mentioned 
the marvelous proof again. Leonhard Euler published a proof for the cube in 
his Algebra of 1770, and others extended the range case by case. No elementary 
proof has ever been found. Wiles announced his in 1993; a gap was found and 
repaired with Taylor, ending a search of 358 years.
The margin story spread because it is the most famous aside in mathematics. 
A boast with a cliffhanger invites everyone to try, and a lost proof makes a better 
legend than a mistaken one. The record’s reading is plainer: the method that 
finally worked, proving that certain curves are modular, was far beyond any tool 
Fermat had. The MacTutor history suggests he may well have realized his proof 
was wrong.
So the note is real, c. 1637, and the proof is not part of the result. The record 
says Fermat almost certainly did not have one.

tury mathematics.

№ 78 · DID KELLY JOHNSON COIN “KEEP IT SIMPLE, STUPID”?
 DISPUTED
The phrase is attributed to Kelly Johnson, lead engineer at Lockheed’s Skunk 
Works, but the attribution is not airtight. A 1938 Minneapolis Star article used the 
variant “keep it short and simple, ” before Johnson’s association with the phrase, 
and the earliest dated record of the acronym itself is a U.S. Navy “Project KISS” 
in 1960.
The popular version credits Johnson alone, the engineer who coined KISS.
The record supports a strong association and no clean first use. The phrase is 
linked to Johnson’s reported instruction that the aircraft his engineers designed 
be repairable in the field by an average mechanic with a few basic tools. An aircraft 
only its designers can service is worse than a simpler one anybody can, because 
a fleet is judged on availability, not peak performance. The Navy documented its 
Project KISS in 1960, and the term was in general use by 1970. The 1938 news -
paper variant sits awkwardly in front of all of this.
The punctuation matters too. By one account Johnson wrote the phrase without 
a comma, so “stupid” is not an insult aimed at the reader. The instruction is to 
keep the thing simple, and foolproof. The famous version, comma included, turns 
a design rule into a scolding.
The principle has a real weakness, which the record states plainly: “simple” is 
not defined. Two engineers who agree on KISS will disagree about which design 
is simpler, both honestly. A problem with essential complexity cannot be made 
simple, only made to look simple in one place and worse in another, and “we kept 
it simple” is a common post-mortem finding.

№ 79 · DID FERMI POSE THE FERMI PARADOX OVER LUNCH?
 TRUE
The question began as a lunchtime remark by the physicist Enrico Fermi at Los 
Alamos in 1950, with his colleagues Teller, Konopinski and York. Firsthand accounts 
differ on his exact words, and the paradox’s name and rigorous form came later, from 
others.
The popular version has Fermi at lunch asking, “Where is everybody?” and 
launching the paradox on the spot.
The record confirms the lunch and loosens the rest. The remark was informal. 
Fermi did not coin the name or write the paradox up. The 1950 exchange is recon -
structed from firsthand accounts that disagree about what he said, so “Where is 
everybody?” is the usual rendering rather than a verified quotation. The question 
was first put into print by Carl Sagan. Michael Hart gave it rigorous form in 1975, 
and Frank Tipler followed.
Hart’s version made the tension sharp. A civilization, he argued, could spread 
across the galaxy in far less time than the galaxy’s age, so the absence of any civiliza-
tion reaching Earth suggests none exists. The paradox sets that argument against 
the numbers: hundreds of billions of stars, an abundance of planets, and a great 
span of time in which intelligent life should have arisen and spread or signaled.
It is an argument, not a law, and it proves nothing about whether aliens exist. Its 
force depends on the premise that intelligent life should be common, which rests on 
the unknown terms of the Drake equation. Proposed resolutions range from Robin 
Hanson’s Great Filter, some improbable step between a lifeless planet and a galactic 
civilization, to the Rare Earth view that complex life is uncommon, to the possibility 
that civilizations exist but cannot be detected. There is no consensus.
The popular version is mostly right, and its errors are of a familiar kind. A single 
quip from a famous physicist is a better story than a lunch remembered differently 
by those present, followed by decades of work by others. The name then credits 
Fermi with an argument he never wrote down.
The record supports the lunch. Fermi raised the question at Los Alamos in 1950; 
the exact words are unknown, and the paradox as an argument belongs to later 
writers, starting with Hart in 1975.

 TRUE
In 1995 the physicist Robert Matthews published “Tumbling toast, Murphy’s 
Law and the fundamental constants” in the European Journal of Physics. It won a 
1996 Ig Nobel Prize, and it argued that toast does tend to land butter-side down, 
for mundane physical reasons.
The popular version treats falling toast as a joke about bad luck with no 
science behind it, the emblem of Sod’s law.
The law itself is a joke, and an old one. Sod’s law is a British colloquialism of 
obscure, undated origin, used where North Americans say “Murphy’s law. ” Its 
name comes from the “unlucky sod” to whom misfortune happens: if something 
can go wrong, it will, and at the worst possible time. The record explains most 
of it as selective memory. People remember the rain on the day they forgot an 
umbrella and forget the dry days. The other queue seems faster because a lagging 
queue is the one people notice. Richard Dawkins dismissed the idea that events 
“know” what a person wants.
The toast is the exception. Matthews’s paper is described in the record as 
tongue-in-cheek but genuine physics. Dropped from a typical table height, a 
slice rotates only about half a turn before it lands. A slice that starts butter-
up therefore tends to arrive butter-down. Physics, not superstition, tips it over, 
which makes the law’s most famous example the one with a real deterministic 
basis.
The assumption that nobody had studied it is understandable. The toast is 
the mascot of a law built on bias, so it is natural to assume it is bias too. A physics 
paper about breakfast also sounds like a joke, and this one was written tongue-
in-cheek.
The record holds both facts. Sod’s law is mostly a trick of memory. Toast 
landing butter-side down is not, and a 1995 paper in the European Journal of 
Physics worked out why.

№ 83 · DID PHILIPPA FOOT INVENT THE TROLLEY PROBLEM?
 TRUE
Philippa Foot introduced the original case in 1967, in a paper called “The Problem of 
Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect. ” She did not name it. Judith Jarvis 
Thomson called it the trolley problem and added its best-known variants in 1976 and 
1985.
The popular version comes in two forms. One treats the puzzle as an old one, too old 
to trace. The other treats it as a modern psychology test.
The record gives it a clear birth. In the basic case a runaway trolley will kill five people 
on the track. In Foot’s version its driver can steer onto a side track, where it would kill 
one; in the version most retold, a bystander pulls a lever to divert it. Most people judge 
that diverting it is permitted. Foot’s case appeared in a paper about the doctrine of 
double effect, the principle that harm intended as a means is worse than equal harm 
merely foreseen as a side effect.
Thomson named the problem and sharpened it. In her footbridge variant, the only 
way to stop the trolley is to push a large stranger off a bridge into its path. Most people 
judge this forbidden, though it too trades one life for five. In her transplant case, a 
surgeon could save five patients by taking the organs of one healthy visitor, and almost 
everyone judges this monstrous.
The psychology came later. In 2001 Joshua Greene’s team brought brain imaging to 
the cases, reporting in Science that personal dilemmas such as the footbridge engage 
emotional brain systems while impersonal ones engage deliberative systems. Those 
findings are influential but contested, and they have faced replication and interpreta -
tion challenges.
Both popular versions have a reason. The puzzle strips a moral choice down to a 
count of lives, so it reads like folk wisdom rather than an argument from a journal. 
It also feels like a laboratory test because it has become one: it shaped experimental 
moral psychology and public discussion of machine ethics, from medical triage to self-
driving cars. And the name hides the author. The label that stuck is Thomson’s, while 
the case is Foot’s.
The trolley problem has a datable origin. Foot set out the case in 1967, Thomson 
named and extended it in 1976 and 1985, and Greene’s team took it into the brain 
scanner in 2001.

№ 84 · WAS WEGENER THE FIRST TO NOTICE THE CONTINENTS FIT TOGETHER?
 FOLKLORE
Alfred Wegener was not the first. Ortelius and others remarked on the jigsaw fit of the 
continents centuries before him. Wegener’s originality, first put to geologists in a 1912 
lecture, lay in combining that fit with fossil, rock and climate evidence.
The popular version has Wegener looking at a map, seeing South America nest into 
Africa and spotting the jigsaw.
The fit is real. The eastern bulge of South America sits neatly in the western curve of 
Africa, the match that most strikingly suggests the two were once joined. But the record 
says plainly that it had been noticed centuries earlier. A related claim that Francis 
Bacon had the idea of drift is, in the record’s words, “a persistent overstatement. ”
What Wegener added was a case built from several independent lines of evidence. 
The German meteorologist and geophysicist set it out in lectures in 1912 and in 
his 1915 book The Origin of Continents and Oceans . Fossils of the freshwater reptile 
Mesosaurus and the seed fern Glossopteris turn up on continents now divided by 
oceans they could not have crossed. Rock formations and mountain belts line up when 
the continents are reassembled. Glacial deposits lie in warm regions, and coal, formed 
in lush climates, lies near the poles. Together they pointed to a single former landmass, 
which he called Pangaea.
What he lacked was a cause. He pictured continents plowing through the ocean crust, 
pushed by a pole-fleeing force and by tides. Calculation showed those forces were far 
too weak, and geophysicists led by figures such as Harold Jeffreys had rejected the idea 
by around 1930. It was vindicated in the 1950s and 1960s through paleomagnetism 
and seafloor spreading, and absorbed into plate tectonics.

that credits Wegener with noticing it gives him the part that was not his and drops the 
part that was. His career also has the shape of a legend: a meteorologist challenging 
geologists, rejected for about half a century and then proved right. A hero in that story 
tends to be handed every observation in it.
The fit was old news by 1912. Wegener’s achievement was the synthesis: coastlines, 
fossils, rocks and ancient climates read together as one argument that the continents 
had moved.

№ 85 · WAS UMAMI DISCOVERED IN A SEAWEED BROTH?
 TRUE
In 1908 the Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda isolated glutamate from kombu 
seaweed broth, coined the word umami and developed MSG as a seasoning. 
Umami is now widely accepted as the fifth basic taste.
The popular version is skeptical. On this telling, umami is a marketing inven-
tion: a word made up to sell a seasoning, not a taste in its own right.
The record says otherwise. Ikeda was the first to identify umami scientifically, 
and he took its name from a Japanese word meaning, roughly, pleasant savory 
taste. The taste comes from glutamate, an amino acid, together with certain 
nucleotides, chiefly inosinate and guanylate. Combined, they show a striking 
synergy: the savory intensity is far greater than the two alone would give. That 
is why Japanese dashi, made from kombu, rich in glutamate, and bonito flakes, 
rich in inosinate, tastes so deep.
Umami is not only MSG. Glutamate occurs naturally in aged cheeses such as 
Parmesan, in tomatoes, mushrooms and broths, and in soy sauce and miso. The 
tongue detects it with dedicated taste receptors, including receptors built from 
taste receptor type 1 proteins and related glutamate receptors. That detection of 
glutamate as a quality separate from sweet, sour, salty and bitter is what makes 
umami a basic taste.
The case was not settled quickly. Recognition took most of a century after 
Ikeda’s 1908 work, and the exact identity and workings of the umami receptors 
are still an area of ongoing research.
The skeptical version spread for understandable reasons. The same chemist 
named the taste and developed the seasoning that delivers it, which looks like 
a sales pitch. The long wait for acceptance gave doubt time to settle in. And 
past fears about MSG, which the record says are unfounded, cast suspicion on 
anything tied to it.
The order of events runs the other way from the cynical story. Ikeda found 
glutamate in kombu broth first and named the taste it produced. The seasoning 
came after, and the receptors found since show that umami is a taste the tongue 
registers on its own.

№ 86 · DID MENDELEEV INVENT THE PERIODIC TABLE ALONE?
 FOLKLORE
Dmitri Mendeleev published his periodic table in 1869, but he did not conceive it 
alone. John Newlands had proposed a related law of octaves in 1865, and Julius 
Lothar Meyer worked out a similar arrangement independently at about the same 
time.
The popular version gives the table to one man in one year: Mendeleev, 1869.
The record shares the credit out. Mendeleev ordered the roughly sixty known 
elements by increasing atomic weight and started new rows so that elements with 
similar chemistry fell into the same columns. That revealed the periodic law: proper-
ties repeat at regular intervals when the elements are listed by weight. But Newlands 
had already proposed a related law, and Lothar Meyer had reached a similar table on 
his own.
Mendeleev is credited chiefly for three things, according to the record. He pub -
lished a full system. He pressed the periodic law harder than anyone. And he made 
bold predictions that came true. Where an element placed strictly by weight would 
have landed in the wrong column, he left a gap and predicted what would fill it. His 
eka-aluminium, eka-boron and eka-silicon were later found as gallium, scandium and 
germanium. Gallium, found in 1875, matched his forecast closely. He also adjusted 
some accepted atomic weights so that elements fell into their chemically proper 
places.
His table was not the last word either. Ordering by weight produced anomalies, 
such as tellurium and iodine, where the chemically correct order reverses the order of 
weights. Henry Moseley resolved them in 1913 by showing that elements should be 
ordered by atomic number. Mendeleev also did not know why the properties repeat; 
electron shells were understood later.
The single-inventor story spread because the predictions make a better tale than 
the priority record. A gap in a table, filled years later by a new element that matches 
the forecast, is a moment people remember. A law of octaves and a parallel table 
drawn up elsewhere are background. Naming an achievement after one person then 
makes it sound as though one person did it.
The periodic system had several authors in the 1860s. Mendeleev earned the name 
by publishing the fullest version and predicting elements before they were found. 
The modern order, by atomic number, came from Moseley in 1913.

In 1936 Fisher began a statistical debate over whether Gregor Mendel’s reported

than by a blending of parental traits. Crossing plants that differ in one trait gives 
a uniform first generation and a ratio of three dominant to one recessive in the 
next. Following two unlinked traits gives nine to three to three to one. The work 
was largely ignored until about 1900, when Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich 
von Tschermak rediscovered it.
Fisher’s question was about the numbers, not the rules. It asked whether 
Mendel’s reported counts sat closer to the ideal ratios than chance would usually 
allow. The record says the debate has run since 1936 and remains open. It does 
not record a finding that Mendel invented results.

known limits. Independent assortment fails for linked genes. Incomplete domi -
nance, codominance and epistasis change the ratios while the alleles still separate 
normally. Mitochondrial inheritance and genomic imprinting break one rule or 
another.

tical argument. “Too good to be true” fits in one sentence; an analysis of how far 
reported ratios should scatter does not. The target makes it memorable too: an 
accusation against the founder of genetics is a better story than a quarrel about 
variance. And a debate that stays open for decades is easily rounded off to a verdict, 
in either direction.
The record supports the open question and nothing stronger. Since Fisher in 
1936, statisticians have argued over whether Mendel’s ratios fit too well. That 
argument is unresolved, and it is not a proven charge that he fabricated his data.

№ 88 · DID GAUSS GUESS THE PRIME NUMBER THEOREM AS A TEENAGER?
 TRUE
The record reports that Gauss, as a teenager in the 1790s, conjectured the prime 
number theorem from tables of primes. It was proved only in 1896, about a 
century later.
The popular version remembers the theorem as a nineteenth-century proof, 
the work of the mathematicians who finally settled it.
The theorem says the number of primes up to a given size is roughly that 
size divided by its natural logarithm. Primes thin out as numbers grow, but 
smoothly. Near a million, whose natural logarithm is about fourteen, roughly one 
number in fourteen is prime. Near a billion, whose logarithm is about twenty-
one, roughly one in twenty-one is.
According to the record, Gauss reached this pattern by looking at tables 
of primes, as a teenager, in the 1790s. Legendre reached it around the same 
time, in about 1797 or 1798. Neither could prove it. The proof came in 1896, 
when Jacques Hadamard and Charles-Jean de la Vallée Poussin each settled it, 
independently, using complex analysis of the Riemann zeta function. Erdős and 
Selberg found an elementary proof in 1948 and 1949.
Part of the problem is still open. Beyond small numbers, the simple estimate 
undercounts the primes, and a refined one, the logarithmic integral, tracks the 
true count far more closely. How small the error can be is tied to the Riemann 
hypothesis, which is unproven.
The guess is less famous than the proof for a plain reason. A proof is an event 
with a date and authors. A conjecture read off a table is a private observation, 
and in this case it had two owners, since Legendre made it too. The teenage 
detail also sounds like the kind of flourish that gathers around famous mathe -
maticians, so it is easy to dismiss as legend.
This one is in the record. Gauss guessed the law from prime tables in the 
1790s, while still a teenager, and it took a century of mathematics and the zeta 
function to prove that he was right.

№ 89 · WAS THE 1919 ECLIPSE A CLEAN PROOF OF EINSTEIN?
 DISPUTED
The 1919 eclipse measurements matched Einstein’s prediction, and modern reanaly-
ses largely uphold the result. But the proof was not clean: blurred plates from one 
telescope were discarded, and historians have debated whether that was justified.
The popular version is a single triumphant photograph. Eddington’s plates proved 
relativity beyond doubt.
The record is more careful. On 29 May 1919 two expeditions, organized by Frank 
Watson Dyson and Arthur Eddington, observed a total solar eclipse: one at Sobral 
in Brazil and one, with Eddington, on the island of Príncipe off West Africa. They 
photographed stars of the Hyades cluster near the darkened Sun and compared 
their positions with photographs of the same stars taken months earlier at night. 
Newtonian gravity predicted that starlight grazing the Sun would bend by about 
0.87 arcseconds. General relativity predicted about twice that, 1.75 arcseconds. The 
measurements came out near Einstein’s value. Announced in November 1919, the 
result made Einstein world-famous almost overnight.
The trouble lies in what was left out. The main telescope at Sobral produced 
blurred, defocused images, apparently because the Sun’s heat distorted the instru -
ment. That dataset gave a deflection closer to the Newtonian value, and it was 
dropped from the final conclusion. The shifts that counted came chiefly from a 
backup telescope at Sobral and from the Príncipe plates. Critics from the 1980s 
onward argued that Eddington may have selectively excluded data that favored 
Einstein, and that the error bars were large relative to the effect.
Modern reanalyses of the surviving plates have generally vindicated the original 
conclusion. They found a real instrumental problem that justified excluding the 
blurred plates. The conclusion has not been overturned.
The clean version spread because the announcement was a public event built 
around a hero. A newspaper can report that a famous theory passed a famous test. It 
cannot easily report error bars or a discarded set of plates. The opposite story, that 
Eddington simply picked the answer he wanted, spread for a similar reason: a scandal 
is also a good shape.
The safe statement sits between them. The 1919 eclipse gave the first major obser-
vational confirmation of general relativity, and its result has survived later checking. 
How its data were handled was contested, and the debate is part of the record.

№ 90 · DID HOOKE PUBLISH HIS LAW AS AN ANAGRAM?
 TRUE
Robert Hooke published his law of springs in 1676 as the anagram “ceiiinosssttuu. ” 
In 1678 he revealed the answer: “ut tensio, sic vis, ” as the extension, so the force.
The popular version assumes nothing of the sort. A law taught as a single line of 
algebra is presumed to have been published plainly, like any other result.
The record gives the sequence. Hooke, an English natural philosopher, said he had 
known the law since 1660. He published it sixteen years later in scrambled form, and 
two years after that he gave the solution. The fourteen letters of the anagram are the 
letters of the Latin phrase, sorted into alphabetical order, with u standing for v as 
printers then used it.
The record does not say why Hooke chose to hide the law. Any reason offered here 
would be invented, so none is.
What he hid was simple. Pull a spring twice as hard and it stretches twice as far. 
Force and deformation rise together in fixed proportion, a straight line whose slope 
is the spring’s stiffness. That is what lets a spring balance read a weight off a length. 
In its general form, stress is proportional to strain, with an elastic modulus such as 
Young’s modulus as the constant, and it is the starting point for the theory of how 
solids deform.
The law has firm edges. It holds only within the elastic limit, for small deforma -
tions. Beyond that, materials yield, deform permanently and then break. Some 
materials are not linear even at small stretches: rubber is only roughly linear for small 
pulls and responds nonlinearly further out. The record calls the law a first-order 
approximation with a well-defined domain, not a law derived from first principles.
The assumption of plain publication is natural. Textbooks present results stripped 
of how they first appeared, so a tidy equation looks as though it arrived tidy. An 
anagram is also the kind of detail that sounds like a later joke rather than a printed 
fact.
It was printed. Hooke’s law first appeared in 1676 as fourteen scrambled letters, 
and the Latin sentence they spell, “ut tensio, sic vis, ” followed in 1678.

№ 91 · DID A COBRA BOUNTY IN DELHI BREED MORE COBRAS?
 FOLKLORE
No record shows that anyone in Delhi bred cobras for a bounty. The idea the 
story named rests on firmer evidence, which is the next story in this round.
The popular version is short. British officials in colonial Delhi paid a reward 
for dead cobras, and local people started breeding cobras to collect it. The 
German economist Horst Siebert liked the story enough to name an idea after 
it. His 2001 book Der Kobra-Effekt describes how a reward offered to solve a 
problem can end up paying people to manufacture it.
The trail behind the story is thin. A 2025 review by the Friends of Snakes 
Society traced it to an 1873 newspaper report on a bounty in the Madras 
Presidency, not Delhi. That report said only that it “was alleged” that some 
natives bred cobras for the reward. The review found no contemporary record 
of a breeding operation or of anyone prosecuted for one.
The story spread because it is the ideal shape for a lesson. It has a plan, a 
twist and a moral that fits in one sentence. It is also set far enough away, and 
long enough ago, that few retellers went looking for the source. Each version 
cited the one before, and repetition did the work that evidence should have 
done.
The idea itself holds up, and its best evidence is a bounty on rats paid in 
French colonial Hanoi, which is the subject of the next question in this round. 
Later cases follow the same pattern. Mexico City’s 1989 driving ban took each 
car off the road one weekday a week by license plate. Households bought a 
second car, often older and dirtier, with a different plate; the economist Lucas 
Davis found no gain in air quality.
So the Cobra Effect is real and the cobras are folklore. A reward tied to 
something people can produce will eventually be met by people producing it.

№ 92 · DID A FRENCH BOUNTY ON RAT TAILS IN HANOI BACKFIRE?
 TRUE
In 1902 the French colonial administration in Hanoi paid a bounty for each rat killed, 
with a severed tail as proof. Rat-catchers began cutting the tails off live rats and letting 
them go to breed. The case is documented in a 2003 paper by M. G. Vann in French 
Colonial History.
The popular version is the same story, which is the surprise. Paying for tails produced 
rats without tails, and rats without tails went on producing rats. It sounds too neat to be 
true, and it shares a shape with the Delhi cobra story, which the record treats as folklore.
The record backs the rat version. Vann’s paper, “Of Rats, Rice, and Race: The Great 
Hanoi Rat Massacre, an Episode in French Colonial History, ” appeared in French Colonial 
History in 2003. The Law Tome record calls the Hanoi bounty the documented case 
behind the cobra effect. Rat-catchers cut the tails off live rats and released them, so the 
animals could breed and supply more tails to sell.
The mechanism is plain once it is named. A bounty pays for a measurable result on the 
assumption that the result can only come from doing the wanted thing. Here the result 
was a tail, and a tail did not require a dead rat. When the thing being counted can be 
produced instead of removed, the reward funds its production.
The same setup has recurred. Wells Fargo set aggressive cross-selling targets, and 
employees opened millions of accounts that customers never authorized; the bank paid 
US$185 million in penalties in 2016 and fired thousands of staff. DigitalOcean’s 2020 
Hacktoberfest rewarded a set number of GitHub pull requests, and after a viral how-to, 
maintainers were buried in spam submissions.
The record is careful about limits. The effect is a warning about one design flaw, not 
a claim that incentives backfire in general; most work as intended. It needs a proxy that 
the people collecting the reward can manufacture, and a sponsor who does not control 
its supply. Schemes that pay for verified outcomes and check where the proof came from 
avoid it.
The rat story is less famous than the cobra because the cobra got the name. Siebert’s 
2001 book took its title from the snake story, so the better-sourced case became the 
footnote. Its resemblance to a famous piece of folklore then made it look like folklore too.
It is not. Hanoi’s 1902 rat bounty is the documented example of a reward that bred the 
problem it paid to end.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 93 · DID MORE EFFICIENT STEAM ENGINES MAKE BRITAIN BURN MORE COAL?
 TRUE
In The Coal Question  (1865), the economist William Stanley Jevons observed that 
England’s coal use soared after more efficient steam engines expanded coal’s indus -
trial uses.
The popular version is an assumption rather than a story. A more efficient engine 
burns less coal for each job, so a country with better engines should burn less coal.
Jevons said the opposite, and bluntly: “It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose 
that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very 
contrary is the truth. ” His case was James Watt’s more efficient steam engines. They 
lowered the cost of coal-powered work, which pushed coal into far more industries 
and raised total British coal consumption in the nineteenth century.
The mechanism is now called the rebound effect. An efficiency gain lowers the price 
of each unit of service, so people use more of it. If demand responds strongly enough, 
the extra use outweighs the saving per unit and total consumption rises. A rebound 
above 100 percent is called backfire, and that is the full Jevons paradox. At the scale 
of a whole economy, the Khazzoom–Brookes postulate, named by Harry Saunders in 
1992, adds that efficiency raises incomes and growth, which lifts demand further.
The record keeps the claim narrow. In many cases the rebound is only partial, 
so efficiency still saves something. Fuel-efficient cars lower the cost per mile and 
encourage more driving, but usually not enough to erase the gain. Lighting may be 
a stronger case: a Sandia study led by Jeff Tsao projected that cheaper solid-state 
lighting could raise lighting consumption roughly tenfold by 2030. Economists still 
debate how large and how common rebound is, and evidence for economy-wide 
backfire is genuine but contested.
The efficiency assumption persists because it is true where people look. Per unit, 
a better engine does burn less. The rebound happens across a whole economy, out of 
sight of any single machine, as new uses appear that the old cost ruled out. Jevons 
named the mistake in his own phrase: a confusion of ideas.
For coal in nineteenth-century Britain, the record supports Jevons. Better engines 
made coal cheaper to use, and the country burned more of it.
TELL SOMEONE
Jevons observed in 1865 that more efficient steam engines made coal cheaper to use, 
so Britain found new uses and burned more of it.

№ 94 · DID BROKEN-WINDOWS POLICING CAUSE NEW YORK’S 1990S CRIME DROP?
 DISPUTED
The claim that broken-windows policing caused New York’s crime decline is disputed and 
confounded. Crime fell across most American cities in the 1990s, including cities that 
never adopted the approach, and a 2006 analysis by Harcourt and Ludwig found little 
causal support for the crime-reduction claim.
The popular version credits the windows. New York fixed the small things, arrested 
fare-dodgers and cleaned off graffiti, and the city became safe.
The theory came first. James Q. Wilson and George Kelling set it out in “Broken 
Windows, ” in The Atlantic Monthly in 1982. Visible disorder, they argued, signals that a 
place is unwatched, which invites more disorder and eventually serious crime. They drew 
on Philip Zimbardo’s abandoned-car experiments. A car left without plates was stripped 
within a day in the Bronx but sat untouched for a week in Palo Alto, until Zimbardo 
smashed it himself and passersby joined in.
In 1990s New York, order-maintenance policing under Bratton and Giuliani targeted 
fare evasion and graffiti. Proponents credited it with the city’s crime decline.
The record treats that credit as unproven. Crime fell nationally in the 1990s, amid the 
end of the crack epidemic, economic growth, more police and other changes, including 
in cities that never used the policy. Harcourt and Ludwig’s analysis of a five-city housing 
experiment found that families moved to neighborhoods with less disorder did not 
commit fewer crimes.
One narrow part of the theory has support. In field experiments published in Science 
in 2008, Keizer, Lindenberg and Steg found that visible graffiti and litter increased 
other rule-breaking, including littering and petty theft. That supports disorder spreading 
disorder. It does not show escalation to serious crime, or that a policing program prevents 
it. A rival account from Sampson and Raudenbush holds that disorder and crime may 
both stem from weak neighborhood cohesion. Kelling’s own version also stressed officer 
discretion, unlike the harder zero-tolerance policing often applied in its name.
The simple version spread because the timing lines up. One city, one visible policy and 
one falling crime rate sit side by side, and coincidence in time is easily read as cause. 
A national decline is hard to see from inside a single city, and the image of one broken 
window is easy to remember.
The record supports a narrower claim. Minor disorder breeds more minor disorder. 
Whether broken-windows policing caused New York’s 1990s crime drop is disputed, 
because crime fell in places that never tried it.

studied 258 transport projects worth some $90 billion. Actual costs averaged about 
28 percent above estimate, and roughly nine in ten were underestimated. Flyvbjerg 
argues that for megaprojects the cause is often not honest optimism but strategic 
misrepresentation: promoters low-ball costs to win approval. The record treats that 
as a rival explanation, not a settled one.

decade and a large multiple of the budget. The variation between sources is real, and 
it invites doubt. But it concerns the precise numbers, not the shape of the story.

№ 98 · DID HARDIN LATER ADMIT THE TRAGEDY OF THE COMMONS WAS MISSING 
A WORD?
 TRUE
Garrett Hardin later conceded that his error had been to omit one word: “unman-
aged. ” His tragedy describes an unmanaged, open-access resource, not every 
shared one.
The popular version treats the tragedy as an iron law: any resource held in 
common will be ruined unless it is privatized or controlled by the state.
The argument first appeared as “The Tragedy of the Commons” in Science in 
1968, given as a presidential address to the Pacific Division of the AAAS. Its logic 
rests on herders sharing a pasture. Adding one more cow gives a herder its full 
benefit, while the cost of overgrazing is divided among everyone, so each private 
calculation favors adding more. That works where no one can be kept out and 
where one person’s use leaves less for others.
The record states the correction. Hardin himself later conceded that his error 
was omitting the word “unmanaged. ” Historical commons were in fact regulated 
by custom and law to prevent overgrazing. The model fits an open-access resource 
with no rules, which is a different thing.
Elinor Ostrom’s fieldwork then showed what managed commons can do. In 
Governing the Commons (1990), she documented communities that govern shared 
resources successfully through their own institutions, without privatizing or na -
tionalizing them. She won the 2009 Nobel Prize. The depletion mechanism is real 
where a resource is truly open: the record points to overfished cod on the Grand 
Banks and to the atmosphere as a sink for greenhouse gases. What the record 
rejects is the claim that ruin is inevitable.
The iron-law reading spread because the essay was short, prominent and mem-
orable, and the qualifier that limits it is a single word, easy to drop. The concession 
came thirty years later, in a 1998 essay, so it never traveled with the original. 
The name itself also invites the misreading. It borrows the word “commons” from 
exactly the kind of regulated pasture to which the model does not apply.
Hardin’s own correction is the safe statement. The tragedy belongs to the un -

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 99 · DID GARRETT HARDIN INVENT THE OVERGRAZED-PASTURE PARABLE?
 FOLKLORE
The overgrazed-pasture parable came from William Forster Lloyd in 1833. Garrett 
Hardin named the idea and popularized it in 1968, 135 years later.
The popular version gives the herders to Hardin. His 1968 essay in Science, on 
this telling, introduced the picture of herders overgrazing a common pasture.
The record splits the credit. Hardin’s paper, “The Tragedy of the Commons, ” 
appeared in Science 162 in 1968 and was given as a presidential address to the 
Pacific Division of the AAAS. It gave the idea its name and its wide audience. But 
the parable itself came earlier, from Lloyd in 1833. Lloyd’s version appeared in 
Two Lectures on the Checks to Population, and Hardin’s essay credits it by name.
The parable is simple, which helps explain why it outlived its author. A pasture 
is open to every herder. Each extra cow brings its owner the full benefit, while the 
cost of the grass it eats is shared among all. So every herder keeps adding cows, 
and the pasture is ruined. It needs two conditions: no one can be kept out, and one 
animal’s grazing leaves less for the rest.
The idea has since been tested against real commons. Elinor Ostrom’s Governing 
the Commons (1990) challenged the claim that such ruin is inevitable, by docu -
menting communities that manage shared resources well. The record presents 
Hardin’s 1968 essay as the version that named and spread the parable, and Lloyd’s 
1833 version as its source.
The misattribution spread because names stick to whoever makes an idea 
famous. Hardin supplied the phrase people repeat, “the tragedy of the commons, ” 
in a prominent journal, and the frame went on to shape debates over pollution, 
conservation and property rights. A memorable title tends to absorb the history 
behind it. It is the same drift met in the round on names that mislead: the person 
remembered is the one who popularized the idea, not the one who first told it.
The parable is older than its name. Lloyd described the overgrazed pasture in 
1833, and Hardin gave it the title it carries in 1968.
TELL SOMEONE

rett Hardin gave it its famous name 135 years later.

TRUE OR FOLKLORE?
№ 100 · DID IRISH FAMINE POTATOES SELL MORE AS THEY GOT DEARER?
 FOLKLORE
The first real-world evidence of Giffen behavior comes from rice in Hunan, in a 2008 
study by Jensen and Miller, not from Ireland. The famine potato, long the textbook 
example, has been discredited: Rosen’s 1999 study of Irish price and quantity data 
concluded that 1840s potatoes were not Giffen goods.
The popular version is the textbook one: during the famine, potatoes were the classic 
case of a good bought more as its price rose.
A Giffen good breaks the basic law of demand. Its consumption rises when its price 
rises, giving it an upward-sloping demand curve over some range. The idea is named after 
the Scottish economist Sir Robert Giffen, to whom Alfred Marshall attributed it in his 
Principles of Economics. Marshall’s own example, as the record gives it, was bread: a rise in 
its price could drain poor families’ budgets so far that they cut back on meat and bought 
still more bread.
Three conditions must hold at once. The good must be inferior, bought less as people 
grow richer. It must take a large share of a poor household’s budget. And it must lack close 
substitutes. When its price rises, the household is poorer in real terms, cannot afford the 
costlier foods it would otherwise buy, and fills the gap with more of the staple.
For a long time no real-world case was firmly documented, and some economists 
doubted any existed. Jensen and Miller’s field study, published in the American Economic 
Review in 2008, found strong evidence of Giffen behavior for rice among poor households 
in Hunan, and weaker evidence for wheat in Gansu. They present it as the first real-
world evidence of such behavior. The same paper says the potato example “has also been 
discredited (Rosen, 1999), ” adds that aggregate potato consumption could hardly have 
risen when blight destroyed the crop, and traces the potato version to the 1964 edition 
of Samuelson’s textbook.
The potato story spread because it fits the conditions on paper. A famine supplies a 
staple, poor households and few alternatives, all in a setting most readers already know. 
Textbooks needed an example, and for decades there was no documented one to give, so 
a plausible historical case filled the space and was repeated from book to book.
The potato case does not survive the data. Giffen goods are real but rare, and their 
clearest documented case is rice in Hunan, reported by Jensen and Miller in 2008.
TELL SOMEONE
The textbook Giffen good, Irish famine potatoes, has been discredited; the first real-world 
evidence came from rice among poor households in Hunan.
